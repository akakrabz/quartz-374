> [!abstract] In one breath
> Look at a canonical LP with $d$ variables as $n + d$ halfspaces in $\mathbb{R}^d$. A **basis** is a choice of $d$ of the constraints; its **location** is the vertex where they are all tight. A basis is **feasible** if its location satisfies every constraint, and **locally optimal** if it is optimal for its own $d$ constraints alone — which is exactly feasibility of the complementary basis of the dual LP. The optimum is the one basis that is both. **Primal simplex** walks from feasible vertex to better feasible neighbor until it is locally optimal ("a falling marble"); **dual simplex** walks from locally optimal vertex to worse locally optimal neighbor until it is feasible ("a rising bubble"); they are the same code run on an LP and on its dual. A starting basis is found by running the other algorithm on a rotated objective or on translated constraints. For transshipment problems the bases are **spanning trees**, and primal **network simplex** is cycle canceling. No pivot rule is known to need only polynomially many pivots, but in fixed dimension **Seidel's randomized algorithm** solves an LP in $O(d!\cdot n)$ expected time.

## I.1 Bases, feasibility, and local optimality

Fix a canonical LP $\max\{c\cdot x : Ax\le b,\ x\ge 0\}$ with $A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$, viewed as the search for the lowest point (the objective points down) of the intersection of $n + d$ halfspaces: the $n$ rows and the $d$ sign constraints. Throughout, the LP is assumed **non-degenerate**: every $d$ of the constraint hyperplanes meet in exactly one point, no point lies on more than $d$ of them, and the objective vector is not a linear combination of fewer than $d$ constraint normals.

> [!definition] Definition — basis, location, value, neighbors
> A **basis** is a set of $d$ constraints (linearly independent, by non-degeneracy). Its **location** is the unique point $x$ where all $d$ hold with equality — a vertex of the **arrangement** of the hyperplanes — and its **value** is $c\cdot x$. There are $\binom{n+d}{d}$ bases; "vertex" and "basis" are used interchangeably. Two bases are **neighbors** if they share $d - 1$ constraints (their locations lie on a common line of the arrangement); each basis has exactly $dn$ neighbors — $d$ constraints to drop times $n$ to add — and the vertices and edges of the feasible polyhedron form a subgraph of this basis graph.

> [!definition] Definition — feasible and locally optimal bases
> A basis is **feasible** if its location satisfies all $n + d$ constraints (it is a vertex of the feasible polyhedron). It is **locally optimal** if its location is optimal for the LP that keeps the objective but only the $d$ constraints of the basis — the lowest point of the cone they bound; equivalently, $c$ is a non-negative combination of their normals, $c = \sum_{i\in B}y_ia_i$ with $y\ge 0$ (the forces of [[4-lecture-notes/h-linear-programming#h7-strong-duality|§H.7]]). **Local optimality is dual feasibility**: $B$ is locally optimal for the LP if and only if the complementary set of $n$ constraints is a feasible basis of the dual LP (match row $i$ of the LP with the sign constraint $y_i\ge 0$, and the sign constraint $x_j\ge 0$ with the $j$th dual row). Locally optimal bases are therefore also called **dual feasible**.

The note says "the same basis is feasible for the dual"; precisely, it is the **complementary** basis — the dual constraints that are tight are exactly those matched with the primal constraints that are *not* in $B$, which is complementary slackness ([[4-lecture-notes/h-linear-programming#h8-complementary-slackness|§H.8]]). The two bases have the same objective value.

> [!key] Facts about bases (non-degenerate LPs)
> - **Weak duality, basis by basis:** every feasible basis has value at most every locally optimal basis — every feasible vertex lies above every locally optimal vertex.
> - **Strong duality:** if the LP has an optimum, it is the **unique** basis that is both feasible and locally optimal — the lowest feasible vertex and the highest locally optimal vertex.
> - No feasible basis: the LP is infeasible. No locally optimal basis: the LP is unbounded.

(The last line needs non-degeneracy; the note's footnote 2 points out that degenerate LPs can have no locally optimal basis and still be infeasible.)

> [!trap] Basis traps
> - **Erickson's basis is the set of $d$ tight constraints.** In the textbook tableau method the "basic variables" are the $n$ variables (original or slack) that are *not* forced to 0 — the complement. A tight sign constraint $x_j\ge 0$ is a non-basic $x_j$; a tight row is a non-basic slack variable.
> - **A basis need not be feasible.** Most vertices of the arrangement lie outside the polyhedron; the dual simplex method lives on them.
> - **Locally optimal is not optimal**: it ignores every constraint outside the basis.

## I.2 The simplex algorithm

### Primal: falling marbles

Dantzig's simplex method, in the note's geometric form. The input is the set $H$ of halfspaces; "lower" means a larger objective value.

```text
PrimalSimplex(H):
    if ∩H = ∅
        return Infeasible
    x ← any feasible vertex
    while x is not locally optimal
        ⟨⟨pivot downward, maintaining feasibility⟩⟩
        if every feasible neighbor of x is higher than x
            return Unbounded
        else
            x ← any feasible neighbor of x that is lower than x
    return x
```

**Why it is correct.** The vertex $x$ stays feasible and every **pivot** moves to a strictly lower vertex, so no vertex repeats and there are at most $\binom{n+d}{d}$ pivots. At the end either $x$ is feasible and locally optimal — the optimum — or $x$ is not locally optimal and has no lower feasible neighbor, so every edge of the polyhedron along which the objective improves is an unbounded ray: the LP is unbounded. (How to find the first feasible vertex, and to detect infeasibility, is §I.3.)

**Pivot rules.** The algorithm does not say *which* lower neighbor to take. There are dozens of **pivoting rules**; nearly all known ones are forced into exponentially many pivots on some polyhedron, and none is known to guarantee polynomially many pivots, even in expectation. Randomized rules do reach subexponential expected time — RandomFacet (Kalai 1992; Matoušek, Sharir and Welzl 1996) — but Friedmann, Hansen and Zwick showed in 2010 that RandomFacet is superpolynomial in the worst case. Hirsch conjectured in 1957 that the optimum is always within $n$ pivots of every feasible basis (for $n + d$ constraints, if the right pivots are chosen); Santos disproved it in 2010 with a 43-dimensional polytope with 86 facets that needs 44.

### Dual: rising bubbles

The same idea on the other side of duality: start from a locally optimal vertex and climb, keeping local optimality, until feasible. By strong duality the target is the highest locally optimal vertex.

```text
DualSimplex(H):
    if there is no locally optimal vertex
        return Unbounded
    x ← any locally optimal vertex
    while x is not feasible
        ⟨⟨pivot upward, maintaining local optimality⟩⟩
        if every locally optimal neighbor of x is lower than x
            return Infeasible
        else
            x ← any locally optimal neighbor of x that is higher than x
    return x
```

Again at most $\binom{n+d}{d}$ pivots; at the end $x$ is locally optimal and feasible (the optimum), or locally optimal, infeasible and without a higher locally optimal neighbor — then the LP is infeasible. **Running PrimalSimplex on an LP and DualSimplex on its dual is the same computation**: the bases correspond by complementation, feasible ↔ locally optimal, with equal values, so the two runs visit corresponding bases pivot for pivot. Only the geometric reading differs.

## I.3 Computing the initial basis

Both algorithms need a first vertex of the right kind. Two observations reduce each problem to the other algorithm:

1. **Feasibility depends only on $A$ and $b$, local optimality only on $A$ and $c$.** Changing the objective does not change which vertices are feasible; translating the hyperplanes (changing $b$) does not change which vertices are locally optimal.
2. **Every basis is locally optimal for some objective and feasible for some offsets.** For the objective, take any **positive combination of the $d$ normals** of the basis — their sum, say — so that all the forces are positive. For the offsets, translate the $d$ hyperplanes of the basis through one point and every other halfspace so that it strictly contains that point.

The note states the first half differently (p. 5): "any vector that has a positive inner product with each of the normal vectors of the $d$ chosen hyperplanes". **That is not enough** when $d\ge 2$: for $\max\ x + 2y$ subject to $x\le 0$ and $x + y\le 0$, the objective $c = (1, 2)$ has positive inner products $1$ and $3$ with the normals $(1, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$, yet $c = -1\cdot(1,0) + 2\cdot(1,1)$ has a negative coefficient and the vertex $(0,0)$ is not locally optimal — $(-1, 1)$ satisfies both constraints with value $1 > 0$. What is needed is a positive *combination* of the normals.

So: to start the primal simplex, pick any vertex, **rotate the objective** so it becomes locally optimal, and run the dual simplex for the rotated objective until it reaches a feasible vertex — which stays feasible when the original objective is restored. Symmetrically, to start the dual simplex, **translate the constraints** to make an arbitrary vertex feasible and run the primal simplex for the translated constraints until it is locally optimal.

```text
DualPrimalSimplex(H):                        ⟨⟨the note's Figure I.4⟩⟩
    x ← any vertex
    H′ ← H with the objective rotated to make x locally optimal
    while x is not feasible                  ⟨⟨dual simplex, w.r.t. H′⟩⟩
        if every locally optimal neighbor of x is lower (w.r.t. H′) than x
            return Infeasible
        else
            x ← any locally optimal neighbor of x that is higher (w.r.t. H′) than x
    while x is not locally optimal           ⟨⟨primal simplex, w.r.t. H⟩⟩
        if every feasible neighbor of x is higher than x
            return Unbounded
        else
            x ← any feasible neighbor of x that is lower than x
    return x
```

`PrimalDualSimplex` (Figure I.5) is the mirror image: translate $H$ so that $x$ becomes feasible, run the primal phase with respect to the translated $H'$ (returning Unbounded if it gets stuck), then the dual phase with respect to $H$ (returning Infeasible if it gets stuck). The rotated problem cannot be unbounded and the translated one cannot be infeasible, so each first phase ends at a vertex of the right kind or with the correct verdict. (The note's Exercise 4 develops the other standard start, an auxiliary LP with one extra variable $\lambda$.)

## I.4 Network simplex

(The note marks this section "needs revision", and closes it with a paragraph saying the presentation should be redone in terms of slacks pushed across cuts, with examples and figures. What it does contain is summarized here, corrected and checked.)

Shortest paths, maximum flows and minimum cuts are LPs, and reading the simplex method in terms of the graph gives a much faster implementation, **network simplex**. The note's example is the **transshipment problem** ([[4-lecture-notes/g-minimum-cost-flows#g4-transshipment-and-transportation|§G.4]]): a directed graph $G = (V,E)$, balances $b\colon V\to\mathbb{R}$ (demand positive, supply negative) and costs $\textsf{cost}\colon E\to\mathbb{R}$, no capacities; find a flow $f\ge 0$ with $\sum_u f(u\to v) - \sum_w f(v\to w) = b(v)$ at every vertex $v$ and least total cost $\sum_e\textsf{cost}(e)f(e)$. As an LP, with one balance constraint dropped (it is implied by the others):
$$
\text{minimize } \textsf{cost}\cdot f\quad\text{subject to}\quad Af = b,\quad f\ge 0,
$$
where $A$ is the $V\times E$ **incidence matrix**, $+1$ in row $v$, column $u\to v$ and $-1$ in row $u$. (The note writes *maximize*, contradicting its own sentence before, and describes $A$ with one row per edge, which does not fit $Af = b$; p. 7–8.) Non-degeneracy here means: no cycle of $G$, traversed in any directions, has total signed cost 0, and no non-empty proper subset of vertices has total balance 0.

The LP has $E$ variables, so a basis is a set of $E$ independent constraints. A **balanced** basis contains all $V - 1$ balance constraints, hence $E - V + 1$ constraints $f(e) = 0$ — the **fixed** edges; the other $V - 1$ edges are **free**.

> [!key] Lemma I.1
> For every balanced basis the free edges form a spanning tree of $G$ (ignoring directions); conversely, every spanning tree $T$ is the set of free edges of a balanced basis. The basis's location is the flow $\mathit{flow}_T$ that is 0 off $T$ and puts on each tree edge $u\to v$ the total balance of the component of $T - u\to v$ that contains $v$.

> [!proof]- Proof sketch (click to expand)
> If the free edges contained an undirected cycle, flow could be pushed around it without violating any basis constraint, so the constraints would not determine a unique point — not a basis. Hence $V - 1$ acyclic free edges: a spanning tree. Conversely, given $T$, the flow $\mathit{flow}_T$ satisfies every balance constraint (each tree edge $u\to v$ carries into $v$'s side exactly what that side demands, and nothing else crosses), and it is the only flow supported on $T$; so the balance constraints plus $f(e) = 0$ for $e\notin T$ are independent. $\square$

The note adds that every flow consistent with a balanced basis is "non-negative on every free edge" (p. 8). It is not: $\mathit{flow}_T$ is negative on a tree edge whenever the head side has net supply — which is exactly what makes a basis infeasible, as the next lines say.

For a non-tree edge $u\to v$ let $\mathit{cycle}_T(u\to v)$ be the cycle formed by $u\to v$ and the tree path from $v$ back to $u$ (tree edges may be traversed backward, counting their cost negatively), and define $\mathit{slack}_T(u\to v)$ as its cost, with $\mathit{slack}_T = 0$ on tree edges. Equivalently, with potentials $\pi$ that make every tree edge tight, the slack is the **reduced cost** $\textsf{cost}(u\to v) + \pi(u) - \pi(v)$ ([[4-lecture-notes/g-minimum-cost-flows#g3-node-potentials-and-reduced-costs|§G.3]]); it plays the role of the dual location.

> [!key] Feasible and locally optimal trees
> - The basis of $T$ is **feasible** iff $\mathit{flow}_T(e)\ge 0$ (hence $> 0$) on every tree edge.
> - It is **locally optimal** iff $\mathit{slack}_T(e)\ge 0$ (hence $> 0$) on every non-tree edge.
> - Complementary slackness is automatic: $\mathit{flow}_T(e)\cdot\mathit{slack}_T(e) = 0$ for every edge. The tree with both properties is optimal.

**Pivots.** A pivot adds a non-tree edge $e_{\rm in}$ and removes a tree edge $e_{\rm out}$ from the cycle that $e_{\rm in}$ closes (equivalently, $e_{\rm in}$ crosses the cut that removing $e_{\rm out}$ leaves). Let $S$ be the side of $T - e_{\rm out}$ that contains the tail of $e_{\rm out}$.

- **Primal network simplex** keeps a feasible tree — the first one from the feasible-flow algorithm of [[4-lecture-notes/f-balances-and-pseudoflows#f2-reduction-to-maximum-flow|note F]]. It picks an edge $e_{\rm in}$ of negative slack and pushes flow around $\mathit{cycle}_T(e_{\rm in})$ until a tree edge traversed backward empties; that edge is $e_{\rm out}$. The cost changes by $\mathit{flow}_T(e_{\rm out})\cdot\mathit{slack}_T(e_{\rm in}) < 0$ (the note says it *decreases* by this negative product). Here $e_{\rm in}$ leaves $S$, and the slacks change by $-\mathit{slack}_T(e_{\rm in})$ on edges from $S$ to $V\setminus S$ and by $+\mathit{slack}_T(e_{\rm in})$ on edges from $V\setminus S$ to $S$. If the cycle has no backward tree edge, it is a directed negative cycle and the LP is unbounded. **Primal network simplex is cycle canceling** ([[4-lecture-notes/g-minimum-cost-flows#g1-minimum-cost-circulations|§G.1]]), restricted to fundamental cycles of a tree.
- **Dual network simplex** keeps a locally optimal tree — for example a shortest-path tree for the costs from a root that reaches every vertex, when there is no negative cycle. It picks a tree edge $e_{\rm out}$ with negative flow, removes it, and adds the edge $e_{\rm in}$ of least slack **among the edges from $V\setminus S$ into $S$** (the note omits the direction); if there is none, the LP is infeasible. This time the slacks change the other way: $+\mathit{slack}_T(e_{\rm in})$ on edges from $S$ to $V\setminus S$ and $-\mathit{slack}_T(e_{\rm in})$ on edges into $S$, so that $e_{\rm in}$ gets slack 0 and no slack becomes negative; the cost rises by $\lvert\mathit{flow}_T(e_{\rm out})\rvert\cdot\mathit{slack}_T(e_{\rm in})$.

## I.5 Linear expected time for fixed dimensions

(Marked "needs careful revision" in the note.) In geometric applications the number of variables $d$ is a small constant while the number of constraints $n$ is large. Seidel's algorithm takes a set $H$ of $n$ halfspaces and a set $B$ of $b$ hyperplanes (the "basis" so far) and returns the lowest point of the intersection of the halfspaces of $H$ and the hyperplanes of $B$; at the top level $B = \varnothing$. A point **violates** a constraint if it is not in its halfspace.

```text
SeidelLP(H, B):
    if |B| = d
        return ∩B
    if |H ∪ B| = d
        return ∩(H ∪ B)
    h ← random element of H
    x ← SeidelLP(H \ h, B)                  ⟨⟨(∗)⟩⟩
    if x violates h
        return SeidelLP(H \ h, B ∪ ∂h)      ⟨⟨∂h: the boundary hyperplane of h⟩⟩
    else
        return x
```

**Why it is correct.** If the optimum $x$ of $H\setminus h$ satisfies $h$, it is also the optimum with $h$. If it violates $h$, the optimum with $h$ lies on the boundary $\partial h$ (by convexity, the segment from it to $x$ crosses $\partial h$, and the objective would improve along that segment otherwise), so the problem drops to one dimension less.

The algorithm assumes that the LP is feasible and that **every subproblem is bounded**. The note suggests guaranteeing boundedness by adding one halfspace to $H$; that halfspace can itself be drawn as $h$ and removed, so the bounding constraints must be kept apart from the random choices (a large box that is always present, never sampled). Also, the second base case returns the vertex $\cap(H\cup B)$, which is the lowest point only when that vertex is locally optimal — again a boundedness assumption.

**Analysis.** The optimum of $(H, B)$ is defined by $B$ and $d - b$ halfspaces of $H$, and the recursive answer $x$ of line $(\ast)$ is wrong exactly when $h$ is one of those $d - b$: the second call (the note's text writes it $\textsf{SeidelLP}(H, B\cup h)$; it is $\textsf{SeidelLP}(H\setminus h, B\cup\partial h)$, as in the code) happens with probability $(d - b)/n$, where $n = \lvert H\rvert$. With $\delta = d - b$ the note's recurrence is
$$
T(n,\delta) = T(n-1,\delta) + \frac{\delta}{n}\,T(n-1,\delta-1),\qquad T(n, 0) = T(\delta,\delta) = 1,
$$
and induction gives $T(n,\delta)\le\delta!\,(n-1) + \tfrac{\delta}{n}(\delta-1)!\,(n-1)\le\delta!\,n$. The note calls $T$ "the expected number of recursive calls", but without a term $1$ for the call itself it counts only the **base-case calls** (for $d = 1$ it gives $H_n$, while the algorithm always makes at least $n$ calls). The number of all calls satisfies the recurrence with $1 +$ added, whose solution is at most $\bigl(\sum_{k=0}^{\delta}\delta!/k!\bigr)\,n < e\,\delta!\,n$ — so $O(\delta!\,n)$ still holds, though not $\le\delta!\,n$. Each call does one $O(d)$ violation test, each base case solves a $d\times d$ system in $O(d^3)$ time, and the note states without proof that a careful analysis gives
$$
\textsf{SeidelLP}\text{ runs in } O(d!\cdot n) \text{ expected time} ,
$$
linear in $n$ for every fixed dimension $d$. The randomness is only the choice of $h$ in each call; the bound holds for every input.

## Beyond the note

Three facts the note leaves out, for orientation:

- **Dictionaries and tableaux.** The usual presentation keeps the LP in slack form and rewrites it at each pivot so that the $n$ basic variables are expressed in terms of the $d$ non-basic ones (set to 0); entering and leaving variables correspond to the constraints added to and removed from Erickson's basis. The [[concepts/simplex-algorithm|concept page]] works an example.
- **An exponential example.** With Dantzig's original rule (enter the variable with the largest objective coefficient), the Klee–Minty cube $\max\sum_j 10^{d-j}x_j$ subject to $2\sum_{j<i}10^{i-j}x_j + x_i\le 100^{i-1}$, $x\ge 0$, takes $2^d - 1$ pivots from the origin (checked for $d\le 7$).
- **Polynomial time.** LPs *can* be solved in polynomial time — by Khachiyan's ellipsoid method (1979) or Karmarkar's interior-point method (1984) — just not, as far as anyone knows, by any simplex pivot rule. This is the fact [[4-lecture-notes/j-approximation-algorithms#j6-lightest-vertex-cover-lp-rounding|LP rounding]] relies on.

## Summary

- A basis is $d$ of the $n + d$ constraints; its location is where they are tight. Feasible: the location satisfies everything. Locally optimal: $c$ is a non-negative combination of the basis normals = the complementary dual basis is feasible. Each basis has $dn$ neighbors; there are $\binom{n+d}{d}$ bases.
- Every feasible value ≤ every locally optimal value; the optimum is the unique basis that is both. No feasible basis: infeasible; no locally optimal basis: unbounded (non-degenerate LPs).
- PrimalSimplex: from a feasible vertex, pivot to better feasible neighbors until locally optimal (or Unbounded). DualSimplex: from a locally optimal vertex, pivot to worse locally optimal neighbors until feasible (or Infeasible). Same code on the dual. At most $\binom{n+d}{d}$ pivots; no pivot rule is known to be polynomial.
- Start: rotate the objective to a positive combination of a basis's normals (not merely positive inner products) and run the dual phase, or translate the constraints and run the primal phase.
- Network simplex for transshipment: balanced bases = spanning trees, $\mathit{flow}_T$ from subtree balances, $\mathit{slack}_T$ = fundamental-cycle cost = reduced cost; feasible iff flows ≥ 0, locally optimal iff slacks ≥ 0. Primal pivots cancel fundamental cycles; dual pivots shift potentials across fundamental cuts.
- SeidelLP: recurse without a random constraint; if the answer violates it, recurse on its boundary. Violation probability $(d-b)/n$; $O(d!\cdot n)$ expected time in fixed dimension.
- The note ends with 9 exercises (feasible and locally optimal neighbors; degenerate examples; separating red and blue points in $O(n)$ time; the auxiliary-LP start; solving any LP with a solver for feasible bounded ones; stretching a tree; the simplex method on the shortest-path and max-flow LPs; totally unimodular matrices).

> [!exam] For CS 374
> **Not on the CS/ECE 374 exams**: linear programming is not taught in CS 374 this semester (CS 473 material at Illinois). Two ideas carry over. A spanning tree as a basis — each non-tree edge closes one fundamental cycle, each tree edge defines one fundamental cut — is the picture behind the cycle and cut properties of [[2-algorithms/07-minimum-spanning-trees|minimum spanning trees]].
