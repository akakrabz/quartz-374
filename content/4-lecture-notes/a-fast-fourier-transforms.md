> [!abstract] In one breath
> A polynomial of degree less than $n$ can be stored as its $n$ **coefficients**, as its roots and a scale factor, or as its values at $n$ fixed **sample points**; each representation makes two of the three basic operations (evaluate, add, multiply) easy and the third slow, and multiplication is the slow one for coefficients. Converting coefficients to samples is multiplication by a **Vandermonde matrix**, $\Theta(n^2)$ time for arbitrary points, but the points can be chosen. Splitting $p(x) = p_{\text{even}}(x^2) + x\,p_{\text{odd}}(x^2)$ halves the work whenever squaring halves the set of points, and the **$n$th roots of unity** ($n$ a power of 2) have exactly that property: evaluating there is the **discrete Fourier transform**, and the radix-2 **fast Fourier transform** computes it in $O(n\log n)$ time by the mergesort recurrence. The inverse transform is the same algorithm with conjugated roots, divided by $n$ (Lemma A.1). So two polynomials are multiplied in $O(n\log n)$ time: transform both, multiply the samples pointwise, transform back. The note ends with Gauss's factoring for composite $n$ and the FFT's circuit: a bit-reversal permutation followed by a butterfly network.

It is divide and conquer ([[concepts/divide-and-conquer]]) applied to a numerical problem: the FFT's recurrence $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + \Theta(n)$ is mergesort's ([[2-algorithms/01-recursion#14-mergesort|§1.4]], [[concepts/master-theorem]]), and the problem it solves, multiplying polynomials, is the one Karatsuba's algorithm solves in $O(n^{\lg 3})$ time ([[2-algorithms/01-recursion#19-fast-multiplication|§1.9]]). **Not assigned in CS/ECE 374 this semester** (most of these notes are CS 473 material at Illinois); the same transform is the workhorse of digital signal processing, with the opposite sign convention (see the trap in §A.5). ## A.1 Polynomials

A **polynomial** in one variable is built from additions, subtractions and multiplications only:
$$
p(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{n} a_j x^j .
$$
The $a_j$ are its **coefficients**, and its **degree** is the largest $j$ with $a_j\ne 0$. A polynomial of degree $n$ is stored as an array $P[0\,..\,n]$ with $P[j] = a_j$. The three operations of the note are **evaluate** ($p(x)$ for a given number $x$), **add** ($r = p+q$, degree $\le n$) and **multiply** ($r = p\cdot q$; two polynomials of degree $n$ give degree $2n$). The school algorithms:

```text
Horner(P[0 .. n], x):                    Multiply(P[0 .. n], Q[0 .. m]):
  y ← P[n]                                 for j ← 0 to n + m
  for i ← n − 1 down to 0                      R[j] ← 0
      y ← x · y + P[i]                     for j ← 0 to n
  return y                                     for k ← 0 to m
                                                   R[j + k] ← R[j + k] + P[j] · Q[k]
                                           return R[0 .. n + m]
```

Evaluation from left to right, keeping the power $x^j$ in a variable, uses $O(n)$ arithmetic operations; **Horner's rule** $p(x) = a_0 + x(a_1 + x(a_2 + \cdots + x\,a_n))$ does it with only $n$ multiplications instead of $2n+2$. Addition is $O(n)$, coefficient by coefficient. Multiplication makes $(n+1)(m+1)$ products, **$\Theta(n^2)$** for two degree-$n$ polynomials. (Throughout, one arithmetic operation costs $O(1)$ time; the bit cost is a separate story — the fastest known algorithm for multiplying two $n$-bit integers, Harvey and van der Hoeven's of 2019, takes $O(n\log n)$ bit operations and is built on the FFT.)

Karatsuba's divide and conquer from Chapter 1 multiplies polynomials exactly as it multiplies integers, in $O(n^{\lg 3}) = O(n^{1.585})$ time ([[2-algorithms/01-recursion#19-fast-multiplication|§1.9]]); cleverer splittings reach $O(n^{1+\varepsilon})$ for any fixed $\varepsilon>0$, but with constants that make them impractical. The FFT does better by changing the representation.

## A.2 Alternate representations

**Roots.** By the fundamental theorem of algebra a polynomial of degree $n$ has $n$ complex roots $r_1,\dots,r_n$ (counted with multiplicity), so
$$
p(x) = s\prod_{j=1}^{n}(x - r_j)
$$
for a scale factor $s$: again $n+1$ numbers. Evaluation takes $O(n)$ time, and so does multiplication — multiply the scale factors and concatenate the lists of roots. Addition is hopeless: the roots of $p+q$ have nothing to do with those of $p$ and $q$, and for most polynomials of degree 5 or more the roots cannot even be computed exactly.

**Samples.** For $n+1$ points $(x_0,y_0),\dots,(x_n,y_n)$ with **distinct** $x_j$ there is exactly one polynomial $p$ of degree **at most** $n$ with $p(x_j) = y_j$ for all $j$ — two points determine a line — and $p$ **interpolates** the points. Once the **sample points** $x_j$ are agreed on, the values $y_j$ represent $p$. Adding and multiplying are now pointwise, $O(n)$ time, provided both polynomials use the same points; for a product of two degree-$n$ polynomials each factor must be sampled at $2n+1$ points, because the product needs that many. Evaluating at a new point uses **Lagrange's formula**:
$$
p(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{n} \frac{y_j}{\prod_{k\ne j}(x_j - x_k)}\;\prod_{k\ne j}(x - x_k),
$$
a sum of $n+1$ polynomials of degree $n$, the $j$th of which is $y_j$ at $x_j$ and $0$ at every other sample point; it takes $O(n^2)$ time. (The note's outer sum stops at $j = n-1$, which drops the last sample: through $(0,1), (1,2), (2,5)$ that version gives $0$ at $x = 2$ instead of $5$. The sum must run to $n$.)

> [!key] Three representations, three operations
> | representation | evaluate | add | multiply |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | coefficients | $O(n)$ | $O(n)$ | $O(n^2)$ |
> | roots + scale | $O(n)$ | impossible in general | $O(n)$ |
> | samples | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n)$ | $O(n)$ |
>
> Each representation does two operations in linear time and the third slowly, if at all.

## A.3 Converting between representations

The fix is to convert quickly to whichever representation makes the current operation easy. Roots are out — they cannot be computed exactly — so the question is coefficients ⇄ samples. Evaluating at each of the sample points with Horner's rule converts coefficients to samples in $O(n^2)$ time; Lagrange's formula converts back, in $O(n^3)$ time if its products are expanded with the school algorithms.

From here on the polynomial has degree at most $n-1$, so $n$ is both the number of coefficients and the number of samples. Fix sample points $x_0,\dots,x_{n-1}$ and let $V$ be the $n\times n$ **Vandermonde matrix** with entries $v_{jk} = x_j^{\,k}$ (rows and columns indexed from 0). Then the coefficient vector $\vec a$ and the sample vector $\vec y$ satisfy
$$
V\vec a = \vec y, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad y_j = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} a_k x_j^{\,k} .
$$
Coefficients → samples is a matrix–vector product, $O(n^2)$ time. Samples → coefficients is solving the linear system, $O(n^3)$ by Gaussian elimination — but because the sample points are fixed in advance, $V^{-1}$ can be computed once and hard-coded into the algorithm, so that direction is a matrix–vector product too: $\vec a = V^{-1}\vec y$, $O(n^2)$ time. ($V$ is invertible because its determinant is $\prod_{j<k}(x_k - x_j)\ne 0$ for distinct points; that is the uniqueness of interpolation again.)

So far nothing is gained over the school algorithms. The remaining freedom is the **choice of the sample points**: with points that have the right recursive structure, the matrix–vector product can be computed much faster.

## A.4 Divide and conquer

Split a polynomial of degree at most $n-1$ into its even and odd coefficients:
$$
p(x) = p_{\text{even}}(x^2) + x\cdot p_{\text{odd}}(x^2),
$$
where $p_{\text{even}}$ has coefficients $a_0, a_2, a_4,\dots$ and $p_{\text{odd}}$ has $a_1, a_3, a_5,\dots$, both of degree at most $n/2-1$. For example, $1 + 2x + 3x^2 + 4x^3 = (1 + 3x^2) + x\,(2 + 4x^2)$. So $p(x)$ costs two half-size evaluations at $x^2$ plus $O(1)$ more operations. This pays off only if the squares of the sample points are **fewer** than the points.

> [!definition] Definition — collapsing set
> A set $X$ of $n$ numbers is **collapsing** if either $X$ has one element, or the set of squares $X^2 = \{x^2\mid x\in X\}$ has exactly $n/2$ elements and is itself collapsing. The size of a collapsing set is a power of 2.

To evaluate $p$ (degree $\le n-1$) at every point of a collapsing set $X$ of size $n$: (1) recursively evaluate $p_{\text{even}}$ at every point of $X^2$; (2) the same for $p_{\text{odd}}$; (3) for each $x\in X$ set $p(x) = p_{\text{even}}(x^2) + x\,p_{\text{odd}}(x^2)$, two values the recursion has already computed. The recurrence is mergesort's,
$$
T(n) = 2T(n/2) + \Theta(n) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T(n) = \Theta(n\log n).
$$

Collapsing sets of every power-of-2 size exist: if $X$ is collapsing, has $n$ elements and does not contain $0$, then $\sqrt X = \{\pm\sqrt x\mid x\in X\}$ is collapsing with $2n$ elements (squaring sends $\sqrt x$ and $-\sqrt x$ to the same $x$). Starting from $X_1 = \{1\}$:
$$
X_2 = \{1, -1\},\qquad X_4 = \{1, -1, i, -i\},\qquad X_8 = X_4\cup\Bigl\{\pm\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}(1+i),\ \pm\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}(1-i)\Bigr\}.
$$
Here and below $i = \sqrt{-1}$, not an index. Real numbers are no longer enough: $\{1, 2\}$, for instance, is not collapsing, because $\{1, 4\}$ still has two elements. (The note uses "collapsing" and "collapsible" for the same thing.)

## A.5 The discrete Fourier transform

The elements of $X_n$ are the **complex $n$th roots of unity**, the $n$ roots of $x^n - 1$, equally spaced around the unit circle. All are powers of the **primitive** $n$th root
$$
\omega_n = e^{2\pi i/n} = \cos\frac{2\pi}{n} + i\sin\frac{2\pi}{n},\qquad \omega_n^k = e^{2\pi i k/n} = \cos\frac{2\pi k}{n} + i\sin\frac{2\pi k}{n}.
$$

> [!key] Properties of the roots of unity (all integers $n\ge1$ and $k$)
> - **Periodicity:** $\omega_n^k = \omega_n^{k \bmod n}$, so there are exactly $n$ distinct $n$th roots of unity.
> - **Halfway is minus:** if $n$ is even, $\omega_n^{k+n/2} = -\omega_n^k$; in particular $\omega_n^{n/2} = -1$.
> - **Inverse is conjugate:** $1/\omega_n^k = \omega_n^{-k} = \overline{\omega_n^k} = (\overline{\omega_n})^k$, where $\overline{a+bi} = a - bi$.
> - **Nesting:** $\omega_n = \omega_{kn}^k$ for $k\ge1$; every $n$th root of unity is a $(kn)$th root of unity.
>
> Together: for $n$ even the squares of the $n$th roots are the $(n/2)$th roots, each hit twice ($(\omega_n^k)^2 = \omega_{n/2}^k$), so **for $n$ a power of 2 the $n$th roots of unity form a collapsing set**.

> [!definition] Definition — discrete Fourier transform
> The **discrete Fourier transform** (DFT) of a coefficient vector $P[0\,..\,n-1]$ is the vector of its samples at the $n$th roots of unity:
> $$
> P^*[j] = p(\omega_n^j) = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} P[k]\,\omega_n^{jk},\qquad 0\le j\le n-1.
> $$
> In matrix form $P^* = V P$ with the Vandermonde matrix $v_{jk} = \omega_n^{jk}$.

The **fast Fourier transform** (FFT) computes the DFT by the collapsing-set recursion. For $n$ not a power of 2, pad $P$ with zeros up to the next power of 2 (fewer than $n$ extra entries). The algorithm, popularized by Cooley and Tukey in 1965, is the note's Figure A.1:

```text
Radix2FFT(P[0 .. n − 1]):
  if n = 1
      return P
  for j ← 0 to n/2 − 1
      U[j] ← P[2j]                      ⟨⟨even coefficients⟩⟩
      V[j] ← P[2j + 1]                  ⟨⟨odd coefficients⟩⟩
  U* ← Radix2FFT(U[0 .. n/2 − 1])
  V* ← Radix2FFT(V[0 .. n/2 − 1])
  ωₙ ← cos(2π/n) + i sin(2π/n)
  ω ← 1
  for j ← 0 to n/2 − 1
      P*[j]       ← U*[j] + ω · V*[j]
      P*[j + n/2] ← U*[j] − ω · V*[j]
      ω ← ω · ωₙ
  return P*[0 .. n − 1]
```

**Why it is correct.** $U^*[j] = p_{\text{even}}(\omega_{n/2}^j) = p_{\text{even}}(\omega_n^{2j})$, and the same for $V^*$; at the loop's $j$th iteration $\omega = \omega_n^j$, so the first line computes $p_{\text{even}}((\omega_n^j)^2) + \omega_n^j\,p_{\text{odd}}((\omega_n^j)^2) = p(\omega_n^j)$. For $j + n/2$ the square is the same, $(\omega_n^{j+n/2})^2 = \omega_n^{2j}$, while $\omega_n^{j+n/2} = -\omega_n^j$: hence the minus sign. **Running time:** $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + \Theta(n) = \Theta(n\log n)$ arithmetic operations — exactly $(n/2)\lg n$ multiplications by $\omega$ and $n\lg n$ additions and subtractions.

> [!example] Example — the DFT of $P = [1, 2, 3, 4]$
> Here $p(x) = 1 + 2x + 3x^2 + 4x^3$, $n = 4$, $\omega_4 = i$. The recursion splits $U = [1,3]$ and $V = [2,4]$, with $U^* = [1+3,\ 1-3] = [4,-2]$ and $V^* = [6, -2]$. Combining with $\omega = 1$ and then $\omega = i$:
> $$
> P^* = [\,4 + 6,\ \ -2 + i(-2),\ \ 4 - 6,\ \ -2 - i(-2)\,] = [\,10,\ -2-2i,\ -2,\ -2+2i\,],
> $$
> which are $p(1)$, $p(i)$, $p(-1)$ and $p(-i)$.

> [!trap] Sign conventions
> The note (like most algorithms texts) uses $\omega_n = e^{+2\pi i/n}$. Signal processing and libraries such as `numpy.fft.fft` use $e^{-2\pi i/n}$ for the forward transform; the note's DFT is `n * numpy.fft.ifft(P)`. Both conventions give the same algorithms with $i$ replaced by $-i$; mixing them silently conjugates every sample.

The note's history paragraph: variants of the algorithm were published many times between 1828 and 1958, and Gauss described and used the full recursive algorithm around 1805 — before Fourier's own work on harmonic analysis — to compute asteroid orbits; the first explicit discrete "Fourier" transform is Clairaut's, from 1754. The names stuck anyway.

## A.6 More general factoring

The algorithm of Figure A.1 is the **radix-2** FFT. Gauss, and later Cooley and Tukey, split a DFT of any **composite** order $n = pq$ into DFTs of orders $p$ and $q$. Write the input index as $jq + k$ ($0\le j<p$, $0\le k<q$) and the output index as $s + tp$ ($0\le s<p$, $0\le t<q$). Expanding $(s + tp)(jq + k)$ and using $\omega_{pq}^{q} = \omega_p$, $\omega_{pq}^{p} = \omega_q$ and $\omega_{pq}^{pq} = 1$:
$$
P^*[s + tp] = \sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\Biggl(\Bigl(\sum_{j=0}^{p-1} P[jq+k]\,\omega_p^{sj}\Bigr)\,\omega_{pq}^{sk}\Biggr)\,\omega_q^{tk}.
$$
The inner sum is entry $s$ of an order-$p$ DFT, the outer sum entry $t$ of an order-$q$ DFT, and the factors $\omega_{pq}^{sk}$ in between are the **twiddle factors** (the official name). Read off as an algorithm: write the input into a $p\times q$ array row by row; take an order-$p$ DFT of every column; multiply entry $(s,k)$ by $\omega_{pq}^{sk}$; take an order-$q$ DFT of every row; read the output column by column.

```text
FactorFFT(P[0 .. pq − 1]):
  for j ← 0 to p − 1
      for k ← 0 to q − 1
          A[j, k] ← P[jq + k]                     ⟨⟨row-major⟩⟩
  for k ← 0 to q − 1
      B[·, k] ← FFT(A[·, k])                       ⟨⟨order p, each column⟩⟩
  for j ← 0 to p − 1
      for k ← 0 to q − 1
          B[j, k] ← B[j, k] · ω_pq^(jk)            ⟨⟨twiddle factors⟩⟩
  for j ← 0 to p − 1
      C[j, ·] ← FFT(B[j, ·])                       ⟨⟨order q, each row⟩⟩
  for j ← 0 to p − 1
      for k ← 0 to q − 1
          P*[j + kp] ← C[j, k]                     ⟨⟨column-major⟩⟩
  return P*[0 .. pq − 1]
```

> [!warning] Corrections to the note's Figure A.2 and derivation (pp. 9–10)
> The figure reads $P[jp+k]$ and writes $P^*[j + kq]$; for $p\ne q$ these are wrong (with $p=2$, $q=3$ it reads index 2 twice, never reads 5, and writes to the nonexistent indices 6 and 7). Row-major is $P[jq+k]$ and column-major is $P^*[j+kp]$. Its twiddle loop scales a whole column $B[\cdot,k]$ instead of the single entry $B[j,k]$, and its row transforms read an undefined array $C[j,\cdot]$ instead of $B[j,\cdot]$. In the derivation on p. 9 the first line mixes up the summation index (it should be $\sum_{\ell} x_\ell\,\omega_{pq}^{(aq+b)\ell}$), and the derivation indexes with $p$ and $q$ in the opposite roles from the algorithm, so its inner sum is the order-$q$ DFT and its outer sum the order-$p$ DFT — the text says "order $q$" for both. The version above is consistent and was checked against the DFT for 13 shapes $p\times q$.

**Radix 2 is the case $p = n/2$, $q = 2$:** the two columns are the even and the odd coefficients, the column transforms give $U^*$ and $V^*$, the twiddle factor on column 1 is $\omega_n^j$, and each row's order-2 DFT is $(u + t,\ u - t)$ — exactly the combining loop of Radix2FFT. **Running time:** $T(pq) = q\,T(p) + p\,T(q) + O(pq)$. Applied recursively, it runs in $O(n\log n)$ time whenever every prime factor of $n$ is bounded by a constant (the leaves are DFTs of constant size). For prime $n$, the algorithms of Rader and of Bluestein reduce the DFT to FFTs whose orders have only small prime factors, so a DFT of **any** order $n$ takes $O(n\log n)$ time.

## A.7 Inverting the FFT

To get the coefficients of a product back from its samples we need the inverse DFT, multiplication by $V^{-1}$ where $v_{jk} = \omega_n^{jk}$. It is almost $V$ itself.

> [!key] Lemma A.1
> $V^{-1} = \overline{V}/n$: the inverse DFT matrix has entries $\omega_n^{-jk}/n$.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> It suffices to show that $M = V\overline{V}$ is $n$ times the identity. Its entry
> $$
> m_{jk} = \sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\omega_n^{j\ell}\,\overline{\omega_n^{\ell k}} = \sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\omega_n^{j\ell - \ell k} = \sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}\bigl(\omega_n^{j-k}\bigr)^\ell .
> $$
> If $j = k$ every term is $\omega_n^0 = 1$ and $m_{jj} = n$. If $j\ne k$ then $0 < |j-k| < n$, so $\omega_n^{j-k}\ne 1$ and the geometric series sums to
> $$
> m_{jk} = \frac{(\omega_n^{j-k})^n - 1}{\omega_n^{j-k} - 1} = \frac{(\omega_n^n)^{j-k} - 1}{\omega_n^{j-k} - 1} = \frac{1 - 1}{\omega_n^{j-k} - 1} = 0. \qquad\square
> $$

So any DFT algorithm also computes the inverse. The generic way: since $V^{-1}y = \overline{V\,\overline{y}}/n$, conjugate the input, run the FFT, conjugate the output and divide by $n$. Or rewrite the radix-2 algorithm with the conjugate root $\overline{\omega_n} = \cos\frac{2\pi}{n} - i\sin\frac{2\pi}{n}$ and a division by 2 at every level ($\lg n$ halvings make $1/n$):

```text
InverseFFT(P*[0 .. n − 1]):              InverseRadix2FFT(P*[0 .. n − 1]):
  P ← FFT(conj(P*))                        if n = 1
  for j ← 0 to n − 1                           return P*
      P[j] ← conj(P[j]) / n                for j ← 0 to n/2 − 1
  return P[0 .. n − 1]                         U*[j] ← P*[2j];  V*[j] ← P*[2j + 1]
                                           U ← InverseRadix2FFT(U*[0 .. n/2 − 1])
                                           V ← InverseRadix2FFT(V*[0 .. n/2 − 1])
                                           ω̄ₙ ← cos(2π/n) − i sin(2π/n);  ω̄ ← 1
                                           for j ← 0 to n/2 − 1
                                               P[j]       ← (U[j] + ω̄ · V[j]) / 2
                                               P[j + n/2] ← (U[j] − ω̄ · V[j]) / 2
                                               ω̄ ← ω̄ · ω̄ₙ
                                           return P[0 .. n − 1]
```

Both take $O(n\log n)$ time. (In the note's Figure A.3 the base case of InverseRadix2FFT returns $P$, which is not yet defined there; it should return its input $P^*$. The figure's generic InverseFFT also leaves out the first conjugation: it transforms $P^*$ itself and conjugates only the output, which gives $\overline{V}\,\overline{P^*}/n$ — correct only for real $P^*$. For the samples of a real polynomial it returns the coefficients in cyclically reversed order $P[0], P[n-1], \dots, P[1]$: on the example of §A.8 it gives $[3, 0, 8, 10]$ instead of $[3, 10, 8, 0]$.)

> [!example] Example — $V$ for $n = 4$
> $$
> V = \begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&1\\ 1&i&-1&-i\\ 1&-1&1&-1\\ 1&-i&-1&i\end{pmatrix},\qquad V\overline{V} = 4I,
> $$
> because two different rows are orthogonal (for instance row 1 times the conjugate of row 3: $1 + i\cdot\overline{(-i)} + (-1)(-1) + (-i)\cdot\overline{i} = 1 - 1 + 1 - 1 = 0$).

## A.8 Fast polynomial multiplication

> [!key] Polynomial multiplication in $O(n\log n)$
> ```text
> FFTMultiply(P[0 .. m − 1], Q[0 .. n − 1]):
>   for j ← m to m + n − 1
>       P[j] ← 0
>   for j ← n to m + n − 1
>       Q[j] ← 0
>   P* ← FFT(P)
>   Q* ← FFT(Q)
>   for j ← 0 to m + n − 1
>       R*[j] ← P*[j] · Q*[j]
>   return InverseFFT(R*)
> ```
> Two polynomials with $m$ and $n$ coefficients are multiplied in $\Theta((m+n)\log(m+n))$ arithmetic operations. With the radix-2 FFT, pad both to the next power of 2 that is at least $m+n$ instead.

**Why it is correct.** The product has $m+n-1$ coefficients, so its samples at $m+n$ (or more) points determine it; the samples of $p\cdot q$ are the products of the samples of $p$ and $q$; and the inverse transform turns samples back into coefficients (Lemma A.1). **Time:** two forward transforms, $O(m+n)$ multiplications, one inverse transform. With integer coefficients the floating-point result is rounded to the nearest integers; in the check of this page 400 random pairs of polynomials (up to 300 coefficients in $[-1000, 1000]$) came out exactly equal to the school product.

## A.9 Inside the radix-2 FFT

Unrolled completely, Radix2FFT is a circuit (the note's Figure A.5, for $n = 16$) with two parts.

- **The bit-reversal permutation.** Splitting into even and odd positions at every level sends input $P[k]$ to the position whose binary representation is that of $k$ read backward. For $n = 8$, $P[3] = P[011_2]$ ends up in position $110_2 = 6$, and the inputs line up as $P[0], P[4], P[2], P[6], P[1], P[5], P[3], P[7]$. (In Figure A.5 one label is wrong: the last column of row $1100$ should read $0011$, not $0010$, which appears twice.)
- **The butterfly network.** $\lg n$ stages of $n/2$ *butterflies* each — multiply one input by a twiddle factor, output the sum and the difference — combine the pieces into DFTs of sizes $2, 4, \dots, n$. Butterfly networks also route messages between processors in parallel computers, connecting any two in $O(\log n)$ steps.

### The circuit as a loop

The same structure gives the usual non-recursive, in-place FFT (not in the note; the standard textbook form, checked against Radix2FFT up to $n = 4096$):

```text
IterativeFFT(P[0 .. n − 1]):
  for k ← 0 to n − 1
      A[rev(k)] ← P[k]                       ⟨⟨rev(k): the lg n bits of k reversed⟩⟩
  for s ← 1 to lg n                          ⟨⟨stage s: 2^s-point DFTs⟩⟩
      m ← 2^s;  ωₘ ← cos(2π/m) + i sin(2π/m)
      for b ← 0 to n − 1 step m
          ω ← 1
          for j ← 0 to m/2 − 1
              u ← A[b + j];  t ← ω · A[b + j + m/2]
              A[b + j] ← u + t;  A[b + j + m/2] ← u − t
              ω ← ω · ωₘ
  return A[0 .. n − 1]
```

Applying FactorFFT recursively to a power of 2, splitting into any smaller powers of 2, gives the same circuit with its stages grouped differently. For many uses of the FFT, polynomial multiplication included, the bit-reversal permutation can be skipped altogether: a transform whose outputs come out in bit-reversed order, followed by an inverse transform that accepts them in that order, still multiplies correctly.

## Summary

- Coefficients, roots + scale, samples: each representation does two of evaluate / add / multiply in $O(n)$ time; multiplying coefficient vectors directly is $\Theta(n^2)$ (Karatsuba: $O(n^{\lg 3})$).
- $n$ samples at distinct points determine a polynomial of degree $< n$; coefficients → samples is multiplication by the Vandermonde matrix, $O(n^2)$ in general (Lagrange: $\sum_{j=0}^{n}$, not $n-1$).
- $p(x) = p_{\text{even}}(x^2) + x\,p_{\text{odd}}(x^2)$: evaluation on a collapsing set costs $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + \Theta(n) = \Theta(n\log n)$; the $n$th roots of unity, $n = 2^k$, are collapsing because $\omega_n^{k+n/2} = -\omega_n^k$.
- DFT: $P^*[j] = p(\omega_n^j) = \sum_k P[k]\,\omega_n^{jk}$. Radix2FFT computes it with $(n/2)\lg n$ butterfly multiplications.
- Lemma A.1: $V^{-1} = \overline{V}/n$, so the inverse DFT is "conjugate, FFT, conjugate, divide by $n$" — also $O(n\log n)$.
- FFTMultiply: pad to $\ge m+n-1$, FFT both, multiply pointwise, inverse FFT: $\Theta((m+n)\log(m+n))$.
- General $n = pq$: order-$p$ DFTs, twiddle factors $\omega_{pq}^{jk}$, order-$q$ DFTs ($O(n\log n)$ for any $n$ with Rader/Bluestein); the radix-2 circuit is a bit-reversal permutation followed by a butterfly network.
- The note ends with 11 exercises (Minkowski sums, well-spaced triples, 3SUM with small integers, bit reversal in $O(n)$ time, counting pencil sets, a radix-3 FFT, the Walsh–Hadamard and Hartley transforms, recursive FactorFFT, a factoring recurrence, cache misses).
