> [!abstract] In one breath
> Give every edge a **cost per unit of flow**, of any sign, and ask for the feasible flow — capacities, lower bounds and vertex balances respected — of least total cost. In the residual graph a backward edge costs minus its edge's cost, and a feasible flow is optimal **if and only if its residual graph has no negative cycle**. **Cycle canceling** (Klein) starts from a feasible flow and pushes flow around negative residual cycles, found by Bellman–Ford; **successive shortest paths** starts from a pseudoflow with no negative residual cycle and pushes along *shortest* excess-to-deficit paths, which keeps it that way (Lemma G.1). **Node potentials** — the reweighting of Johnson's algorithm, which Edmonds, Karp and Tomizawa invented for exactly this purpose — make every residual cost non-negative, so each shortest path costs one Dijkstra. Uncapacitated special cases have their own names: **transshipment**, **transportation** (bipartite, to which every instance reduces) and **assignment** (minimum-cost perfect matching); with a single supply vertex, transshipment *is* the shortest-path problem.

## G.1 Minimum-cost circulations

> [!definition] Definition — minimum-cost flow
> A network has capacities $c(e)$, lower bounds $\ell(e)$ with $0\le\ell(e)\le c(e)$, balances $b(v)$ with $\sum_v b(v) = 0$ (inflow minus outflow, as in note F: positive is demand), and a **cost** $\textsf{cost}(e)\in\mathbb{R}$ per unit of flow on every edge (the note writes it with a dollar sign). A flow is **feasible** if $\ell(e)\le f(e)\le c(e)$ on every edge and the net inflow at every vertex is $b(v)$. Its cost is
> $$
> \textsf{cost}(f) = \sum_{e\in E}\textsf{cost}(e)\cdot f(e),
> $$
> and the **minimum-cost flow problem** asks for a feasible flow of least cost. With all balances and lower bounds zero, a feasible flow is a **circulation** — a sum of directed cycles — and the problem is **minimum-cost circulation**.

**Maximum flow is a special case.** Give every edge of an $(s,t)$-network cost 0, and add an edge $t\to s$ of infinite capacity and cost $-1$. A feasible $(s,t)$-flow $f$ extends to a circulation by $f(t\to s) = \lvert f\rvert$, of cost $-\lvert f\rvert$; conversely a circulation restricted to the old edges is an $(s,t)$-flow of value $+f(t\to s)$ (the note says $-f(t\to s)$, p. 2: conservation at $s$ makes the flow returning on $t\to s$ equal to the net flow leaving $s$). So a minimum-cost circulation is a maximum flow.

### Cycle canceling

In the residual graph $G_f$ of a flow, a forward residual edge keeps its edge's cost and a backward residual edge $v\to u$, which cancels flow on $u\to v$, costs $-\textsf{cost}(u\to v)$. For a directed cycle $\gamma$ in $G_f$, let $c_f(\gamma)$ be its smallest residual capacity and $\textsf{cost}(\gamma)$ the sum of its residual costs. Pushing $c_f(\gamma)$ units around $\gamma$ — forward edges up, backward edges down — gives a feasible flow $f'$ with the same balances and
$$
\textsf{cost}(f') = \textsf{cost}(f) + c_f(\gamma)\cdot\textsf{cost}(\gamma).
$$

> [!key] Optimality criterion
> A feasible flow $f$ has minimum cost if and only if its residual graph $G_f$ has no negative-cost cycle.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> If $G_f$ has a negative cycle, pushing around it lowers the cost. Conversely, suppose $G_f$ has none, and let $g$ be any feasible flow. The difference $g - f$, read as a flow in $G_f$ (positive differences on forward edges, negative ones on backward edges), respects the residual capacities and has net inflow 0 at every vertex — a circulation in $G_f$. By the flow decomposition theorem ([[2-algorithms/10-maximum-flows-and-minimum-cuts#105-combining-and-decomposing-flows|§10.5]]) it is a positive combination of directed cycles of $G_f$, none negative, so $\textsf{cost}(g) - \textsf{cost}(f)\ge 0$. $\square$
>
> (The note "immediately concludes" both directions from the augmentation formula, p. 3; the direction "no negative cycle ⇒ optimal" needs the decomposition.)

**Klein's algorithm (1967), after Tolstoy's railway studies of the 1920s:** start with the zero circulation and augment around an arbitrary negative residual cycle until none is left. On the maximum-flow instance above, every negative residual cycle is an $s$–$t$ path closed by $t\to s$, so cycle canceling is Ford–Fulkerson.

**Analysis.** A negative cycle can be found in $O(VE)$ time by [[concepts/bellman-ford|Bellman–Ford]]: start from a new vertex with a 0-cost edge to every vertex; if some edge is still tense after $V$ rounds of relaxation, following predecessor pointers back from it leads into a negative cycle. With integer capacities and costs every cancellation lowers the cost by at least 1, so the algorithm stops after at most $\lvert\textsf{cost}(f^*)\rvert$ cancellations, $O(VE\cdot\lvert\textsf{cost}(f^*)\rvert)$ time; since $\textsf{cost}(f^*)\ge -ECK$ with $C = \max c$ and $K = \max\lvert\textsf{cost}\rvert$, that is $O(VE^2CK)$. As for Ford–Fulkerson, these bounds are exponential in the input size and tight, and with irrational capacities the algorithm may not terminate or even converge. (With integer capacities it always terminates, since the cost strictly decreases over finitely many integral circulations.)

**Choosing the cycle.** Some natural rules are NP-hard to follow, such as canceling the most negative cycle (with every cost $-1$ it is a longest cycle). The note also calls the negative cycle with fewest edges NP-hard (p. 4), but that one is polynomial: its length is the smallest $k$ for which some closed walk of $k$ edges has negative cost, which $k$ rounds of a Bellman–Ford-style recurrence from each start vertex detect, $O(V^2E)$ time in all. Goldberg and Tarjan (late 1980s) cancel a **minimum-mean cycle**, whose average cost per edge is smallest, found in $O(VE)$ time by Karp's algorithm; with dynamic trees and more machinery the note quotes $O(VE^2\log V)$ time. Their paper's abstract states $O(VE\log V\cdot\min\{\log(VC'), E\log V\})$ with $C'$ the largest absolute cost — $O(VE^2\log^2 V)$ independently of the numbers — so the note's bound appears to be missing a logarithm.

### The general problem in two stages

With lower bounds and balances: (1) find any feasible flow $f$, by the maximum-flow reduction of [[4-lecture-notes/f-balances-and-pseudoflows#f2-reduction-to-maximum-flow|note F]] (start from $\ell$, then balance), or report that none exists; (2) find a minimum-cost circulation $f'$ in the residual graph $G_f$, by cycle canceling; return $f + f'$. The residual graph must respect the lower bounds: a backward edge may cancel only $f(e) - \ell(e)$, not all of $f(e)$ as in Chapter 10 — with backward capacity $f(e)$, cycle canceling can push a flow below its lower bound. The note quotes $O(VE^2\log V)$ for the whole computation, by the Goldberg–Tarjan bound above.

## G.2 Successive shortest paths

Cycle canceling yields a characterization: a pseudoflow $\psi$ is a minimum-cost flow if and only if it is
- **feasible:** $\ell(e)\le\psi(e)\le c(e)$ on every edge,
- **balanced:** net inflow $b(v)$ at every vertex, and
- **locally optimal:** $G_\psi$ has no negative-cost cycle.

Cycle canceling keeps the first two and works toward the third. **Successive shortest paths** (Ford and Fulkerson in the 1950s; Jewell 1958, Iri 1960, Busacker and Gowen 1960) keeps the first and third and works toward the second. It needs two assumptions, each of which can be arranged at the cost of non-zero balances:
- **lower bounds are zero** — otherwise start from the pseudoflow $\ell$ and work in its residual graph;
- **costs are non-negative** — otherwise start from the pseudoflow that saturates every edge of cost $\le 0$; its residual graph has only non-negative costs.

Then the zero pseudoflow is feasible and locally optimal, and the algorithm repeatedly pushes flow along a cheapest path from an excess to a deficit, where a path's length is the sum of its residual costs.

```text
SuccessiveShortestPaths(V, E, c, b, cost):
    for every edge e ∈ E
        ψ(e) ← 0
    B ← Σ_v |b(v)| / 2
    while B > 0
        construct G_ψ
        s ← any vertex with b_ψ(s) < 0
        t ← any vertex with b_ψ(t) > 0
        σ ← a shortest path in G_ψ from s to t      ⟨⟨Bellman–Ford⟩⟩
        Augment(s, t, σ)
    return ψ

Augment(s, t, σ):
    R ← min{−b_ψ(s), b_ψ(t), min_{e∈σ} c_ψ(e)}
    B ← B − R
    for every edge u→v of σ
        if u→v ∈ E
            ψ(u→v) ← ψ(u→v) + R
        else
            ψ(v→u) ← ψ(v→u) − R                    ⟨⟨the note: ψ(e) ← ψ(e) − R⟩⟩
```

**Unreachable deficits.** The pseudocode assumes $t$ is reachable from $s$. The note enforces it by adding a vertex with zero balance and very expensive, uncapacitated edges to and from every vertex, and says that if a shortest path ever uses them, the instance is infeasible. With $s$ and $t$ chosen arbitrarily that is wrong: with two separate pairs $s_1\to t_1$ and $s_2\to t_2$, pairing $s_1$ with $t_2$ must go through the new vertex, although a feasible flow exists. Either choose $t$ among the deficits reachable from $s$ (as FeasibleFlow does), or run to the end and declare infeasibility only if the final flow uses the expensive edges.

The algorithm is FeasibleFlow with shortest augmenting paths, so it finds a feasible flow whenever one exists. Optimality, and even the existence of shortest paths, rests on the following lemma.

> [!key] Lemma G.1
> After every iteration of SuccessiveShortestPaths, $G_\psi$ has no negative cycle.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Induction. Initially all residual costs are non-negative. Suppose $G_\psi$ has no negative cycle, $\sigma$ is a shortest $s$–$t$ path in it, and augmenting along $\sigma$ gives $\psi'$ whose residual graph has a negative cycle $\gamma$. Every edge of $\gamma$ that is not in $G_\psi$ is the reversal of an edge of $\sigma$ (augmenting creates no other edges). So the sum $\sigma + \gamma$ of one unit along each, with those opposite pairs cancelling, is a non-negative flow of value 1 from $s$ to $t$ on edges of $G_\psi$ (capacities aside). Decompose it into one $s$–$t$ path $\sigma'$ and cycles of $G_\psi$, all of non-negative cost: $\textsf{cost}(\sigma')\le\textsf{cost}(\sigma) + \textsf{cost}(\gamma) < \textsf{cost}(\sigma)$, contradicting the choice of $\sigma$. $\square$

At the end $\psi$ is feasible, balanced and locally optimal: a minimum-cost flow. On the maximum-flow instance of §G.1 (after saturating $t\to s$, which needs a finite capacity such as $\sum_w c(s\to w)$), $s$ is the only excess, $t$ the only deficit, and the reversed edge $s\to t$ of cost 1 is the only edge that is not free: the algorithm is Ford–Fulkerson, followed by one push along $s\to t$.

**Analysis.** Each shortest path costs $O(VE)$ by Bellman–Ford — Dijkstra would do for the first one, but augmenting creates backward edges of negative cost. With integer capacities and balances each iteration lowers $B$ by at least 1, so there are at most $B$ iterations and $O(VEB)$ time (the note says "the cycle canceling algorithm", p. 7); exponential in the worst case, and possibly endless with irrational capacities.

> [!example] Example — successive shortest paths on the running example
> | iteration | shortest path | residual cost | units | $B$ after |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | 1 | $s\to a\to b\to t$ | $1 + 1 + 1 = 3$ | 2 ($a\to b$ full) | 2 |
> | 2 | $s\to b\to t$ | $3 + 1 = 4$ | 1 ($b\to t$ full) | 1 |
> | 3 | $s\to b\to a\to t$ | $3 - 1 + 4 = 6$ | 1 | 0 |
>
> Path costs never decrease (3, 4, 6). The third path uses $b\to a$ backward: it cancels one of the two units on $a\to b$, so that $a$ can send it to $t$. Total cost $2\cdot 3 + 4 + 6 = 16$, the flow of the first figure.

## G.3 Node potentials and reduced costs

Bellman–Ford in every iteration is the bottleneck. The fix — proposed by Jack Edmonds and Richard Karp (1969, published 1972) and independently by Nobuaki Tomizawa (1970, published 1971), and later borrowed by Donald Johnson for [[2-algorithms/09-all-pairs-shortest-paths#94-johnsons-algorithm|all-pairs shortest paths]] — is to give every vertex a **potential** $\pi(v)$ and use the **reduced costs**
$$
\textsf{cost}_\pi(u\to v) = \pi(u) + \textsf{cost}(u\to v) - \pi(v).
$$
Along any path $\alpha$ from $u$ to $v$ the intermediate potentials cancel: $\textsf{cost}_\pi(\alpha) = \pi(u) + \textsf{cost}(\alpha) - \pi(v)$. Hence shortest paths are the same for both cost functions, and every cycle has the same reduced cost as cost — so a network has a negative cycle if and only if it has a cycle of negative reduced cost.

> [!key] Lemma G.2
> A network has no negative-cost cycle if and only if some potential function makes every reduced cost non-negative.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> If $\gamma$ is a negative cycle, $\textsf{cost}_\pi(\gamma) = \textsf{cost}(\gamma) < 0$ for every $\pi$, so some edge of $\gamma$ has negative reduced cost. Conversely, without negative cycles shortest-path distances $\textsf{dist}(v)$ from a fixed vertex are well defined (add expensive edges from it to every vertex if needed, so that all are finite), and every edge satisfies $\textsf{dist}(v)\le\textsf{dist}(u) + \textsf{cost}(u\to v)$ — no edge is tense. With $\pi = \textsf{dist}$ that says $\textsf{cost}_\pi(u\to v)\ge 0$. $\square$

Starting with $\pi\equiv 0$ (all costs are non-negative), each iteration runs Dijkstra with the reduced costs and then folds the distances it found into the potentials:

```text
FastSuccessiveShortestPaths(V, E, c, b, cost):
    for every edge e ∈ E
        ψ(e) ← 0
    for every vertex v ∈ V
        π(v) ← 0
    B ← Σ_v |b(v)| / 2
    while B > 0
        construct G_ψ
        s ← any vertex with b_ψ(s) < 0
        t ← any vertex with b_ψ(t) > 0
        dist ← Dijkstra(G_ψ, s, cost_π)        ⟨⟨reduced costs, all ≥ 0⟩⟩
        σ ← the shortest path from s to t that Dijkstra found
        Augment(s, t, σ)
        for every vertex v ∈ V
            π(v) ← π(v) + dist(v)              ⟨⟨the note: π(v) ← dist(v)⟩⟩
    return ψ
```

> [!key] Lemma G.3
> After every iteration of FastSuccessiveShortestPaths, every edge of $G_\psi$ has non-negative reduced cost.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Induction; initially all costs are non-negative and $\pi = 0$. Suppose every edge of $G_\psi$ has $\textsf{cost}_\pi\ge 0$; let $\textsf{dist}$ be the distances from $s$ for $\textsf{cost}_\pi$, and $\pi' = \pi + \textsf{dist}$, so that $\textsf{cost}_{\pi'}(u\to v) = \textsf{dist}(u) + \textsf{cost}_\pi(u\to v) - \textsf{dist}(v)$. For an edge $u\to v$ that was already in $G_\psi$ this is $\ge 0$, because no edge is tense after Dijkstra. Otherwise $u\to v$ is the reversal of an edge $v\to u$ of the shortest path $\sigma$, so $\textsf{dist}(u) = \textsf{dist}(v) + \textsf{cost}_\pi(v\to u)$, and with $\textsf{cost}_\pi(u\to v) = -\textsf{cost}_\pi(v\to u)$ the reduced cost is exactly 0. $\square$

The proof — the note's own (p. 9) — computes reduced costs for $\pi + \textsf{dist}$; the note's pseudocode sets $\pi(v)\leftarrow\textsf{dist}(v)$ instead, which drops the old potentials, and then reduced costs can turn negative and Dijkstra can return wrong distances (the checks found such instances; the example below shows one negative reduced cost). It also initializes "$\pi(e)$" for $\pi(v)$. With the correct update, every shortest path costs one Dijkstra, $O(E\log V)$ with a binary heap, and the algorithm runs in **$O(BE\log V)$** time. Every vertex must be reachable for its distance to be finite — the expensive extra vertex of §G.2 takes care of that.

Orlin's algorithm (early 1990s), the fastest whose running time depends only on $V$ and $E$, reduces the problem to $O(E\log V)$ Dijkstra computations: $O(E^2\log^2 V)$ time. (Since 2022, algorithms of Chen, Kyng, Liu, Peng, Probst Gutenberg and Sachdeva solve minimum-cost flow with polynomially bounded integer data in almost-linear time, $E^{1+o(1)}$ — beyond the note.)

> [!example] Example — potentials on the running example
> | iteration | reduced distances $d(s), d(a), d(b), d(t)$ | path | potentials $\pi$ afterwards |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 1 | $0, 1, 2, 3$ | $s\,a\,b\,t$ | $0, 1, 2, 3$ |
> | 2 | $0, 1, 1, 1$ | $s\,b\,t$ | $0, 2, 3, 4$ |

A reduced distance converts back by $d(t) + \pi(t) - \pi(s)$: $1 + 3 = 4$ and $2 + 4 = 6$, the path costs of §G.2. With the note's update $\pi\leftarrow\textsf{dist} = (0, 1, 1, 1)$ after iteration 2, the residual edge $b\to s$ (cost $-3$) would get reduced cost $1 - 3 - 0 = -2$.

## G.4 Transshipment and transportation

A **transshipment** network has balances and costs but no capacities: every edge may carry any non-negative amount (Orden, 1956, for a practice as old as long-distance trade). Its vertices are **supply** (negative balance), **demand** (positive) or **transshipment** vertices (zero). A **transportation** problem (Hitchcock 1942, Koopmans 1947; Kantorovich and Tolstoy earlier, Monge's continuous version in the late 1700s) has no transshipment vertices and every edge goes from a supply to a demand: a bipartite network. Formulations that want a complete bipartite graph can add the missing edges with infinite cost.

**Every minimum-cost flow problem reduces to transportation** (Fulkerson 1960, generalizing Orden). Replace each edge $u\to v$ (bounds $\ell\le f\le c$, cost $k$) by a new vertex $z_{uv}$ with balance $c - \ell$ and uncapacitated edges $u\to z_{uv}$ of cost $k$ and $v\to z_{uv}$ of cost 0; then add $\ell$ to $b(u)$ and subtract $c$ from $b(v)$. A feasible $f$ becomes $f'(u\to z_{uv}) = f - \ell$ and $f'(v\to z_{uv}) = c - f$, and back by $f = f'(u\to z_{uv}) + \ell$; the cost changes by the constant $\ell k$ (the note writes the converse cost as $\textsf{cost}(f) + \ell k$, p. 11, for $\textsf{cost}(f') + \ell k$). Doing this to every edge gives a bipartite network with $V + E$ vertices and $2E$ uncapacitated edges, all from an original vertex to a new one, in $O(E)$ time; in a feasible instance every original vertex ends with balance $\le 0$, a supply.

**Assignment.** If every supply is $-1$ and every demand $+1$, transportation is the **assignment problem**: a minimum-cost perfect matching in a weighted bipartite graph (negate the costs for a maximum-weight one). Jacobi solved it in the early 1800s (published posthumously in 1865); Kuhn rediscovered the method in the 1950s and named it "Hungarian" after the work of Kőnig and Egerváry; Munkres showed it runs in $O(V^4)$ time, and with more care it runs in $O(V^3)$. A transportation instance with integer balances becomes an assignment instance by replacing each vertex $v$ with $\lvert b(v)\rvert$ copies — pseudo-polynomially larger.

> [!example] Example — a transportation problem
> Warehouses $p$ and $q$ supply 3 and 2 units; stores $x, y, z$ need 2, 2 and 1. Unit costs: from $p$, 4, 6, 9 to $x, y, z$; from $q$, 5, 3, 8. The cheapest plan sends $p\to x$ 2, $p\to z$ 1 and $q\to y$ 2, for $8 + 9 + 6 = 23$; every other integral plan costs at least 25. As an assignment problem: 5 copies of the warehouses ($p, p, p, q, q$) against 5 copies of the stores ($x, x, y, y, z$), also of minimum cost 23.

**One supply vertex: shortest paths.** If $s$ is the only supply vertex and there are no negative cycles, let $T$ be a shortest-path tree from $s$ ([[2-algorithms/08-shortest-paths#81-shortest-path-trees|§8.1]]) and $\textsf{dist}(v)$ the distances. The **canonical flow** puts on each tree edge $u\to v$ the total demand $\sum_{w\downarrow v} b(w)$ of $v$'s subtree ($w\downarrow v$: $w$ is a descendant of $v$) and 0 elsewhere; it is the sum of path flows of $b(v)$ units along the tree path to each $v$, so
$$
\textsf{cost}(f_T) = \sum_v\textsf{dist}(v)\cdot b(v),
$$
and it is optimal: a negative residual cycle would contain a shorter path to some vertex. When $T$ is unique it is exactly what successive shortest paths builds. Ford's generic relaxation algorithm ([[2-algorithms/08-shortest-paths#83-the-only-sssp-algorithm|§8.3]]) is morally cycle canceling: a tense edge $u\to v$ closes, with the residual tree path from $v$ back to $u$, a negative cycle, and relaxing it replaces $v$'s tree edge by $u\to v$. More generally, every transshipment network without negative cycles that has a minimum-cost flow has one that is positive only on the edges of a spanning tree (a forest, if the graph is disconnected) — the starting point of the network simplex method.

## Summary

- Minimum-cost flow: capacities, lower bounds, balances (inflow − outflow), costs of any sign; minimize $\sum_e\textsf{cost}(e)f(e)$. Maximum flow is the circulation case with an edge $t\to s$ of cost $-1$.
- Residual costs: forward edges keep their cost, backward edges cost its negative. A feasible flow is optimal iff its residual graph has no negative cycle (decomposition proves the hard direction).
- Cycle canceling (Klein): cancel negative residual cycles found by Bellman–Ford, $O(VE)$ each; $O(VE\lvert\textsf{cost}(f^*)\rvert) = O(VE^2CK)$ with integers. Minimum-mean cycles (Goldberg–Tarjan) make it strongly polynomial. The general problem: a feasible flow first (note F), then cancel cycles in its residual graph, with backward capacities $f - \ell$.
- Successive shortest paths: costs $\ge 0$ and lower bounds 0 (arranged by an initial pseudoflow); push along a shortest excess-to-deficit path; no negative residual cycle ever appears (Lemma G.1); $\le B$ iterations, $O(VEB)$ with Bellman–Ford.
- Potentials: $\textsf{cost}_\pi(u\to v) = \pi(u) + \textsf{cost}(u\to v) - \pi(v)$ preserves shortest paths and cycle costs; no negative cycle ⇔ some $\pi$ makes all reduced costs $\ge 0$ (Lemma G.2); updating $\pi\leftarrow\pi + \textsf{dist}$ keeps them $\ge 0$ (Lemma G.3), so Dijkstra suffices: $O(BE\log V)$. Orlin: $O(E^2\log^2 V)$.
- Transshipment (uncapacitated), transportation (bipartite; every instance reduces to it with $V + E$ vertices and $2E$ edges) and assignment (unit balances; Hungarian method, $O(V^3)$). With one supply vertex, transshipment is single-source shortest paths: $\textsf{cost} = \sum_v\textsf{dist}(v)\,b(v)$, carried by a shortest-path tree.
- The note ends with 5 exercises (Jacobi's assignment problem, series-parallel networks, committee assignment, cheapest loans, updating a minimum-cost flow when one cost changes).
