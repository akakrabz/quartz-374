> [!abstract] In one breath
> Drop the source and target and give every vertex a **balance** instead: how much flow it must absorb (positive, **demand**) or emit (negative, **supply**). A feasible flow obeys the capacities and every balance; one exists if and only if a new source feeding the supplies and a new target draining the demands admit a **saturating** maximum flow (Lemma F.1), and otherwise some cut $(S,T)$ has less capacity into $T$ than $T$'s net demand (Theorem F.2). Run directly on the original network, Ford–Fulkerson becomes **FeasibleFlow**: keep a **pseudoflow** — any function on the edges — and push flow along residual paths from a vertex with excess to a vertex with deficit until every balance is met. Two stages — first any feasible flow, then a maximum flow in its residual graph — handle maximum flows with balances and with **lower bounds** on the edges. Finally, **push-relabel** gives up on paths altogether: it floods the network from the source and pushes excess "downhill" along a height function, in $O(V^2E)$ time.

## F.1 Unbalanced flows

> [!definition] Definition — balances, flows, feasibility
> A flow network without source and target has capacities $c(e)\ge 0$ and a **balance function** $b\colon V\to\mathbb{R}$. A **flow** is now a function $f\colon E\to\mathbb{R}$ with
> $$
> \sum_u f(u\to v) - \sum_w f(v\to w) = b(v)\quad\text{for every vertex } v,
> $$
> net inflow equals balance: $b(v) > 0$ is a **demand** (deficit), $b(v) < 0$ a **supply** (excess). It is **feasible** if $0\le f(e)\le c(e)$ on every edge. The problem: find a feasible flow or prove that none exists. There is no value to maximize.

**The balances must sum to zero.** Summing the balance condition over all vertices counts every edge once with $+$ (at its head) and once with $-$ (at its tail), so $\sum_v b(v) = 0$ whenever a flow exists. That is necessary, not sufficient — the next section gives the exact condition.

## F.2 Reduction to maximum flow

Assume from here on that $\sum_v b(v) = 0$ (otherwise there is no flow). Build $G'$ from $G$: add a source $s$ with an edge $s\to v$ of capacity $-b(v)$ for every supply vertex ($b(v) < 0$), and a target $t$ with an edge $v\to t$ of capacity $b(v)$ for every demand vertex ($b(v) > 0$). Call an $(s,t)$-flow in $G'$ **saturating** if it saturates every edge out of $s$ — equivalently every edge into $t$, since both sets have total capacity $D = \sum_{b(v)>0} b(v)$. A saturating flow has value $D$ and is a maximum flow; so either every maximum flow of $G'$ is saturating or none is.

> [!key] Lemma F.1
> $G$ has a feasible flow if and only if $G'$ has a saturating $(s,t)$-flow.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Given a feasible flow $f$ in $G$, saturate every new edge and keep $f$ on $E$. Capacities hold, and at a vertex $v$ the new edge adds $-b(v)$ to the net inflow if $v$ is a supply ($s\to v$ carries $-b(v)$ in) or subtracts $b(v)$ if it is a demand ($v\to t$ carries $b(v)$ out); either way the net inflow becomes $b(v) - b(v) = 0$: conservation. Conversely, the restriction of a saturating flow to $E$ has net inflow exactly $b(v)$ at every $v$, by the same computation read backwards. $\square$

So a feasible flow costs one maximum-flow computation, $O(VE)$ time with Orlin's algorithm. When there is none, a cut says why.

> [!definition] Definition — infeasible cut
> For a partition $(S, T)$ of $V$, let $\lVert S,T\rVert = \sum_{u\in S,\,v\in T} c(u\to v)$ and $b(T) = \sum_{v\in T} b(v)$. The cut is **infeasible** if $\lVert S,T\rVert < b(T)$: $T$ needs more net inflow than the edges into it can carry.

> [!key] Theorem F.2
> Every flow network whose balances sum to zero has either a feasible flow or an infeasible cut (and not both).

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> (The note leaves this to the reader, as a consequence of the maxflow-mincut theorem, and omits the hypothesis $\sum_v b(v) = 0$; without it the theorem fails — a single vertex of balance $-1$ has neither.) *Not both:* for a feasible flow and any cut, $b(T)$ is the net flow into $T$, which is at most the flow on the edges from $S$ to $T$, which is at most $\lVert S,T\rVert$. *One of them:* if $G$ has no feasible flow, the maximum flow of $G'$ is less than $D$, so some $(s,t)$-cut $(S+s, T+t)$ of $G'$ has capacity less than $D$. That capacity is $\sum_{v\in T,\,b(v)<0}(-b(v)) + \lVert S,T\rVert + \sum_{v\in S,\,b(v)>0} b(v)$; subtracting it from $D = \sum_{v\in S,\,b(v)>0} b(v) + \sum_{v\in T,\,b(v)>0} b(v)$ gives $b(T) - \lVert S,T\rVert > 0$. $\square$

> [!example] Example — the note's network
> In the figures, $a, c, e$ supply $5 + 1 + 4 = 10$ units and $b, d, f$ absorb $2 + 6 + 2 = 10$. The flow of the first figure, with the six new edges of $G'$ saturated, is a saturating flow of value 10; so is the maximum flow any algorithm finds in $G'$. Had $c\to d$ capacity 5 instead of 15, the cut $T = \{d\}$ would be infeasible: $d$ needs net inflow 6, and only $c\to d$ enters it.

## F.3 Pseudoflows

Instead of reducing to maximum flow, run Ford–Fulkerson on $G'$ and watch what happens inside $G$. The algorithm no longer keeps a flow, only a pseudoflow that is gradually balanced.

> [!definition] Definition — pseudoflow, residual balance, residual network
> A **pseudoflow** is any function $\psi\colon E\to\mathbb{R}$; it is **feasible** if $0\le\psi\le c$. Its **residual capacities** are as in Chapter 10 — $c_\psi(u\to v) = c(u\to v) - \psi(u\to v)$ if $u\to v\in E$, and $c_\psi(u\to v) = \psi(v\to u)$ if $v\to u\in E$ — and the **residual balance** of $v$ is its balance minus its net inflow:
> $$
> b_\psi(v) = b(v) - \sum_u\psi(u\to v) + \sum_w\psi(v\to w).
> $$
> $\psi$ is a flow if and only if $b_\psi\equiv 0$. The **residual network** $G_\psi$ has the edges of positive residual capacity, and balances $b_\psi$; for $\psi = 0$ it is $G$.

An **augmenting path** is now a path in $G_\psi$ from a vertex with $b_\psi < 0$ (excess) to a vertex with $b_\psi > 0$ (deficit). Pushing $R$ units along a path from $u$ to $v$ raises $b_\psi(u)$ and lowers $b_\psi(v)$ by $R$ and leaves every interior vertex as it was; the largest useful $R$ is the minimum of the excess $-b_\psi(u)$, the deficit $b_\psi(v)$ and the path's residual capacities. Each push lowers the **total residual demand**
$$
B_\psi = \sum_{v}\lvert b_\psi(v)\rvert/2 = \sum_{v:\,b_\psi(v)>0} b_\psi(v)
$$
by $R$. (The note's $B_\psi = \sum_v\lvert b_\psi(v)\rvert$, p. 4, omits the $/2$: that sum is twice the total residual supply it is named for, and a push lowers it by $2R$; its pseudocode uses $\sum_v\lvert b(v)\rvert/2$.)

**When no augmenting path exists, a cut certifies infeasibility.** If a vertex $v$ with $b_\psi(v) < 0$ reaches no deficit, let $S$ be the set of vertices reachable from $v$ in $G_\psi$ and $T = V\setminus S$. No residual edge leaves $S$, so $\psi$ saturates every edge from $S$ to $T$ and is zero on every edge from $T$ to $S$; then $b_\psi(S) = b(S) + \lVert S,T\rVert$, and $b_\psi(S) < 0$ because no vertex of $S$ has a deficit and $v$ has an excess — so $\lVert S,T\rVert < -b(S) = b(T)$. Symmetrically, if a vertex with $b_\psi(v) > 0$ is reached by no excess, take $T$ = the vertices that **can reach** $v$. (The note, p. 4, swaps the two rules — "reachable from $v$" for a deficit, "can reach $v$" for an excess — and with the swap the "cut" can even be all of $V$; see the example below.)

```text
FeasibleFlow(V, E, c, b):
    for every edge e ∈ E
        ψ(e) ← 0
    B ← Σ_v |b(v)| / 2
    while B > 0
        construct G_ψ
        s ← any vertex with b_ψ(s) < 0
        if s cannot reach a vertex t in G_ψ with b_ψ(t) > 0
            return Infeasible
        t ← any vertex reachable from s with b_ψ(t) > 0
        π ← any path in G_ψ from s to t
        R ← min{−b_ψ(s), b_ψ(t), min_{e∈π} c_ψ(e)}
        B ← B − R
        for every edge u→v of π
            if u→v ∈ E
                ψ(u→v) ← ψ(u→v) + R        ⟨⟨push forward⟩⟩
            else
                ψ(v→u) ← ψ(v→u) − R        ⟨⟨cancel flow on v→u⟩⟩
    return ψ
```

(The note's last line reads $\psi(e)\leftarrow\psi(e) - R$ for a residual edge $e\notin E$, p. 5; it must update the reversed edge, as here.) It is Ford–Fulkerson on $G'$, with the same strengths and weaknesses. Each path takes $O(V+E)$ time by whatever-first search. With integer capacities and balances every push moves $R\ge 1$, so there are at most $B = \sum_v\lvert b(v)\rvert/2$ iterations; with irrational capacities it may run forever without converging (the note also wonders, in an editing remark, about irrational balances). Choosing the shortest augmenting path — over all excess–deficit pairs at once, by one breadth-first search started from every excess vertex, which is exactly Edmonds–Karp in $G'$ — gives $O(VE^2)$ time.

> [!trap] Pseudoflow traps
> - **Sign convention.** Here $b(v)$ is inflow minus outflow, so demands are positive; the note flips it in §F.4 and again in §F.5 (see there). Fix one convention per problem.
> - **Balances summing to zero is not enough**: Theorem F.2's cut condition must hold for every $T$.
> - **The residual balance of a vertex changes only at the ends of a push**, never in the middle of the path.

## F.4 Variations on a theme

Several variants of maximum flow reduce to two stages:
1. find a **feasible** flow $f$ in $G$ (as in §F.2–F.3);
2. find a **maximum** flow $f'$ in the residual graph $G_f$, which is an ordinary flow network;
3. return $f + f'$.

This works because, for every feasible flow $g$, the difference $g - f$ is an ordinary $(s,t)$-flow in $G_f$ (conserved wherever $f$ and $g$ meet the same constraint) with value $\lvert g\rvert - \lvert f\rvert$, and conversely $f$ plus a feasible flow of $G_f$ is feasible. Ford–Fulkerson itself fits the scheme, with $f = 0$, or with $f$ the flow after any number of augmentations.

### Maximum flows with non-zero balances

Now $G$ has a source $s$, a target $t$ and balances on the other vertices, and we want an $(s,t)$-flow — balances respected at every $v\ne s,t$ — of maximum value $\lvert f\rvert = \sum_w f(s\to w) - \sum_u f(u\to s)$. (The note writes this balance condition as outflow minus inflow, p. 6, the opposite of §F.1; the construction below does not depend on it.) For stage 1, set $b(s) = b(t) = 0$, add a vertex $z$ with $b(z) = -\sum_v b(v)$ — so that the balances sum to zero — and connect $z$ to $s$ and $t$ by uncapacitated edges; then feasible flows of the new network restrict to feasible $(s,t)$-flows of $G$ and vice versa. Stage 1 then costs $O(VE)$ (Orlin) or $O(VE^2)$ (shortest augmenting paths), and stage 2 is a standard maximum flow.

**The note adds only $t\to z$ and $z\to s$** (p. 6). Those edges force the flow on $z\to s$, which equals $\lvert f\rvert$, and the flow on $t\to z$, which equals the net flow into $t$, to be non-negative, so an $(s,t)$-flow whose value or net inflow at $t$ is negative cannot be extended — contrary to the note's claim that every one can. When *every* feasible $(s,t)$-flow is like that, the construction reports "infeasible" for a network that has a maximum flow. Adding the reverse edges $z\to t$ and $s\to z$, also uncapacitated, repairs it.

### Lower bounds on edges

Now each edge also has a **lower bound** $\ell(e)$, and a flow is feasible if $\ell(e)\le f(e)\le c(e)$. Lower bounds may even be negative, if a negative flow on $u\to v$ is read as a positive flow on $v\to u$. To make that precise the note extends every function **antisymmetrically** to reversed edges: $\psi(v\to u) = -\psi(u\to v)$, $\ell(v\to u) = -c(u\to v)$ and $c(v\to u) = -\ell(u\to v)$, so that $\ell\le\psi\le c$ holds on an edge exactly when it holds on its reversal. Residual bounds are then simply
$$
\ell_\psi(e) = \ell(e) - \psi(e),\qquad c_\psi(e) = c(e) - \psi(e)\quad\text{for every edge } e \text{ and its reversal},
$$
and $\ell_\psi(v\to u) = -c_\psi(u\to v)$, as antisymmetry demands. (The note's second cases, for a pair $u\to v$ whose reversal $v\to u$ is the edge, read $\psi(u\to v) - c(v\to u)$ and $\psi(u\to v) - \ell(v\to u)$, p. 7; the flow there must be $\psi(v\to u)$, the flow on the edge itself, or antisymmetry fails whenever $\psi\ne 0$. Its residual network also keeps the edges with $c_\psi\ge 0$; as everywhere else it should keep those with $c_\psi > 0$.)

> [!recipe] Recipe — maximum $(s,t)$-flow with lower bounds (note F)
> 1. Start with the pseudoflow $\psi(e) = \max\{\ell(e), 0\}$; it meets every positive lower bound (it needs $c\ge 0$ and $\ell\le c$).
> 2. In the residual network every residual lower bound $\ell - \psi$ is $\le 0$, and vertices may now have non-zero residual balances.
> 3. Replace each edge $u\to v$ whose residual lower bound is negative by $u\to v$ with capacity $c_\psi(u\to v)$ plus an opposing edge $v\to u$ with capacity $-\ell_\psi(u\to v)$; to keep the network free of antiparallel edges, route them as paths $u\to x\to v$ and $v\to y\to u$ through new vertices.
> 4. Solve the resulting maximum flow with balances by the two stages above, and add $\psi$ back: $O(VE)$ time in all.

## F.5 Push-relabel

(♥ in the note, which marks this section for "figures and an editing sweep".) Push-relabel, or preflow-push, algorithms are due to Andrew Goldberg and Robert Tarjan (1986) and do not augment along paths at all. They keep a **preflow**: a feasible pseudoflow in which every vertex except $s$ has non-negative **excess** $\textit{ex}(v) = \sum_u\psi(u\to v) - \sum_w\psi(v\to w)$. (The note calls the excess the residual balance $b_\psi$, the negative of §F.3's $b_\psi$ when $b = 0$.) A vertex $v\ne t$ with positive excess is **active**; $\psi$ is a flow exactly when no vertex is active. Each vertex also has an integer **height** $\mathit{ht}(v)$, and an edge $u\to v$ with $\mathit{ht}(u) > \mathit{ht}(v)$ is **downward**.

```text
PushRelabel(V, E, c, s, t):
    ψ(s→v) ← c(s→v) for every edge s→v;  ψ(e) ← 0 for every other edge
    ht(s) ← |V|;  ht(v) ← 0 for every vertex v ≠ s
    while some vertex u is active
        if G_ψ has a downward edge u→v
            push min{ex(u), c_ψ(u→v)} units along u→v       ⟨⟨push⟩⟩
        else
            ht(u) ← ht(u) + 1                             ⟨⟨relabel⟩⟩
    return ψ
```

(The note describes the loop in words; it lets the active vertex and the downward edge be arbitrary.) A push is **saturating** if it uses up the residual edge — $\psi(u\to v) = c(u\to v)$ for a forward edge, or the cancelled flow drops to 0 for a backward one — and **non-saturating** otherwise; a non-saturating push moves all of $u$'s excess. The heights and $\psi$ are **compatible** if $\mathit{ht}(u)\le\mathit{ht}(v) + 1$ for every residual edge $u\to v$: a residual edge never drops by more than one level.

> [!key] Lemmas F.3–F.5 (correctness)
> - **F.3.** After every step, the heights and $\psi$ are compatible.
> - **F.4.** After every step, $G_\psi$ has no path from $s$ to $t$.
> - **F.5.** If the algorithm terminates, it returns a maximum $(s,t)$-flow.

> [!proof]- Proofs (click to expand)
> **F.3**, by induction. Initially every residual edge enters $s$ or joins two vertices of height 0. A push along $u\to v$ requires $\mathit{ht}(v) < \mathit{ht}(u)$, so the reverse edge $v\to u$ it may create satisfies $\mathit{ht}(v)\le\mathit{ht}(u) + 1$. A relabel of $u$ happens only when $\mathit{ht}(u)\le\mathit{ht}(w)$ for every residual edge $u\to w$, so afterwards $\mathit{ht}(u)\le\mathit{ht}(w) + 1$; edges into $u$ only become easier.
>
> **F.4.** A simple residual path $s = v_0\to v_1\to\cdots\to v_k = t$ has $k\le\lvert V\rvert - 1$, and compatibility gives $\mathit{ht}(v_i)\ge\mathit{ht}(v_{i-1}) - 1$. The heights of $s$ and $t$ never change ($s$ and $t$ are never active), so $0 = \mathit{ht}(t)\ge\lvert V\rvert - k > 0$, a contradiction.
>
> **F.5.** At termination no vertex other than $s, t$ has excess, so $\psi$ is a flow (the note says "non-zero residual balance" for zero, p. 9), and by F.4 its residual graph has no $s$–$t$ path: by Chapter 10 it is a maximum flow. $\square$

> [!key] Lemmas F.6–F.8 (running time)
> - **F.6.** Every height stays below $2\lvert V\rvert$, so each vertex is relabeled fewer than $2\lvert V\rvert$ times: $O(V^2)$ relabels.
> - **F.7.** Each residual edge gets at most $\lvert V\rvert$ saturating pushes: $O(VE)$ saturating pushes.
> - **F.8.** There are $O(V^2E)$ non-saturating pushes.
>
> With adjacency lists, a push takes $O(1)$ time and a relabel time proportional to the degree, so push-relabel runs in **$O(V^2E)$** time — and in particular it terminates.

> [!proof]- Proofs (click to expand)
> **F.6.** The excess of an active vertex $v$ came from $s$, so following that flow backward gives a residual path from $v$ to $s$, of at most $\lvert V\rvert - 1$ edges; compatibility gives $\mathit{ht}(v)\le\mathit{ht}(s) + \lvert V\rvert - 1 < 2\lvert V\rvert$. Heights change only by relabels of active vertices, upward.
>
> **F.7.** A push along $u\to v$ needs $\mathit{ht}(u) = \mathit{ht}(v) + 1$ (downward and compatible). After a saturating push, $u\to v$ returns only after a push along $v\to u$, which needs $\mathit{ht}(v) = \mathit{ht}(u) + 1$: $v$ must rise by 2, and before the next push along $u\to v$, $u$ must rise by 2 as well. Since $v$ rises fewer than $2\lvert V\rvert$ times, there are at most $\lvert V\rvert$ saturating pushes along $u\to v$.
>
> **F.8.** Let $\Phi$ be the sum of the heights of the active vertices: $\Phi\ge 0$, $\Phi = 0$ at the start (active vertices have height 0) and at the end. A relabel raises $\Phi$ by 1, a saturating push by at most $\mathit{ht}(v) < 2\lvert V\rvert$ (it may activate $v$); in total $O(V^2) + O(VE)\cdot 2\lvert V\rvert = O(V^2E)$. A non-saturating push along $u\to v$ deactivates $u$ and at most activates $v$, lowering $\Phi$ by at least $\mathit{ht}(u) - \mathit{ht}(v) = 1$. The decreases cannot exceed the increases (the note says they are equal, but saturating pushes can also lower $\Phi$), so there are $O(V^2E)$ non-saturating pushes. $\square$

**Choosing the active vertex.** **FIFO:** keep the active vertices in a queue and work on the front vertex until it is inactive: $O(V^3)$ non-saturating pushes and $O(V^3)$ time. **Highest label:** always work on an active vertex of maximum height: $O(V^2\sqrt E)$. With dynamic trees, pushing along many edges at once, $O(VE\log(V^2/E))$, among the fastest bounds before Orlin's $O(VE)$ — though in practice the simple one-edge-at-a-time version is usually faster.

> [!example] Example — two small runs (FIFO)
> On the network $s\to a$ (3), $s\to b$ (2), $a\to b$ (1), $a\to t$ (2), $b\to t$ (3): saturating $s$'s edges gives $\textit{ex}(a) = 3$, $\textit{ex}(b) = 2$, and $\mathit{ht}(s) = 4$. Vertex $a$ has no downward edge, is relabeled to height 1, and pushes 1 unit to $b$ and 2 to $t$; then $b$ is relabeled to 1 and pushes its 3 units to $t$. Two relabels and three (saturating) pushes give the maximum flow 5.
>
> On $s\to a$ (3), $a\to t$ (1), with $\mathit{ht}(s) = 3$: $a$ is relabeled to 1 and pushes 1 unit to $t$; its other 2 units can only go back to $s$, which requires $\mathit{ht}(a) > \mathit{ht}(s)$, so $a$ is relabeled to 2, 3 and 4 and then pushes 2 units back along the residual edge $a\to s$ (a non-saturating push). The flow of value 1 is maximum. Excess that cannot reach $t$ climbs above height $\lvert V\rvert$ and drains back to the source.

## Summary

- Balances: $b(v)$ = inflow − outflow, positive = demand, negative = supply; they must sum to zero. A feasible flow respects capacities and every balance.
- Lemma F.1: feasible flow ⇔ saturating flow in $G'$ (source to supplies, demands to target) — one maximum flow, $O(VE)$. Theorem F.2: otherwise there is a cut with $\lVert S,T\rVert < b(T)$.
- Pseudoflows: any edge function; residual balance $b_\psi = b - (\text{net inflow})$. FeasibleFlow pushes $\min\{\text{excess}, \text{deficit}, \text{residual capacity}\}$ along excess-to-deficit paths; at most $\sum\lvert b\rvert/2$ iterations with integers, $O(VE^2)$ with globally shortest paths. If an excess vertex reaches no deficit, its reachable set $S$ is an infeasible cut.
- Two stages — any feasible flow, then a maximum flow in its residual graph — solve maximum flow with balances (vertex $z$ joined to $s$ and $t$ in both directions) and with lower bounds (start from $\max\{\ell, 0\}$; negative residual lower bounds become opposing edges), $O(VE)$.
- Push-relabel keeps a preflow and heights ($\mathit{ht}(s) = V$), pushes excess along downward residual edges, relabels by $+1$; compatibility ⇒ no residual $s$–$t$ path ⇒ maximum flow at the end; $O(V^2)$ relabels, $O(VE)$ saturating and $O(V^2E)$ non-saturating pushes: $O(V^2E)$ time; FIFO $O(V^3)$, highest label $O(V^2\sqrt E)$.
- The note ends with 5 exercises (path covers that may share vertices, decomposition of flows with balances, maximum preflow to maximum flow, edge covers with degree bounds, bounds on vertex inflow and balance).
