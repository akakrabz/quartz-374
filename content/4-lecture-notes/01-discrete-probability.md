> [!abstract] In one breath
> A **discrete probability space** is a countable sample space $\Omega$ with non-negative weights that sum to 1; an **event** is a subset of $\Omega$, and a **random variable** is a function on $\Omega$ — neither random nor a variable. Probabilities obey the union bound, inclusion–exclusion and Bayes' rule, and the **expectation** $\operatorname{E}[X] = \sum_x x\Pr[X = x]$ is **linear even for dependent variables**, which is the single most useful fact in the analysis of randomized algorithms. The note's main technique, used throughout the later notes, is to write a complicated count as a **sum of indicator variables**, so that its expectation is a sum of probabilities. Applications: von Neumann's trick turns a coin of unknown bias into a fair coin in $1/(pq)$ expected flips; collecting all $n$ Pokémon cards (the coupon collector) takes $nH_n = \Theta(n\log n)$ purchases on average; and a uniformly random permutation can be generated in $O(n)$ time by the in-place shuffle usually (mis)attributed to Fisher and Yates. The last section, on properties of random permutations, is an unfinished sketch.

## 1.1 Discrete probability spaces

The first two sections are a reference summary rather than an introduction.

> [!definition] Definition — discrete probability space
> A **discrete probability space** $(\Omega,\Pr)$ consists of a non-empty countable set $\Omega$, the **sample space**, and a **probability mass function** $\Pr\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$ with
> $$
> \Pr[\omega]\ge 0 \text{ for every } \omega\in\Omega \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\Pr[\omega] = 1,
> $$
> which forces $\Pr[\omega]\le 1$. Examples: a **fair coin**, $\Omega = \{\textsf{heads},\textsf{tails}\}$ with probability $1/2$ each; a **fair die**, $\Pr[\omega] = 1/6$ on $\{1,\ldots,6\}$; a **loaded die** with $\Pr[\omega] = \omega/21$ (so $\Pr[4] = 4/21$); and a rock-paper-scissors player who always plays rock, $\Pr[\textsf{rock}] = 1$.

Other countable sample spaces: the 52 cards of a deck, its $52!$ orderings, $\mathbb{N}$, $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, all finite bit strings, rooted trees or graphs, all finite execution traces of an algorithm. Uncountable spaces (a uniform point in an interval) need measure theory and are avoided. In practice $\Omega$ is left implicit: it can be taken to be the set of all tuples of values of the random quantities under discussion.

### 1.1.1 Events and probability

> [!definition] Definition — event, probability of an event
> An **event** is a subset $A\subseteq\Omega$; the elements of $\Omega$ are **sample points**, elementary events or atoms. Think of $\omega$ as a possible outcome of an experiment and of $A$ as a condition that some outcomes satisfy. The probability of an event is the total weight of its atoms:
> $$
> \Pr[A] = \sum_{\omega\in A}\Pr[\omega],
> $$
> so $\Pr[\varnothing] = 0$ and $\Pr[\Omega] = 1$; this extends $\Pr$ from atoms to a function $2^\Omega\to[0,1]$.

The running example: roll a red and a blue fair die. Then $\Omega = \{1,\ldots,6\}^2$ and every pair has probability $1/36$, so the probability of an event is its number of outcomes over 36.

### 1.1.2 Combining events

Events are sets, but because they are conditions they are usually combined with logical notation: $A\wedge B$ (and, intersection), $A\vee B$ (or, union), $\neg A$ (not, complement), $A\oplus B$ (exactly one), and $A\Rightarrow B$, which is shorthand for $\neg A\vee B$ and says nothing about causality. With the two dice:

| event | probability |
|---|---|
| two 5s $=$ red 5 $\wedge$ blue 5 | $1/36$ |
| at least one 5 $=$ red 5 $\vee$ blue 5 | $11/36$ |
| at most one 5 $= \neg$(two 5s) | $1 - 1/36 = 35/36$ |
| no 5s $= \neg$(at least one 5) | $1 - 11/36 = 25/36$ |
| exactly one 5 $=$ red 5 $\oplus$ blue 5 | $10/36 = 5/18$ |
| blue 5 $\Rightarrow$ red 5 $= \neg$(blue 5) $\vee$ red 5 | $1 - 5/36 = 31/36$ |

> [!definition] Definition — conditional probability
> For events $A$ and $B$ with $\Pr[B] > 0$, the **conditional probability of $A$ given $B$** is
> $$
> \Pr[A\mid B] = \frac{\Pr[A\wedge B]}{\Pr[B]}.
> $$
> It is the probability of $A$ in the smaller space $B$, with the weights rescaled to sum to 1.

| conditional event | probability | why |
|---|---|---|
| blue 5 $\mid$ red 5 | $1/6$ | $(1/36)/(6/36)$ |
| at least one 5 $\mid$ at most one 5 | $2/7$ | exactly one 5: $(10/36)/(35/36)$ |
| at most one 5 $\mid$ at least one 5 | $10/11$ | $(10/36)/(11/36)$ |
| red 5 $\mid$ at least one 5 | $6/11$ | $(6/36)/(11/36)$ |
| red 5 $\mid$ at most one 5 | $1/7$ | $(5/36)/(35/36)$ |
| blue 5 $\mid$ (blue 5 $\Rightarrow$ red 5) | $1/31$ | only $(5,5)$: $(1/36)/(31/36)$ |
| (blue 5 $\Rightarrow$ red 5) $\mid$ red 5 | $1$ | the implication holds whenever red is 5 |
| (blue 5 $\Rightarrow$ red 5) $\mid$ (red 5 $\Rightarrow$ blue 5) | $26/31$ | both hold iff both or neither are 5: $26/36$ |

> [!definition] Definition — disjoint and independent events
> $A$ and $B$ are **disjoint** if $A\cap B = \varnothing$, and **independent** if $\Pr[A\wedge B] = \Pr[A]\cdot\Pr[B]$. A family $\{A_i\mid i\in I\}$ is **mutually** (fully) **independent** if $\Pr\big[\bigwedge_{i\in J}A_i\big] = \prod_{i\in J}\Pr[A_i]$ for every finite $J\subseteq I$; it is **$k$-wise independent** if every $k$ of its events are mutually independent, and **pairwise independent** for $k = 2$.

With two dice, "red 5" and "blue 5" are independent ($1/36 = 1/6\cdot 1/6$), while "red 5" and "total 5" are disjoint and therefore *not* independent. The events "red 5", "blue 5" and "total 7" are pairwise independent — each pair meets in exactly one outcome, $1/36 = 1/6\cdot1/6$ — but not mutually independent: all three together are impossible, and $0\ne 1/216$. (The note's example of three pairwise disjoint events, "red 5", "blue 5" and "total 5", does not work: red 5 and blue 5 both contain $(5,5)$. "Red 5", "red 6" and "total 5" are pairwise disjoint.)

> [!trap] Disjoint is not independent
> - **Disjoint events of positive probability are never independent**: if one happens, the other cannot. Independence is a statement about probabilities, disjointness about sets.
> - **Probability 0 does not mean disjoint**: for the always-rock player, "plays paper" and "does not play rock" have $\Pr[\text{both}] = 0$, yet both contain the atom $\textsf{paper}$.
> - **Pairwise independence does not imply mutual independence** (red 5, blue 5, total 7).

### 1.1.3 Identities and inequalities

Each of these follows from the corresponding fact about sets, for arbitrary events $A_1,\ldots,A_n$:

- **Union bound.** $\Pr\big[\bigvee_{i=1}^n A_i\big]\le\sum_{i=1}^n\Pr[A_i]$: the right side counts each atom of the union once for every event that contains it, the left side once.
- **Disjoint union.** If the $A_i$ are pairwise disjoint, the union bound is an equality.
- **Inclusion–exclusion.** $\Pr[A\vee B] = \Pr[A] + \Pr[B] - \Pr[A\wedge B]$. Proof: $A\vee B$ is the disjoint union of $A\wedge B$, $A\wedge\neg B$ and $\neg A\wedge B$; adding $\Pr[A\wedge B]$ to both sides and regrouping gives $\big(\Pr[A\wedge B] + \Pr[A\wedge\neg B]\big) + \big(\Pr[\neg A\wedge B] + \Pr[A\wedge B]\big) = \Pr[A] + \Pr[B]$. In general,
  $$
  \Pr\Big[\bigvee_{i=1}^n A_i\Big] = \sum_{\varnothing\ne I\subseteq[1..n]}(-1)^{\lvert I\rvert+1}\Pr\Big[\bigwedge_{i\in I}A_i\Big],
  $$
  which the note writes as $1 - \sum_{I\subseteq[1..n]}(-1)^{\lvert I\rvert}\Pr[\bigwedge_{i\in I}A_i]$, the term $I = \varnothing$ being $\Pr[\Omega] = 1$.
- **Independent union.** If $A$ and $B$ are independent, $\Pr[A\vee B] = \Pr[A] + \Pr[B] - \Pr[A]\Pr[B] = 1 - (1 - \Pr[A])(1 - \Pr[B])$; for mutually independent events, $\Pr\big[\bigvee_i A_i\big] = 1 - \prod_i\big(1 - \Pr[A_i]\big)$ — the complement of "none of them happens".
- **Bayes' theorem.** If $\Pr[A], \Pr[B] > 0$, the definition of conditional probability, applied both ways, gives
  $$
  \Pr[A\mid B]\cdot\Pr[B] = \Pr[A\wedge B] = \Pr[B\mid A]\cdot\Pr[A].
  $$
  (The note's display $\Pr[A\mid B]/\Pr[A] = \Pr[A\wedge B] = \Pr[B\mid A]/\Pr[B]$ has the wrong middle term; it should be $\Pr[A\wedge B]/(\Pr[A]\Pr[B])$. With $A$ = red 5 and $B$ = total 6, the outer terms are $6/5$ and $\Pr[A\wedge B] = 1/36$. The conclusion, the display above, is right.)

## 1.2 Random variables

> [!definition] Definition — random variable
> A **random variable** $X$ is a function from the sample space $\Omega$ to some value set. A function $\Omega\to\mathbb{Z}$ is an **integer random variable** (random integer), $\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$ a **real random variable**, and $\Omega\to\{0,1\}$ an **indicator random variable** (random bit). Values need not be numbers: a random graph is a function from $\Omega$ to graphs, a random point in the plane a function to $\mathbb{R}^2$.

The note's slogan: **a random variable is not random, nor is it a variable** — all the randomness is in the choice of $\omega$; $X$ is a fixed function. Upper-case letters are almost universal. Every random bit is a random integer and every random integer a random real.

### 1.2.1 It is a fungus

Nevertheless random variables are manipulated as if they were variables standing for unknown values. Functions apply by composition: for $X\colon\Omega\to V$ and $f\colon V\to V'$, $f(X) = f\circ X$ is a random variable, so $X + \alpha$, $\alpha X$ and $f(X, X')$, defined by $f(X,X')(\omega) = f(X(\omega), X'(\omega))$, are random variables too. A predicate on values is identified with an event:
$$
\Pr[X = x] = \Pr\big[\{\omega\mid X(\omega) = x\}\big],\qquad \Pr[X = Y] = \Pr\big[\{\omega\mid X(\omega) = Y(\omega)\}\big],
$$
and likewise $\Pr[X\le x]$ or $\Pr[X\text{ is prime}]$; $\textsf{True}$ and $\textsf{False}$ are identified with the events $\Omega$ and $\varnothing$.

### 1.2.2 Expectation

> [!definition] Definition — expectation, conditional expectation
> The **expectation** of a real random variable $X$ is
> $$
> \operatorname{E}[X] = \sum_x x\cdot\Pr[X = x],
> $$
> summed over the countably many values $x$ with $\Pr[X = x]\ne 0$. For an event $A$ with $\Pr[A] > 0$, the **conditional expectation** is
> $$
> \operatorname{E}[X\mid A] = \sum_x x\cdot\Pr[X = x\mid A] = \sum_x\frac{x\cdot\Pr[X = x\wedge A]}{\Pr[A]}.
> $$

On an infinite sample space the series need not converge. The note says the sum is "always well-defined" because the set of values of positive probability is countable (it calls that set a subset of $\Omega$; it is a set of values, countable because $\Omega$ is). Countable is not enough: if $\Pr[X = 2^k] = 2^{-k}$ for $k\ge 1$, every term $2^k\cdot 2^{-k}$ is 1 and $\operatorname{E}[X] = \infty$. For the running times in these notes $X\ge 0$ and an infinite expectation is a legitimate answer; in general $\operatorname{E}[X]$ is defined when the series $\sum_x\lvert x\rvert\Pr[X = x]$ converges.

**Tail formula.** For an integer random variable,
$$
\operatorname{E}[X] = \sum_{x\ge 1}\Pr[X\ge x] - \sum_{x\le -1}\Pr[X\le x],\qquad\text{so } \operatorname{E}[X] = \sum_{x\ge1}\Pr[X\ge x] \text{ when } X\ge 0.
$$
(Each positive value $v$ is counted once in each of $\Pr[X\ge 1],\ldots,\Pr[X\ge v]$.) The note starts both sums at $x = 0$, which adds $\Pr[X\ge0] - \Pr[X\le0] = \Pr[X>0] - \Pr[X<0]$: for a fair die it gives $9/2$ instead of $7/2$.

**Total expectation.** If $0<\Pr[A]<1$, then $\operatorname{E}[X] = \operatorname{E}[X\mid A]\Pr[A] + \operatorname{E}[X\mid\neg A]\Pr[\neg A]$; more generally $\operatorname{E}[X] = \sum_y\operatorname{E}[X\mid Y = y]\Pr[Y = y]$ for any random variable $Y$. This is how the expected running time of an algorithm that may "start over" is computed (§1.4).

**Independence.** Random variables $X$ and $Y$ are **independent** if the events $X = x$ and $Y = y$ are independent for all $x$ and $y$ (and mutually, $k$-wise or pairwise independent families are defined as for events). Independent real random variables satisfy $\operatorname{E}[XY] = \operatorname{E}[X]\operatorname{E}[Y]$, and mutually independent ones $\operatorname{E}\big[\prod_i X_i\big] = \prod_i\operatorname{E}[X_i]$. The converse fails: for $X$ uniform on $\{-1,0,1\}$ and $Y = X^2$, $\operatorname{E}[XY] = \operatorname{E}[X^3] = 0 = \operatorname{E}[X]\operatorname{E}[Y]$, but $\Pr[X = 0\wedge Y = 1] = 0\ne\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac23$.

> [!key] Linearity of expectation
> For any real random variables $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ and real constants $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n$,
> $$
> \operatorname{E}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_iX_i\Big] = \sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_i\operatorname{E}[X_i].
> $$
> **No independence is required.** (Proof: write both sides as sums over $\omega\in\Omega$ and exchange the order of summation.)

### 1.2.3 Examples

Two fair dice again, with $R$ the red value, $B$ the blue value, $S = R + B$, and $R' = 7 - R$, the bottom face of the red die (the note draws $R$ upside down). $R$ and $B$ are independent, and so are $R'$ and $B$; no other pair among $R, B, S, R'$ is.

| expression | value | reason |

## 1.3 Common probability distributions

The **distribution** of a random variable $X\colon\Omega\to V$ is the function $P\colon V\to[0,1]$ with $P(x) = \Pr[X = x] = \sum\{\Pr[\omega]\mid X(\omega) = x\}$; its **support** is the set of values of positive probability. The note's Figure 1.1, with $q = 1-p$:

| name | models | parameters | support | $\Pr[X = x]$ | $\operatorname{E}[X]$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| trivial | always the same result | — | $\{a\}$ | $1$ | $a$ |
| uniform | fair die roll | — | finite $S\ne\varnothing$ | $1/\lvert S\rvert$ | $\big(\sum S\big)/\lvert S\rvert$ |
| Bernoulli | one biased coin flip | $0\le p\le 1$ | $\{0,1\}$ | $p$ if $x = 1$, $q$ if $x = 0$ | $p$ |
| binomial | number of successes in $n$ flips | $0\le p\le1$, $n\ge 0$ | $[0..n]$ | $\binom{n}{x}p^xq^{n-x}$ | $np$ |
| geometric | failures before the first success | $0<p\le 1$ | $\mathbb{N}$ | $q^xp$ | $q/p$ |
| negative binomial | failures before the $n$th success | $0<p\le1$, $n\ge 0$ | $\mathbb{N}$ | $\binom{n+x-1}{x}q^xp^n$ | $nq/p$ |

- **Trivial**: a "random" experiment with one outcome — still a probability distribution.
- **Uniform**: equal probability on a finite set; uniform on $[1..n]$ has mean $(n+1)/2$. It models idealized coins, dice and lotteries, and is what many people wrongly take "random" to mean.
- **Bernoulli**: a **Bernoulli trial** succeeds (1) with probability $p$ and fails (0) with probability $q$. **Every indicator variable is Bernoulli, and its expectation is its success probability:** $\operatorname{E}[X] = 0\cdot q + 1\cdot p = \Pr[X = 1]$. For $p = 1/2$ this is a fair coin; for $p\in\{0,1\}$ it is trivial.
- **Binomial**: the number of successes in $n$ independent Bernoulli($p$) trials; $\binom nx$ ways to choose which $x$ trials succeed. Its mean $np$ is linearity applied to the $n$ trials.
- **Geometric**: $X = x$ iff the first $x$ trials fail and trial $x+1$ succeeds, so $\Pr[X = x] = q^xp$ by independence.
- **Negative binomial**: $X = x$ iff trial $n + x$ is a success and exactly $x$ of the first $n + x - 1$ fail; it is a sum of $n$ independent geometric variables, and for $n = 1$ it *is* the geometric distribution.

> [!trap] Two geometric conventions
> The note's geometric variable counts the failures **before** the first success: mean $q/p = (1-p)/p$. The number of trials **up to and including** the first success is one more, with mean $1 + q/p = 1/p$. §1.4 and §1.5 use the second: a coin that comes up heads with probability $p$ needs $1/p$ flips on average to show heads.

## 1.4 Coin flips

A coin is **fair** if heads (1) and tails (0) each have probability exactly $1/2$, and **biased** otherwise. Real coins are close to fair when flipped high and allowed to bounce; bending a coin biases it. The question: given a biased coin, generate fair random bits.

### 1.4.1 Removing unknown bias

John von Neumann's trick (1951) works without knowing the bias: flip twice; if the flips differ, return the first; otherwise start over.

```text
VonNeumannCoin():
    x ← BiasedCoin()
    y ← BiasedCoin()
    if x ≠ y
        return x
    else
        return VonNeumannCoin()
```

There is no worst-case bound on its running time — in principle the coin keeps repeating itself forever — but it is a perfectly good algorithm in expectation. Assume (1) every flip shows 1 with the same unknown probability $p$, and 0 with $q = 1-p$, where $pq > 0$; and (2) all flips are mutually independent.

> [!key] Von Neumann's coin
> If $0<p<1$, VonNeumannCoin returns 1 with probability exactly $1/2$, and the expected number of biased flips is
> $$
> \operatorname{E}[T] = \frac{1}{pq}.
> $$

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> **Fairness.** By independence $\Pr[x = 1\wedge y = 0] = pq = \Pr[x = 0\wedge y = 1]$, so given $x\ne y$ each value is returned with probability $pq/2pq = 1/2$. The flips of later rounds are independent of earlier ones, so the same holds in every recursive call that returns.
>
> **Running time.** Condition on the first round: $\Pr[x\ne y] = 2pq$, and then $T = 2$; otherwise the experiment starts over after 2 flips, so $\operatorname{E}[T\mid x = y] = 2 + \operatorname{E}[T]$. By total expectation,
> $$
> \operatorname{E}[T] = 2\cdot 2pq + \big(2 + \operatorname{E}[T]\big)(1 - 2pq),
> $$
> whose solution is $\operatorname{E}[T] = 1/(pq)$. Alternatively: each round is a Bernoulli trial that succeeds with probability $2pq$; the number of failed rounds is geometric with mean $1/(2pq) - 1$, so there are $1/(2pq)$ rounds on average, of 2 flips each. $\square$

For $p = 1/3$ that is $9/2$ flips; even a fair coin costs 4 flips per output bit. (The note writes $T$ without an argument because the algorithm has no input.)

### 1.4.2 Removing known bias

If the bias is known, one can do better. For $p = 1/3$ (BiasedCoin(1/3) returns 1 with probability $1/3$), the outcomes 01 and 10 together have probability $4/9$, and so does 00 alone:

```text
FairCoin():
    x ← BiasedCoin(1/3)
    y ← BiasedCoin(1/3)
    if x ≠ y                 ⟨⟨probability 4/9⟩⟩
        return 0
    else if x = y = 0        ⟨⟨probability 4/9⟩⟩
        return 1
    else                     ⟨⟨x = y = 1: probability 1/9⟩⟩
        return FairCoin()
```

It returns 0 and 1 with equal probability, and $\operatorname{E}[T] = 2 + \tfrac19\operatorname{E}[T]$ gives $\operatorname{E}[T] = 9/4$, half of von Neumann's $9/2$. (The note's pseudocode tests $x = y = 1$ in the second branch and labels it $4/9$; with $\Pr[1] = 1/3$, that branch has probability $1/9$, and the printed code would return 1 with probability only $1/5$. The note's own text, $\Pr[x\ne y] = 4/9 = \Pr[x = y = 0]$, is right; the code above follows it.)

## 1.5 Pokémon collecting

The oversimplified model: each purchase is one card, chosen **independently and uniformly at random** from $n$ types; buy until you own every type. This is the **coupon collector's problem**, which the note traces back to de Moivre (1712). Two questions: how many types do you own after $n$ purchases, and how many purchases does a complete collection take?

### 1.5.1 After n trials

> [!recipe] The indicator method
> To find the expectation of a count $X$:
> 1. write $X = \sum_i X_i$, where $X_i$ is the **indicator** of the $i$th thing being counted;
> 2. by linearity, $\operatorname{E}[X] = \sum_i\operatorname{E}[X_i] = \sum_i\Pr[X_i = 1]$;
> 3. compute each probability separately — no independence among the $X_i$ is needed.

Let $X_i = [\text{you own type } i]$, so that $X = \sum_i X_i$ is the number of distinct types you own. You miss type $i$ only if all $n$ purchases avoid it, which has probability $(1 - 1/n)^n\approx 1/e$, so
$$
\operatorname{E}[X] = \sum_{i=1}^n\Pr[X_i = 1] = n\Big(1 - \big(1 - \tfrac1n\big)^n\Big) \approx \big(1 - \tfrac1e\big)n\approx 0.63212\,n.
$$
After $n$ cards you expect to own a little less than two thirds of the types. After $cn$ cards the fraction is $1 - (1-1/n)^{cn}\approx 1 - e^{-c}$: about 86% for $c = 2$, 95% for $c = 3$ and 98% for $c = 4$.

### 1.5.2 Gotta catch 'em all

Let $T(n)$ be the number of purchases until all $n$ types are owned. Split the process into $n$ **phases**: phase $i$ ends with the purchase that brings the $i$th distinct type, and $T_i(n)$ is its number of purchases, so $T(n) = \sum_i T_i(n)$. During phase $i$ you own $i - 1$ types, so each purchase is new with probability $p_i = (n - i + 1)/n$, independently: $T_i(n)$ counts the trials up to and including the first success, with mean $1/p_i$. By linearity,
$$
\operatorname{E}[T(n)] = \sum_{i=1}^n\frac{n}{n-i+1} = n\sum_{j=1}^n\frac1j = nH_n,
$$
where $H_n = \sum_{j=1}^n 1/j$ is the $n$th **harmonic number** ($H_0 = 0$, $H_n = H_{n-1} + 1/n$).

> [!key] Coupon collector and harmonic numbers
> Collecting all $n$ equally likely types takes $nH_n$ purchases in expectation, and comparing $\sum 1/j$ with $\int dx/x$ from above and below gives
> $$
> \ln(n+1)\le H_n\le\ln n + 1,
> $$
> so $\operatorname{E}[T(n)] = nH_n = \Theta(n\log n)$.

For the 150 original Pokémon that is $150\,H_{150}\approx 838.68$ cards; for the 9184 card types of 2013, $9184\,H_{9184}\approx 89{,}107.65$ — an underestimate in practice, because real cards are not equally common.

> [!trap] Indicator-method traps
> - **The indicators are dependent** (owning type 1 makes owning type 2 slightly less likely), and that does not matter for the expectation. It would matter for the variance or for "with high probability" statements ([[4-lecture-notes/04-tail-inequalities|note 4]]).
> - **The phases are not of equal length**: the last type alone takes $n$ purchases on average, half the types take only about $n\ln 2$ in total.
> - **Expectation of a count, not of a ratio or a maximum**: linearity gives $\operatorname{E}[\sum X_i]$, never $\operatorname{E}[\max X_i]$ or $\operatorname{E}[1/X]$.

## 1.6 Random permutations

To shuffle a deck of $n$ cards, every one of the $n!$ permutations should come out with probability $1/n!$. The gold standard is millennia old: **drawing lots** from an opaque container.

```text
DrawLots(L):
    n ← |L|
    for i ← 1 to n
        remove a random lot x from L
        R[i] ← x
    return R[1 .. n]
```

There are $n$ choices for the first lot, $n - 1$ for the second, and so on: $n!$ equally likely sequences of choices, each producing a different permutation, so every permutation has probability $1/n!$.

**Fisher and Yates (1938)** described it for a list of numbers: repeatedly pick an unchosen number at random, copy it to the output, strike it out. With a Boolean array and $\textsf{Random}(n)$, which returns a uniform integer in $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ in $O(1)$ time:

```text
FisherYates(L[1 .. n]):
    for i ← 1 to n
        Chosen[i] ← False
    for i ← n down to 1
        repeat
            r ← Random(n)
        until ¬Chosen[r]
        R[i] ← L[r]
        Chosen[r] ← True
    return R[1 .. n]
```

The repeat loop picks a uniform index among the unchosen ones, so this is DrawLots. But it is **the coupon collector in disguise**: each call to Random is a purchase and Chosen records which "types" are owned, so the expected number of calls is exactly $nH_n$ and the algorithm runs in $\Theta(n\log n)$ **expected** time. (Fisher and Yates later replaced it with an algorithm of C. R. Rao, finding their own "tiresome".)

**Durstenfeld (1961)** gave the in-place version, almost universally called "the Fisher–Yates shuffle" (or the Knuth shuffle):

```text
SelectionShuffle(A[1 .. n]):
    for i ← n down to 1
        swap A[i] ↔ A[Random(i)]
```

It runs in $O(n)$ time (worst case), and it is uniform by the same count: $n\cdot(n-1)\cdots 1 = n!$ equally likely sequences of Random results, each giving a different permutation. It *is* lot-casting: after each iteration the suffix $A[i..n]$ holds the lots drawn so far (the list $R$) and the prefix $A[1..i-1]$ the unchosen ones (the bag $L$). Unlike FisherYates it reorders the bag as it goes, which is harmless: only the set of unchosen elements matters.

Running the loop the other way also works:

```text
InsertionShuffle(A[1 .. n]):
    for i ← 1 to n
        swap A[i] ↔ A[Random(i)]
```

Again $1\cdot 2\cdots n = n!$ equally likely choice sequences give $n!$ different outputs. Alternatively, by induction, after iteration $i$ the prefix $A[1..i]$ is a uniformly shuffled copy of the first $i$ items; or: InsertionShuffle is SelectionShuffle run backward in time (putting lots back into the bag), and the inverse of a uniform permutation is uniform. The names are Erickson's: SelectionShuffle *selects* a random card for one end of the output, like selection sort; InsertionShuffle *inserts* the next card at a random place in the shuffled prefix, like insertion sort.

> [!trap] Shuffling traps
> - **"Each swap is random" is not enough.** A correct shuffle needs every permutation to arise from the same number of equally likely choice sequences; the counting argument above checks exactly that. The note's Exercise 10 shows that changing Random($i$) to Random($n$) in InsertionShuffle breaks uniformity.
> - **FisherYates' repeat loop is not $O(n)$**: the late draws mostly hit chosen indices, $nH_n$ draws in all.

## 1.7 Properties of random permutations

This section is an unfinished sketch in the note (three bullet points in a box marked as a placeholder). Its claims, for a uniformly random permutation:

- **Fixed positions.** For any fixed set of positions, the *set* of values that land there is a uniformly random subset of that size, and the *order* of those values within the positions is a uniformly random permutation, independent of the set.
- **Fixed values.** Symmetrically, for any fixed set of values, the set of positions they occupy is a uniformly random subset and their relative order is uniform, independently.
- **Example.** In a shuffled 52-card deck, the expected number of hearts among the first five face cards (J, Q, K) is $5/4$: by the second property the 12 face cards appear in uniformly random relative order, so each of the first five face cards is a heart with probability $3/12$, and linearity gives $5\cdot\tfrac14$.

### Two more indicator computations (not in the note)

Both are standard and use only the tools above. Let $\pi$ be a uniformly random permutation of $[1..n]$.

- **Fixed points.** $\Pr[\pi(i) = i] = 1/n$ for each $i$, so the expected number of fixed points is $n\cdot\tfrac1n = 1$, for every $n$ — although the indicators are dependent. (The probability of no fixed point tends to $1/e$; for $n = 8$ it is $14833/40320\approx 0.3679$.)
- **Cycles.** Give each element weight $1/(\text{length of its cycle})$; the weights on one cycle add up to 1, so the number of cycles is the sum of the weights. Element $i$ lies on a cycle of length $k$ with probability exactly $1/n$ for each $k = 1,\ldots,n$, so the expected number of cycles is $n\cdot\sum_{k=1}^n\tfrac1n\cdot\tfrac1k = H_n$.

## Summary

- A discrete probability space is a countable $\Omega$ with weights $\Pr[\omega]\ge 0$ summing to 1; events are subsets; $\Pr[A\mid B] = \Pr[A\wedge B]/\Pr[B]$; independent means $\Pr[A\wedge B] = \Pr[A]\Pr[B]$ (disjoint events of positive probability are dependent; pairwise ≠ mutual independence).
- Union bound $\Pr[\bigvee A_i]\le\sum\Pr[A_i]$; inclusion–exclusion; independent union $1 - \prod(1 - \Pr[A_i])$; Bayes $\Pr[A\mid B]\Pr[B] = \Pr[B\mid A]\Pr[A]$.
- A random variable is a function on $\Omega$. $\operatorname{E}[X] = \sum_x x\Pr[X = x]$; for $X\ge 0$ integer, $\operatorname{E}[X] = \sum_{x\ge1}\Pr[X\ge x]$; total expectation $\operatorname{E}[X] = \sum_y\operatorname{E}[X\mid Y = y]\Pr[Y = y]$; $\operatorname{E}[XY] = \operatorname{E}[X]\operatorname{E}[Y]$ for independent $X, Y$.
- **Linearity of expectation needs no independence.** Indicator method: $\operatorname{E}[\text{count}] = \sum\Pr[\text{event } i]$.
- Means: Bernoulli $p$, binomial $np$, geometric (failures before success) $(1-p)/p$, trials until the first success $1/p$, negative binomial $n(1-p)/p$.
- Von Neumann: flip twice, return the first if they differ — fair for any unknown $p$, $1/(pq)$ expected flips. With $p = 1/3$ known: $9/4$ flips.
- Coupon collector: $n(1 - (1-1/n)^n)\approx 0.632n$ types after $n$ purchases; all types after $nH_n = \Theta(n\log n)$ expected purchases; $\ln(n+1)\le H_n\le\ln n + 1$.
- Uniform shuffles: DrawLots; FisherYates with rejection, $nH_n$ expected Random calls; SelectionShuffle and InsertionShuffle, $O(n)$, uniform because $n!$ equally likely choice sequences give $n!$ distinct outputs.
- The note ends with 17 exercises (coin games, simulating dice and biased coins, shuffling algorithms such as QuickShuffle and MergeShuffle, card puzzles, random walks).
