> [!abstract] In one breath
> An algorithm's running time is an **upper bound** on the complexity of its problem; a **lower bound** must hold for **every** algorithm, so it needs a precise **model of computation**. In the **decision-tree** model an algorithm is a tree of queries about the input, and its worst-case time is the tree's depth. If the problem has $N$ possible outputs and each query has at most $k$ answers, the tree needs $N$ leaves and hence depth at least $\lceil\log_k N\rceil$ — the **information-theoretic bound**. It shows binary search is optimal ($n+1$ outputs) and that every **comparison sort** needs $\lceil\lg n!\rceil = \Omega(n\log n)$ comparisons (the $n!$ orders), matched by mergesort. The bound depends on the model: hashing and radix sort escape it with queries of many outcomes. When the output space is small — the maximum has only $n$ possible answers — counting leaves is too weak, and an **adversary argument** shows $n-1$ comparisons are needed.

> [!note] Where this fits
> It explains why the $O(n\log n)$ of mergesort ([[2-algorithms/01-recursion#14-mergesort|§1.4]]) cannot be beaten by any comparison sort, and why binary search's $O(\log n)$ is optimal. Fooling sets ([[concepts/fooling-sets]]) are the same idea for automata: a lower bound that holds for every machine. Continued in [[4-lecture-notes/13-adversary-arguments|note 13]] (adversary arguments). **Not assigned in CS 374 this semester** (CS 473 material at Illinois), but the $\Omega(n\log n)$ sorting bound is standard background. Concept page: [[concepts/comparison-lower-bounds]].

## 12.1 Huh? Whuzzat?

Let $T_A(X)$ be the running time of algorithm $A$ on input $X$. The worst-case running time of $A$ and the worst-case **complexity** of a problem $\Pi$ are
$$
T_A(n) = \max_{|X| = n} T_A(X),
\qquad
T_\Pi(n) = \min_{A\text{ solves }\Pi} T_A(n) = \min_{A\text{ solves }\Pi}\;\max_{|X| = n} T_A(X).
$$
Every algorithm for $\Pi$ gives an upper bound $T_\Pi(n)\le T_A(n)$. A lower bound $T_\Pi(n) = \Omega(f(n))$ is a statement about **all** algorithms: none runs in $o(f(n))$ time. That is much harder to prove than an upper bound, and "all algorithms" has no formal meaning until a model of computation is fixed.

## 12.2 Decision trees

> [!definition] Definition — decision tree
> A **decision tree** is a rooted tree in which every internal node is labeled by a **query** (any question about the input), the edges out of a node by the possible answers, and every leaf by an output. To run it, start at the root, follow the edge given by each answer, and output the label of the leaf reached. Its **running time** on an input is the number of queries on that root-to-leaf path; its worst-case running time is its **depth**. In a **$k$-ary** decision tree every query has at most $k$ answers ($k$ a constant); in a **binary** one, two.

Counting only queries ignores all other work, but it is a lower bound on the true running time, which is what a lower bound needs. Examples from the note:
- *Guess the animal*: yes/no questions ("Does it live in the water?", "Does it have scales?", …) with an animal at each leaf. Its tree has six leaves and depth 3; reaching "frog" takes 2 queries.
- *The dictionary problem*: a fixed array $A$ of $n$ numbers; given $x$, return its position in $A$, or "none". Sort $A$ and binary search: each node of the implicit search tree becomes the query "is $x < k$?", and each leaf the query "is $x = A[i]$?" with two leaf children, $i$ and "none".

## 12.3 Information theory

> [!key] The information-theoretic lower bound
> If a problem has $N$ different possible outputs, every decision tree that solves it has at least $N$ leaves (several leaves may give the same output). A $k$-ary tree of depth $d$ has at most $k^d$ leaves, so the depth — the worst-case number of queries — is at least
> $$
> \lceil\log_k N\rceil = \Omega(\log N).
> $$

For the dictionary problem there are $n + 1$ outputs (a position or "none"), so every decision tree needs depth $\lceil\log_k(n+1)\rceil = \Omega(\log n)$: binary search, $O(\log n)$, is **optimal** in this model. In the figure, $n = 8$ gives 9 outputs and $\lceil\lg 9\rceil = 4$ — exactly the depth of the tree.

## 12.4 But wait a second…

Hashing answers membership queries in $O(1)$ **expected** time. No contradiction: "what is the hash value of $x$?" has far more than a constant number of answers, so a hashing algorithm is not a $k$-ary decision tree for constant $k$. Without the degree limit the bound collapses entirely — the single query "for which index $i$, if any, is $A[i] = x$?" solves the dictionary problem in constant time, and the decision-tree model allows any question about the input. Choosing the model is the crux of every lower bound: too powerful, and the problem becomes trivial; too restrictive, and it may not be solvable at all.

## 12.5 Sorting

Decision trees cannot move data, so sorting is rephrased: *given distinct numbers $x_1, \ldots, x_n$, find the permutation $\pi$ with $x_{\pi(1)} < x_{\pi(2)} < \cdots < x_{\pi(n)}$.* There are $n!$ possible outputs, so every $k$-ary decision tree for sorting has depth at least $\lceil\log_k n!\rceil$. Stirling's approximation $n! = (n/e)^n\sqrt{2\pi n}\,(1 + \Theta(1/n)) > (n/e)^n$ makes this explicit:

> [!key] Sorting lower bound
> $$
> \log_k(n!) > \log_k\Bigl(\frac ne\Bigr)^n = n\log_k n - n\log_k e = \Omega(n\log n).
> $$
> Every $k$-ary decision tree that sorts $n$ distinct numbers has depth $\Omega(n\log n)$; with yes/no comparisons, at least $\lceil\lg n!\rceil$.

| $n$ | 4 | 5 | 8 | 12 | 16 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\lceil\lg n!\rceil$ (lower bound) | 5 | 7 | 16 | 29 | 45 |
| mergesort, worst case | 5 | 8 | 17 | 33 | 49 |

(Mergesort's worst case is $n\lceil\lg n\rceil - 2^{\lceil\lg n\rceil} + 1$ comparisons.) The bound is matched up to a constant factor by **mergesort** and **heapsort** — and by **quicksort** if its pivot is the median, found by linear-time selection ([[2-algorithms/01-recursion#18-linear-time-selection|§1.8]]); with an arbitrary pivot quicksort's worst case is $\Theta(n^2)$ ([[2-algorithms/01-recursion#15-quicksort|§1.5]]) and only its expected time is $O(n\log n)$. So the decision-tree complexity of sorting is $\Theta(n\log n)$.

**Comparison trees.** For these algorithms to count as optimal they must fit the model, and they do: they are **comparison trees**, where every query is "is $x_i$ smaller or larger than $x_j$?". Such an algorithm treats two inputs identically whenever all comparisons come out the same — only the order of the input values matters, not the values. Bubble sort, selection sort, insertion sort, shellsort, quicksort, heapsort and mergesort are all comparison trees; **radix sort and bucket sort are not** (they use the digits of the values as array indices — queries with many outcomes).

> [!example] Example — the bound is exact for small $n$
> An exhaustive search over all comparison trees shows that the minimum worst-case number of comparisons to sort $n$ elements is $0, 1, 3, 5, 7$ for $n = 1, \ldots, 5$ — exactly $\lceil\lg n!\rceil$. For $n = 5$ that is 7 comparisons, one fewer than mergesort's 8. (The bound is not always tight: it is known that 12 elements need 30 comparisons, not $\lceil\lg 12!\rceil = 29$; that fact is not checked here.)

## 12.6 Finding the maximum and adversaries

Rephrased for decision trees: *given distinct $x_1, \ldots, x_n$, find the index $m$ of the largest.* A linear scan uses $n - 1$ comparisons. But there are only $n$ outputs, so the information-theoretic bound is just $\lceil\lg n\rceil$ — and it is tight in the unrestricted model: $\lceil\lg n\rceil$ "unreasonable" yes/no questions such as "is bit $j$ of the index of the maximum 1?" find $m$. A non-trivial lower bound needs (1) a more realistic model, the **comparison tree** model (every query is "is $x_i > x_j$?"), and (2) an **adversary argument**.

> [!definition] Adversary argument
> An all-powerful **adversary** pretends to have chosen an input, and answers each query of the algorithm in whatever way makes the algorithm work hardest — always consistently with some input. If the algorithm stops too early, two inputs are consistent with all the answers but need different outputs; whatever the algorithm outputs, the adversary "reveals" the other one as the input it had all along.

> [!key] Maximum: $n - 1$ comparisons are necessary
> Every comparison tree that finds the maximum of $n$ distinct numbers has depth at least $n - 1$.

> [!proof]- Proof (the adversary, click to expand)
> The adversary answers every comparison as if $x_i = i$ for all $i$, and after each answer it **marks** the smaller of the two elements — the algorithm knows (or should know) it is not the maximum. Each comparison marks at most one new element, and $x_n$ is never marked. Suppose the algorithm stops after fewer than $n - 1$ comparisons. Then fewer than $n - 1$ elements are marked, so some $x_k$ with $k\ne n$ is unmarked: it has never lost a comparison. Raising $x_k$ to $n + 1$ is consistent with every answer given (every comparison involving $x_k$ had $x_k$ as the larger), but now $x_k$ is the maximum instead of $x_n$. The algorithm sees the same answers on both inputs, so it is wrong on one of them. $\square$

Two features make the argument work. No algorithm can tell this adversary from an honest user who fixed $x_i = i$ in advance. And the adversary assumes **nothing** about the order in which the algorithm compares: every comparison-based algorithm either makes $n - 1$ comparisons or errs on some input. (The note adds, without proof, that the $n - 1$ bound holds even for *algebraic decision trees*, whose queries compare two polynomials in the inputs.)

## Summary

- **Complexity of a problem** = min over algorithms of the worst-case time. Upper bounds come from one algorithm; lower bounds must cover all algorithms in a fixed **model of computation**.
- **Decision tree**: queries at internal nodes, outputs at leaves; worst-case time = depth. **$N$ outputs ⇒ at least $N$ leaves ⇒ depth $\ge\lceil\log_k N\rceil$.**
- Dictionary problem: $n + 1$ outputs, $\Omega(\log n)$ — binary search is optimal. Hashing is not a constant-degree decision tree.
- **Sorting**: $n!$ outputs ⇒ $\lceil\lg n!\rceil = n\lg n - O(n)$ comparisons; matched by mergesort and heapsort; applies to every comparison sort, not to radix or bucket sort.
- **Maximum**: only $\lceil\lg n\rceil$ from counting, but $n - 1$ comparisons by an **adversary** ($x_i = i$; every element but the maximum must lose a comparison).
- The note ends with 5 exercises (twenty questions, a multi-dictionary problem, $k$-sorting, nuts and bolts, the maximum without comparing elements).
