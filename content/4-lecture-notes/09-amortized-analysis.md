> [!abstract] In one breath
> Some operations are usually cheap and occasionally expensive; **amortized analysis** bounds the *total* cost of every sequence of operations, and charges each operation its share. The running example is incrementing a binary counter: one Increment can flip $\Theta(\log n)$ bits, but counting from 0 to $n$ flips exactly $2n - \#_1(n) < 2n$ bits, so the amortized cost is 2. The note derives this four ways — **summation** (add up the total), **taxation** (prepay a fixed tax that always covers the bill), **charging** (bill expensive steps to earlier cheap ones) and **potential** (amortized cost = actual cost + change of a potential function $\Phi\ge\Phi_0$). A counter that must also decrement uses a pair of bit strings $(P, N)$ with value $P - N$ and costs at most 2 amortized per operation; Gray codes flip one bit per step, and Ehrlich's loopless algorithm even finds that bit in $O(1)$ worst-case time. Amortized bounds involve no probability, say nothing about a single operation, and are not unique.

> The textbook quotes amortized bounds without developing them: the Fibonacci heaps behind the $O(E + V\log V)$ versions of Jarník's ([[2-algorithms/07-minimum-spanning-trees#74-jarníks-prims-algorithm|§7.4]]) and Dijkstra's algorithms ([[2-algorithms/08-shortest-paths#86-best-first-dijkstras-algorithm|§8.6]], [[concepts/dijkstras-algorithm]]), and the union-find structure in Kruskal's algorithm ([[2-algorithms/07-minimum-spanning-trees#75-kruskals-algorithm|§7.5]], [[concepts/union-find]]), whose "relabel the smaller set" bound is a summation argument. This note supplies the methods; [[4-lecture-notes/10-scapegoat-and-splay-trees|note 10]] applies them to binary search trees and [[4-lecture-notes/11-disjoint-sets|note 11]] to union-find. ## 9.1 Incrementing a binary counter

A common Homework 0 proof shows that every $n\ge 0$ is a sum of distinct powers of 2 by induction on $n$: take a set for $n-1$, add $2^0$, and while the multiset contains two copies of some $2^i$, replace them by one $2^{i+1}$ — the sum is unchanged and the multiset shrinks, so the process stops with a set. Read as an algorithm, the proof adds 1 to a binary counter. With the counter stored as an unbounded bit array, $B[i] = 1$ exactly when $2^i$ is in the sum:

```text
Increment(B[0 .. ∞]):
    i ← 0
    while B[i] = 1
        B[i] ← 0
        i ← i + 1
    B[i] ← 1
```

**Cost.** Increment clears the trailing 1s and sets the first 0: if the counter ends in $k$ ones it flips $k + 1$ bits, $\Theta(k)$ time. A number $n\ge 1$ has $\lfloor\lg n\rfloor + 1$ bits, so for counter values up to $n$ one Increment takes $\Theta(\log n)$ time in the worst case (from $2^k - 1$ to $2^k$ it flips all $k + 1$ bits).

## 9.2 Counting from 0 to n

Call Increment $n$ times on a zero counter. Multiplying the worst case by $n$ gives $O(n\log n)$, which is correct but loose: the total is $\Theta(n)$. The four methods below prove this; they are largely interchangeable, but each is the most natural one for some problems.

### 9.2.1 Summation

$B[0]$ flips at every Increment, $B[1]$ at every second one, and $B[i]$ at every $2^i$-th, so $n$ Increments flip $B[i]$ exactly $\lfloor n/2^i\rfloor$ times and the total is
$$
\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor\lg n\rfloor}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2^i}\right\rfloor \;<\; \sum_{i\ge 0}\frac{n}{2^i} = 2n.
$$
Exactly, it is $2n - \#_1(n)$, where $\#_1(n)$ is the number of 1s in the binary representation of $n$: counting to 16 flips 31 bits, counting to 1000 flips $2000 - 6 = 1994$. So the average Increment flips fewer than two bits.

> [!definition] Definition — amortized cost (summation, or aggregate, method)
> If $T(n)$ is the worst-case total time of a sequence of $n$ operations, the **amortized time** of each operation is $T(n)/n$. Here the average is over the operations of one sequence, in the worst case — **no probability is involved**, unlike the expected times of randomized algorithms. With "cost" as a synonym for running time: Increment has worst-case cost $\Theta(\log n)$ and amortized cost $O(1)$.

The word comes from accounting, where a large one-time expense is spread over many periods (as with the equal payments of a loan).

### 9.2.2 Taxation

Measure time in dollars, one per bit flip, and let a tax collector charge more than the actual cost of cheap steps so that the savings pay for expensive ones. This is also called the **accounting method**.

- **Tax on steps.** Charge \$2 whenever a bit changes from 0 to 1: one dollar pays for the change, the other stays on that bit and later pays for clearing it. Every Increment sets exactly one bit, so every Increment pays exactly \$2, and every 1 bit always holds the dollar it needs. *Method:* certain steps pay taxes, chosen so that the total tax always covers the total cost; the amortized cost of an operation is the tax it pays.
- **Tax by parts of the structure.** Let bit $B[i]$ charge $2^{-i}$ dollars at every Increment, whether it changes or not: $\sum_{i\ge 0}2^{-i} = 2$ dollars per Increment. $B[i]$ flips at every $2^i$-th Increment, exactly when it has collected one dollar. *Method:* parts of the data structure collect taxes at every operation; the amortized cost is the total collected per operation.

Designing the tax schedule is the analyst's job. Different schedules give different bounds; the tightest come from schedules that just barely stay out of debt.

### 9.2.3 Charging

Instead of where tax is collected, look at where it is spent: **charge** the cost of some steps to earlier steps. For the counter, charge every clearing of a bit (1 to 0) to the Increment that last set that bit (0 to 1). An Increment flipping $k$ bits sends $k - 1$ of them to earlier Increments; conversely, the bit it sets is charged at most once in the future, when it is next cleared. So each Increment pays for at most two flips: its own setting flip and at most one charge from the future. *Method:* the amortized cost of an operation is its actual cost, minus what it charges to earlier operations, plus what later operations charge to it. Charging overestimates the total, because a charge from the future may never arrive.

### 9.2.4 Potential

The most powerful and least mechanical method stores prepaid work not on particular steps or bits but in a **potential** — a function of the whole data structure, like potential energy.

> [!definition] Definition — the potential method
> Let $D_i$ be the data structure after $i$ operations, $\Phi_i = \Phi(D_i)$ its potential, and $c_i$ the actual cost of the $i$th operation. The **amortized cost** of the $i$th operation is its actual cost plus the increase in potential:
> $$
> a_i = c_i + \Phi_i - \Phi_{i-1}.
> $$
> The sum telescopes: $\sum_{i=1}^n a_i = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i + \Phi_n - \Phi_0$. The potential is **valid** if $\Phi_i\ge\Phi_0$ for all $i$; then for every sequence
> $$
> \sum_{i=1}^n c_i = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i - (\Phi_n - \Phi_0) \;\le\; \sum_{i=1}^n a_i,
> $$
> so bounds on the amortized costs bound the actual total. (In practice: $\Phi_0 = 0$ and $\Phi\ge 0$ always.)

**The counter.** Let $\Phi_i$ be the number of 1 bits after $i$ Increments; $\Phi_0 = 0\le\Phi_i$, so it is valid. Writing $s_i$ and $r_i$ for the numbers of bits the $i$th Increment sets and clears, $c_i = s_i + r_i$ and $\Phi_i - \Phi_{i-1} = s_i - r_i$, so
$$
a_i = 2s_i = 2,
$$
because every Increment sets exactly one bit. Here the potential is exactly the unspent tax of the first schedule; in general a potential need not be a sum of local credits, which is what makes the method more flexible than taxation or charging.

> [!recipe] Recipe — choosing a potential
> A good potential rises a little during every cheap operation and drops a lot during every expensive one, so that the drop pays for the expense. There is no general method for finding one: experiment with the structure, and expect most attempts to fail. Different potentials can give different amortized bounds for the same algorithm.

## 9.3 Incrementing and decrementing

A standard binary counter that also decrements is not efficient even amortized: alternating between $2^k - 1$ and $2^k$ flips $k + 1$ bits at every operation. Instead, represent an integer by a **pair $(P, N)$ of bit strings** with the invariant
$$
P\wedge N = 0 \qquad\text{(bitwise: for every } i\text{, at most one of } P[i], N[i] \text{ is 1),}
$$
whose value is $P - N$ ($P$ the "positive" part, $N$ the "negative" part, both read as binary numbers). Zero has only the representation $(0,0)$, but every other integer has infinitely many: $1 = (1, 0) = (10, 01) = (100, 011) = \cdots$. Increment adds 1 to $P$, and Decrement adds 1 to $N$, each modified so that the invariant survives — where the carry would land on a position whose bit is 1 in the other string, it clears that bit instead:

```text
Increment(P, N):                  Decrement(P, N):
    i ← 0                             i ← 0
    while P[i] = 1                    while N[i] = 1
        P[i] ← 0                          N[i] ← 0
        i ← i + 1                         i ← i + 1
    if N[i] = 1                       if P[i] = 1
        N[i] ← 0                          P[i] ← 0
    else                              else
        P[i] ← 1                          N[i] ← 1
```

> [!example] Example — the note's trace
> | step | $P$ | $N$ | $P - N$ |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | start | `10001` | `01100` | 5 |
> | Increment | `10010` | `01100` | 6 |
> | Increment | `10011` | `01100` | 7 |
> | Increment | `10000` | `01000` | 8 |
> | Decrement | `10000` | `01001` | 7 |
> | Decrement | `10000` | `01010` | 6 |
> | Increment | `10001` | `01010` | 7 |
>
> The third Increment carries through $P[0]$ and $P[1]$ and stops at position 2, where $N[2] = 1$: it clears $N[2]$ instead of setting $P[2]$, and $16 - 8 = 8$.

Start from $(0,0)$ and perform any sequence of $n$ Increments and Decrements. One operation can still take $\Theta(\log n)$ time (the 1024th Increment from $(0,0)$ flips 11 bits), and the summation method does not apply, because the sequence is unknown.

**Potential.** Let $\Phi$ be the total number of 1 bits in $P$ and $N$. Exactly as for the counter, $c_i$ = (bits set) + (bits cleared) and $\Delta\Phi$ = (bits set) − (bits cleared), so $a_i = 2\times$(bits set). Each operation sets at most one bit (the loop only clears; the last step clears or sets), so **every operation has amortized cost at most 2**, and $n$ operations from $(0,0)$ flip at most $2n$ bits.

**Taxation.** The note also lets every bit $P[i]$ and $N[i]$ collect $2^{-i}$ dollars per operation, $\sum_{i\ge 0}2\cdot 2^{-i} = 4$ dollars in all, justified by the fact that once $P[i]$ or $N[i]$ is set, at least $2^i$ operations pass before it is cleared. That pays for the clearing flips; for each bit to pay for *all* its own flips, one needs the stronger fact below, which also holds — so the amortized bound 4 is right, though weaker than the potential's 2.

> [!key] Lemma — flips of one position are far apart
> Starting from $(0,0)$, any two changes of the same bit $P[i]$ (or $N[i]$) are at least $2^i$ operations apart, and its first change happens at operation $2^i$ or later.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Let $L$ be the value of the low part, $(P \bmod 2^i) - (N\bmod 2^i)$; by the invariant, $-(2^i - 1)\le L\le 2^i - 1$. An operation that stops below position $i$ changes $L$ by exactly $\pm 1$ and leaves positions $\ge i$ alone. An Increment reaches position $i$ only if $P[0..i-1]$ are all 1, that is $L = 2^i - 1$, and then leaves $P[0..i-1] = 0$ and $N[0..i-1] = 0$, so $L = 0$; symmetrically, a Decrement reaches position $i$ only from $L = -(2^i - 1)$ and leaves $L = 0$. Initially $L = 0$. So between two operations that reach position $i$ — and before the first — $L$ must walk from 0 to $\pm(2^i - 1)$ in steps of $\pm 1$, which takes at least $2^i - 1$ operations; with the operation that reaches position $i$, that is at least $2^i$. A bit at position $i$ changes only in an operation that reaches position $i$. $\square$

## 9.4 Gray codes

(★ in the note: optional.) A **Gray code** lists the integers as bit strings so that consecutive numbers differ in exactly one bit. The 4-bit **binary reflected** Gray code of $0, 1, \ldots, 15$ is
`0000, 0001, 0011, 0010, 0110, 0111, 0101, 0100, 1100, 1101, 1111, 1110, 1010, 1011, 1001, 1000`
— the code of $k$ is $k \oplus \lfloor k/2\rfloor$ (bitwise exclusive or). To increment, **flip the bit that a binary counter would set from 0 to 1**. In bit flips this is perfect — one per increment or decrement — but finding that bit naively (keep the binary count alongside, or scan the code for its lowest 1) takes $\Theta(\log n)$ time in the worst case — $O(1)$ amortized for increments alone, but $\Theta(\log n)$ per operation when increments and decrements alternate — exactly like the binary counter.

**Ehrlich's algorithm** (Gideon Ehrlich, 1973) finds the bit in $O(1)$ **worst-case** time per increment — a *loopless* algorithm. Besides the code $G[0..n-1]$ it keeps a **focus array** $F[0..n]$:

```text
EhrlichGrayInit(n):                 EhrlichGrayIncrement(n):
    for i ← 0 to n − 1                  j ← F[0]
        G[i] ← 0                        F[0] ← 0
    for i ← 0 to n                      if j = n
        F[i] ← i                            G[n − 1] ← 1 − G[n − 1]
                                        else
                                            G[j] ← 1 − G[j]
                                            F[j] ← F[j + 1]
                                            F[j + 1] ← j + 1
```

Each increment runs a constant number of steps, with no loop. After the $(2^n - 1)$st increment the code is $10\cdots0$ and $F[0] = n$; the next increment takes the branch $j = n$, flips $G[n-1]$ back to 0 and restores the initial state, so the counter cycles through all $2^n$ codes.

**Why it works.** Let $B$ be the ordinary binary representation of the number of increments so far. The focus array satisfies:

> [!key] Invariant of the focus array (corrected)
> If $B[j] = 1$ and $B[j-1] = 0$ (with $B[-1] = 0$), then $F[j]$ is the smallest $k > j$ with $B[k] = 0$; otherwise $F[j] = j$. In particular $F[0]$ is always the position of the lowest 0 bit of $B$ — the bit the binary counter would set next, which is the Gray-code bit to flip.

The note states this invariant with the roles of 0 and 1 exchanged ("if $B[j] = 0$ and $B[j-1] = 1$, then $F[j]$ is the smallest $k > j$ with $B[k] = 1$"); that version fails already after the first increment ($B = 1$, but $F[0] = 1$, not 0) and again at count 2 ($F[1] = 2$). In the figure: at $k = 3$ (binary `0011`) $F[0] = 2$, the lowest 0 bit, and the fourth increment flips $G[2]$. The note leaves decrementing a Gray code in constant worst-case time as an open question ("Extra credit, anyone?").

## 9.5 Generalities and warnings

Amortization applies to any sequence of costs, not only to data structures: banks amortize loans into fixed payments, tax law lets businesses spread expenses over several years, and some phone plans roll unused minutes into the next month.

**Amortized bounds are not unique.** When a structure supports several operations, different schemes can assign different amortized costs to the same algorithms — say $O(\log n)$ for Fold and Spindle and $O(n)$ for Mutilate under one potential, and $O(\sqrt n)$ for Fold and $O(1)$ for the other two under another. Both can be correct at once, and which one is more useful depends on how often each operation is used.

> [!trap] What an amortized bound does and does not promise
> - **It bounds the total of every sequence**, from the stated starting state: $n$ operations cost at most $n$ times the amortized bound (plus $\Phi_0 - \Phi_n$, which is $\le 0$ for a valid potential). It is a worst-case guarantee, not an average over random inputs or coin flips.
> - **It says nothing about one operation.** Increment is $O(1)$ amortized but $\Theta(\log n)$ in the worst case; a structure with a real-time deadline per operation needs worst-case bounds, like Ehrlich's.
> - **The starting state matters.** From an arbitrary counter value $v$, $n$ Increments flip exactly $2n + \#_1(v) - \#_1(v + n)$ bits — up to $\#_1(v)$ more than $2n$, the potential the counter starts with. A potential must never drop below its initial value.
> - **Amortized is not expected.** "Expected amortized" bounds (such as dynamic hash tables in [[2-algorithms/05-basic-graph-algorithms#54-data-structures|§5.4]]) combine both: an average over random choices of a total over a sequence.

## Summary

- **Amortized cost** = share of the worst-case total over a sequence of operations; it bounds every sequence, involves no probability, and allows single expensive operations.
- **Binary counter:** one Increment flips (trailing 1s) + 1 bits, $\Theta(\log n)$ worst case; counting from 0 to $n$ flips exactly $2n - \#_1(n) < 2n$ bits, so 2 amortized.
- **Four methods:** summation ($T(n)/n$); taxation (\$2 per bit set, or $2^{-i}$ per bit per operation); charging (bill each clearing to the Increment that set the bit; at most 2 blocks per stack); potential ($a_i = c_i + \Phi_i - \Phi_{i-1}$, valid if $\Phi_i\ge\Phi_0$, then $\sum c_i\le\sum a_i$; $\Phi$ = number of 1s gives $a_i = 2$).
- **Increment and decrement:** a standard counter costs $\Theta(k)$ per operation around $2^k$; pairs $(P,N)$ with $P\wedge N = 0$ and value $P - N$ cost at most 2 amortized ($\Phi$ = number of 1s; each operation sets at most one bit).
- **Gray codes:** consecutive codes differ in one bit, the one a binary counter sets from 0 to 1; Ehrlich's focus array finds it in $O(1)$ worst-case time; $F[0]$ = lowest 0 bit of the binary count.
- **Warnings:** amortized bounds are not unique, depend on the starting state, and do not bound a single operation.
- The note ends with 16 exercises (cost functions to amortize, counters with extra work, extendable arrays, ordered stacks, multistacks, multi-pull queues, decimating queues, hash-table rebuilding, queues from stacks, a radmuque, modular "nudge" counters, Fibonacci and doubly lazy counters).

The summation argument (count how often each bit, or element, can change) is worth knowing anyway: it is the same argument as "each element is relabeled at most $\lg n$ times" in Kruskal's union-find.
