> [!abstract] In one breath
> A **tail inequality** bounds the probability that a random variable lands far from its mean. **Markov's inequality** needs only non-negativity: $\Pr[Z\ge z]\le\mathrm{E}[Z]/z$. For a sum $X$ of indicator variables with mean $\mu$, more independence buys thinner tails: **pairwise** independence gives **Chebyshev's inequality**, $\Pr[X\ge(1+\delta)\mu] < 1/(\delta^2\mu)$; $2k$-wise independence gives polynomial tails of degree $k$; and **full** independence gives the exponentially small **Chernoff bounds**, $\Pr[X\ge x]\le e^{x-\mu}(\mu/x)^x$ for $x\ge\mu$, or in simple form $\exp(-\delta^2\mu/3)$ above and $\exp(-\delta^2\mu/2)$ below ($0<\delta<1$). Fair coin flips show the difference. Applied to treaps: the indicators $[i\uparrow k]$ on each side of $k$ are mutually independent, so a Chernoff bound and a union bound show that an $n$-node random treap has depth at most $8\ln n$ with probability at least $1 - 2/n$.

## 4.1 Markov's inequality

> [!key] Markov's inequality
> Let $Z$ be a non-negative random variable. For every real $z > 0$, $\Pr[Z\ge z]\le\mathrm{E}[Z]/z$. In multiplicative form: $\Pr[Z\ge(1+\delta)\,\mathrm{E}[Z]]\le 1/(1+\delta)$.

> [!proof]- Proof (the note's, for integer-valued Z)
> Plot $z\mapsto\Pr[Z\ge z]$: a staircase. Cut the region under it into horizontal slabs, one per value $j$ of $Z$, of height $\Pr[Z=j]$ and width $j$: its area is $\sum_j j\Pr[Z=j] = \mathrm{E}[Z]$. Cut it into vertical columns instead: the area is $\sum_{j\ge1}\Pr[Z\ge j]$. For any $z$, the rectangle of width $z$ and height $\Pr[Z\ge z]$ fits under the staircase, so $z\Pr[Z\ge z]\le\mathrm{E}[Z]$. (For real-valued $Z$: $z\,[Z\ge z]\le Z$ pointwise; take expectations.) $\square$

Markov alone is usually too weak. For treaps, $\mathrm{E}[\mathit{depth}] < 2\ln n$ and a union bound over the $n$ nodes give only that every depth is $O(n^2\log n)$ with high probability — when every depth is less than $n$ anyway. Stronger bounds need more structure: independence.

## 4.2 Independence

> [!definition] Definition — independence
> Random variables $X$ and $Y$ are **independent** if $\Pr[X = x\wedge Y = y] = \Pr[X = x]\Pr[Y = y]$ for all values $x, y$ (equivalently $\Pr[X = x\mid Y = y] = \Pr[X = x]$). $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are **mutually** (or **fully**) independent if $\Pr[\bigwedge_i X_i = x_i] = \prod_i\Pr[X_i = x_i]$ for all values; **$k$-wise independent** if every $k$ of them are mutually independent; **pairwise** independent if $k = 2$ (the same notions for events: [[4-lecture-notes/01-discrete-probability#112-combining-events|note 1, §1.1.2]]).

For independent variables $\mathrm{E}[XY] = \mathrm{E}[X]\,\mathrm{E}[Y]$ (for mutually independent ones, $\mathrm{E}[\prod_i X_i] = \prod_i\mathrm{E}[X_i]$), and functions of independent variables are independent. For dependent variables these properties *need not* hold — the note says they do not hold, which is too strong for the first: $X$ uniform on $\{-1,0,1\}$ and $Y = X^2$ are dependent, yet $\mathrm{E}[XY] = 0 = \mathrm{E}[X]\mathrm{E}[Y]$. Two coin flips are independent; the numbers of heads and tails in $n$ flips are not (they add up to $n$).

$k$-wise independence implies $j$-wise for $j < k$, not conversely. **Example:** for independent random bits $X, Y, Z$ and $W = (X+Y+Z)\bmod 2$, any three of $W, X, Y, Z$ are mutually independent, but all four are not ($W$ is determined by the others). Limited independence matters because cheap hash functions often provide only that much.

## 4.3 Chebyshev's inequality

From here on $X = \sum_i X_i$ is a sum of indicator variables (random bits) with $p_i = \Pr[X_i = 1]$, and $\mu = \mathrm{E}[X] = \sum_i p_i$.

> [!key] Chebyshev's inequality
> If $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are **pairwise** independent (and $\mu > 0$), then $\Pr[(X-\mu)^2\ge z] < \mu/z$ for all $z > 0$.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Let $Y_i = X_i - p_i$ and $Y = \sum_i Y_i = X - \mu$. Expanding the square, $\mathrm{E}[Y^2] = \sum_i\mathrm{E}[Y_i^2] + \sum_{i\ne j}\mathrm{E}[Y_iY_j]$, and pairwise independence makes each cross term $\mathrm{E}[Y_i]\mathrm{E}[Y_j] = 0$. Each $\mathrm{E}[Y_i^2] = p_i(1-p_i)^2 + (1-p_i)p_i^2 = p_i(1-p_i)$, so $\mathrm{E}[Y^2] = \sum_i p_i(1-p_i) < \mu$. Apply Markov to the non-negative variable $Y^2$. $\square$

The proof shows that the **variance** $\sigma^2 = \sum_i p_i(1-p_i)$ adds up under pairwise independence, so the sharper $\Pr[\lvert X-\mu\rvert\ge\Delta]\le\sigma^2/\Delta^2$ holds too. With $z = \Delta^2$ and $\Delta = \delta\mu$, for all positive $\Delta$ and $\delta$:

| | additive | multiplicative |
|---|---|---|
| upper tail | $\Pr[X\ge\mu+\Delta] < \mu/\Delta^2$ | $\Pr[X\ge(1+\delta)\mu] < 1/(\delta^2\mu)$ |
| lower tail | $\Pr[X\le\mu-\Delta] < \mu/\Delta^2$ | $\Pr[X\le(1-\delta)\mu] < 1/(\delta^2\mu)$ |

**Law of large numbers.** For independent bits with $\Pr[X_i = 1] = p$ and $\overline{X}_n = \frac1n\sum_{i\le n}X_i$, Chebyshev gives $\Pr[\lvert\overline{X}_n - p\rvert > \varepsilon]\le p(1-p)/(n\varepsilon^2)\to 0$: the **weak** law. The **strong** law, $\Pr[\lim_n\overline{X}_n = p] = 1$, is stated without proof.

## 4.4 Higher moment inequalities

> [!key] General moment inequality (corrected)
> For every fixed integer $k > 0$: if $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are $2k$-wise independent and $\mu\ge 1$, then $\mathrm{E}[(X-\mu)^{2k}] = O(\mu^k)$, so $\Pr[(X-\mu)^{2k}\ge z] = O(\mu^k/z)$ for all $z > 0$, with constants depending on $k$. Hence
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \Pr[X\ge\mu+\Delta],\ \Pr[X\le\mu-\Delta] &= O\big((\mu/\Delta^2)^k\big),\\
> \Pr[X\ge(1+\delta)\mu],\ \Pr[X\le(1-\delta)\mu] &= O\big((1/(\delta^2\mu))^k\big).
> \end{aligned}
> $$

$k = 1$ is Chebyshev. For $k = 2$, 4-wise independence kills every term of $\mathrm{E}[(\sum_i Y_i)^4]$ in which some $Y_i$ appears exactly once, leaving $\sum_i\mathrm{E}[Y_i^4] + 3\sum_{i\ne j}\mathrm{E}[Y_i^2]\mathrm{E}[Y_j^2]\le\mu + 3\mu^2$. The more independence, the higher the degree of the polynomial tail. The note states the inequality without proof and with $(X-\mu)^k$ in place of $(X-\mu)^{2k}$, which does not yield the bounds listed under it; and the condition $\mu\ge 1$ (or a bound $O((\mu^k+\mu)/z)$) is needed: a single bit with tiny $\Pr = p$ has $\Pr[X\ge\mu+\tfrac12] = p$, far above $16p^2$.

## 4.5 Chernoff bounds

Chernoff (then at Illinois) published these bounds in 1952, crediting them later to his colleague Herman Rubin. They need **full** independence.

> [!key] Exponential moment inequality
> If $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are mutually independent indicators, then $\mathrm{E}[\alpha^X]\le e^{(\alpha-1)\mu}$ for every $\alpha > 0$.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> $\mathrm{E}[\alpha^{X_i}] = p_i\alpha + (1-p_i) = 1 + (\alpha-1)p_i\le e^{(\alpha-1)p_i}$, by $1 + t\le e^t$ (true for every real $t$). Independence turns the expectation of the product $\alpha^X = \prod_i\alpha^{X_i}$ into the product of the expectations: $\mathrm{E}[\alpha^X]\le\prod_i e^{(\alpha-1)p_i} = e^{(\alpha-1)\mu}$. $\square$

(The note states it for $\alpha\ge 1$ only, but its proof of the lower tail uses $\alpha < 1$; the proof above works for every $\alpha > 0$.)

> [!key] Chernoff bounds
> If $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are mutually independent indicators, then
> $$
> \Pr[X\ge x]\le e^{x-\mu}\Big(\frac{\mu}{x}\Big)^{x}\quad\text{for } x\ge\mu,\qquad\qquad \Pr[X\le x]\le e^{x-\mu}\Big(\frac{\mu}{x}\Big)^{x}\quad\text{for } 0 < x\le\mu.
> $$

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Upper tail: $t\mapsto(x/\mu)^t$ is increasing, so $\Pr[X\ge x] = \Pr[(x/\mu)^X\ge(x/\mu)^x]\le\mathrm{E}[(x/\mu)^X]/(x/\mu)^x$ by Markov, and the exponential moment inequality with $\alpha = x/\mu$ bounds the numerator by $e^{x-\mu}$. Lower tail: now $x/\mu\le 1$ and $t\mapsto(x/\mu)^t$ is decreasing, so $\Pr[X\le x] = \Pr[(x/\mu)^X\ge(x/\mu)^x]$, and the rest is the same. $\square$

The same argument with any $\alpha$ gives $\Pr[X\ge x]\le e^{(\alpha-1)\mu}/\alpha^x$ for $\alpha > 1$ and $\Pr[X\le x]\le e^{(\alpha-1)\mu}/\alpha^x$ for $0 < \alpha < 1$; calculus shows $\alpha = x/\mu$ minimizes the right side. Substituting $x = \mu\pm\Delta$ or $x = (1\pm\delta)\mu$ gives the traditional forms, for $\Delta,\delta > 0$ and, in the lower tail, $\Delta < \mu$ and $\delta < 1$ (the note says any positive $\Delta$ and $\delta$) — note the asymmetry between the tails:

| | additive | multiplicative |
|---|---|---|
| upper tail | $\Pr[X\ge\mu+\Delta]\le e^{\Delta}\big(\frac{\mu}{\mu+\Delta}\big)^{\mu+\Delta}$ | $\Pr[X\ge(1+\delta)\mu]\le\Big(\frac{e^{\delta}}{(1+\delta)^{1+\delta}}\Big)^{\mu}$ |
| lower tail | $\Pr[X\le\mu-\Delta]\le e^{-\Delta}\big(\frac{\mu}{\mu-\Delta}\big)^{\mu-\Delta}$ | $\Pr[X\le(1-\delta)\mu]\le\Big(\frac{e^{-\delta}}{(1-\delta)^{1-\delta}}\Big)^{\mu}$ |

> [!key] Chernoff bounds, simple forms (for 0 < δ < 1)
> $$
> \Pr[X\ge(1+\delta)\mu]\le\exp(-\delta^2\mu/3),\qquad\qquad \Pr[X\le(1-\delta)\mu]\le\exp(-\delta^2\mu/2).
> $$

They follow from the multiplicative forms because $\delta - (1+\delta)\ln(1+\delta)\le-\delta^2/3$ (from $\ln(1+\delta)\ge 2\delta/(2+\delta)$, which is the note's $1/\ln(1+\delta)\le\tfrac12 + 1/\delta$) and $-\delta - (1-\delta)\ln(1-\delta)\le-\delta^2/2$, which needs $(1-\delta)\ln(1-\delta)\ge-\delta+\delta^2/2$. The note's second ingredient, "$\ln(1-\delta)\ge-\delta+\delta^2/2$", is false for every $\delta\in(0,1)$ (in fact $\ln(1-\delta) < -\delta$); the factor $1-\delta$ is what makes it true.

Concept page: [[concepts/tail-inequalities]].

## 4.6 Coin flips

Flip $N$ fair coins; $X$ = the number of heads, a sum of independent indicators with $p_i = 1/2$, so $\mu = N/2$ and variance $N/4$. Bound $\Pr[X\ge\alpha N]$ for a constant $\tfrac12 < \alpha < 1$, i.e. $\Delta = (2\alpha-1)N/2$ and $\delta = 2\alpha - 1$:

| bound | $\Pr[X\ge\alpha N]\le$ | $\alpha = 3/4$ | $\alpha = 9/10$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Markov | $1/(2\alpha)$ | $2/3$ | $5/9$ |
| Chebyshev, $\mu/\Delta^2$ | $2/((2\alpha-1)^2N)$ | $8/N$ | $25/(8N)$ |
| Chebyshev, variance $N/4$ | $1/((2\alpha-1)^2N)$ | $4/N$ | $25/(16N)$ |
| fourth moment | $O(1/N^2)$ | | |
| Chernoff, simple form | $\exp(-(2\alpha-1)^2N/6)$ | $e^{-N/24}$ | $e^{-8N/75} < e^{-N/10}$ |
| binomial coefficients | $\big(2\alpha^\alpha(1-\alpha)^{1-\alpha}\big)^{-N}$ | $\approx e^{-0.13081N}$ | $\approx e^{-0.36806N}$ |

For the lower tail ($\alpha < 1/2$) the simple Chernoff form gives $\Pr[X\le\alpha N]\le\exp(-(1-2\alpha)^2N/4)$, so $\Pr[X\le N/4] < e^{-N/16}$ and $\Pr[X\le N/10] < e^{-4N/25}\le e^{-N/7}$. The last row, which uses that the coins are fair, holds for both tails ($\alpha\ge\tfrac12$ above, $\alpha\le\tfrac12$ below) and is symmetric: $\Pr[X\le N/4]\le e^{-0.13081N} < e^{-N/8}$ and $\Pr[X\le N/10]\le e^{-0.36806N} < e^{-N/3}$.

The note's Chebyshev line reads "$1/((2\alpha-1)N) = O(1/(\alpha N))$", with $2/N$ and $5/(4N)$ (p. 6): it drops the square. Its own form $\mu/\Delta^2$ gives $2/((2\alpha-1)^2N)$, that is $8/N$ and $25/(8N)$; the variance $N/4$ gives $4/N$ and $25/(16N)$. (The numbers $2/N$ and $5/(4N)$ happen to be valid bounds, but not consequences of Chebyshev; the general formula is false: for $\alpha = 0.55$ and $N = 100$ it gives $0.1$, while $\Pr[X\ge 55]\approx 0.184$.) For constant $\alpha$ all of these are $O(1/N)$, with a constant that blows up like $(2\alpha-1)^{-2}$ as $\alpha\to\tfrac12$.

## 4.7 Back to treaps, take 1

An informal proof, following the good-pivot analysis of quicksort, that a fixed node $k$ of an $n$-node random treap has depth $O(\log n)$ with high probability. Let $v_i$ be the ancestor of $k$ at depth $i$ and $n_i$ the size of its subtree (the subproblem containing $k$ after $i$ levels of quicksort). For $i\ge1$, call level $i$ **good** if $n_{i-1}/4\le n_i\le 3n_{i-1}/4$, as long as $i\le\mathit{depth}(k)$, and decide by a fair coin flip for $i>\mathit{depth}(k)$. (The note flips the coin already at $i = \mathit{depth}(k)$; then the next claim fails: for $n = 5$, $k = 2$, level 1 is good with probability only $7/15$.)

- Whatever happened above it, each level is good with probability at least $1/2$ (the root of a subtree is uniform among its nodes). The note says the events are independent (they are not: for $n = 3$ and $k = 1$, levels 1 and 2 are good with probabilities $5/6$ and $7/12$, but both with probability $1/2$, not $35/72$); what holds, and suffices, is this conditional bound, which lets the levels be compared with independent fair coins.
- At most $\log_{4/3}n$ of the levels $1,\dots,\mathit{depth}(k)$ are good, since each good level shrinks the subtree by a factor $3/4$ and $n_{\mathit{depth}(k)}\ge1$.

So $\mathit{depth}(k)\ge c\log_{4/3}n$ requires at most $\log_{4/3}n$ heads in about $c\log_{4/3}n$ fair flips, and the lower-tail Chernoff bound of §4.6 with $N = c\log_{4/3}n$ and $\alpha = 1/c$ (for $c > 2$) gives
$$
\Pr\big[\mathit{depth}(k)\ge c\log_{4/3}n\big]\le\exp\Big(-\frac{(1-2/c)^2}{4}\,c\log_{4/3}n\Big) = n^{-(c-2)^2/(4c\ln(4/3))}.
$$
With $c = 20\ln(4/3)\approx 5.75364$ the threshold is $20\ln n$ and the exponent is $2.128$: $\Pr[\mathit{depth}(k) > 20\ln n] < 1/n^2$. The binomial-coefficient bound with $c = 10$ gives $\Pr[\mathit{depth}(k)\ge 10\log_{4/3}n] < n^{-10/(3\ln(4/3))} = n^{-11.587} < 1/n^{11}$.

## 4.8 Back to treaps: take 2

Recall from [[4-lecture-notes/03-treaps-and-skip-lists#313-random-priorities|note 3]]: $\Pr[i\uparrow k] = [i\ne k]/(\lvert k-i\rvert+1)$ and $\mathrm{E}[\mathit{depth}(k)] = H_k + H_{n-k+1} - 2 < 2\ln n$. (Here the note writes $H_k + H_{n-k} - 2$ (p. 8), which is off by one.) To bound the *maximum* depth, split the depth into the ancestors on each side:
$$
\mathit{depth}(k) = \mathit{depth}_<(k) + \mathit{depth}_>(k),\qquad \mathit{depth}_<(k) = \sum_{i<k}[i\uparrow k],\quad \mathit{depth}_>(k) = \sum_{i>k}[i\uparrow k].
$$

> [!key] Lemma 1
> For every $k$, the $k-1$ indicators $[i\uparrow k]$ with $i < k$ are mutually independent, and so are the $n-k$ indicators with $i > k$.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Take $i > k$ and induct on $n$. $[n\uparrow k] = 1$ iff node $n$ has the smallest priority among nodes $k,\dots,n$; the other indicators depend only on the relative order of the priorities of nodes $k,\dots,n-1$. Of the $(n-k+1)!$ equally likely orders of the priorities of nodes $k..n$, exactly $(n-k)!$ put node $n$ first, and in those (and in the others) the order of nodes $k..n-1$ is uniform. So conditioning on $[n\uparrow k]$ does not change the joint distribution of the rest, which are mutually independent by induction. (The note counts "$(n-k-1)!$ permutations of the $n-k$ priorities", off by one.) $\square$

The two groups together are *not* independent (the note's Exercise 1), which is why the depth is split. Let $Z_2,\dots,Z_n$ be independent indicators with $\Pr[Z_j = 1] = 1/j$ and $Z = \sum_j Z_j$, so $\mathrm{E}[Z] = H_n - 1 < \ln n$. By Lemma 1, $\mathit{depth}_<(k)$ has the distribution of $Z_2 + \dots + Z_k$ and $\mathit{depth}_>(k)$ that of $Z_2 + \dots + Z_{n-k+1}$; both are at most $Z$ in distribution.

> [!key] Lemma 2
> $\Pr[Z > 4\ln n] < 1/n^2$.

> [!proof]- Proof (corrected)
> Apply the Chernoff bound with $x = 4\ln n\ge\mu$: $\Pr[Z\ge x]\le e^{x-\mu}(\mu/x)^x$. As a function of $\mu$, the right side increases on $(0, x]$ (its logarithm has derivative $x/\mu - 1\ge0$), so it may be evaluated at $\mu = \ln n > H_n - 1$: $\Pr[Z\ge 4\ln n]\le e^{3\ln n}(1/4)^{4\ln n} = n^{3-4\ln4}\approx n^{-2.54518} < 1/n^2$. $\square$
>
> The note instead writes $\Pr[Z\ge 4\mu]\le(e^3/4^4)^\mu < (e^3/4^4)^{\ln n}$ (p. 9, with "$\delta = c-1$" for $\delta = 3$). The last step is backwards: the base $e^3/4^4\approx 0.078$ is less than 1 and $\mu < \ln n$, so $(e^3/4^4)^\mu$ is the *larger* of the two. The monotonicity above repairs it; the final number is right.

> [!key] Theorem 3
> An $n$-node random treap has depth $O(\log n)$ with high probability: $\Pr[\max_k\mathit{depth}(k) > 8\ln n] < 2/n$.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Union bound over the $n$ nodes, and for each node over the two halves: $\mathit{depth}(k) > 8\ln n$ forces $\mathit{depth}_<(k) > 4\ln n$ or $\mathit{depth}_>(k) > 4\ln n$, each of probability at most $\Pr[Z > 4\ln n] < 1/n^2$ (Lemma 2). Total: $< 2n/n^2 = 2/n$. $\square$
>
> (Typos on p. 9: "bound the probability that the depth is at most $8\ln n$" means *more than*; "Lemma ??" is Lemma 1; the sum $\sum_{i=1}^{\ell-1}Z_\ell$ should be $\sum_{j=2}^{k}Z_j$; and the "symmetric" case is $\mathit{depth}_>(k)$.)

So search, insertion, deletion, split and join take $O(\log n)$ time with high probability, and the expected *worst-case* depth is $O(\log n)$ as well: at most $8\ln n + (n-1)\cdot 2/n < 8\ln n + 2$. The true values are much smaller — the expected height of a random 1000-node treap is $21.04$, against $8\ln 1000\approx 55.3$.

**The general bound.** The note ends: for any constant $c$, the depth exceeds $c\ln n$ with probability at most $2/n^{c\ln c - c}$. That is false. For $n = 1000$ and $c = 4$ it promises $4.6\cdot10^{-5}$, but the exact probability that the height exceeds $4\ln n\approx 27.6$ is $0.0033$. Rerunning the proof with threshold $c\ln n$ per half (Chernoff with $x = c\ln n$ and $\mu\to\ln n$ gives $n^{c-1-c\ln c}$, times $2n$) bounds the probability that the depth exceeds **$2c\ln n$** by $2/n^{c\ln c - c}$; equivalently, depth $> c\ln n$ with probability at most $2/n^{(c/2)\ln(c/2) - c/2}$, which is meaningful for $c > 2e$ and gives Theorem 3 at $c = 8$.

## Summary

- **Markov:** $Z\ge0\Rightarrow\Pr[Z\ge z]\le\mathrm{E}[Z]/z$. Weak alone; it is the engine inside every other bound (apply it to $(X-\mu)^2$, $(X-\mu)^{2k}$ or $\alpha^X$).
- **Independence** comes in degrees: mutual ⇒ $k$-wise ⇒ pairwise, not conversely; with $W = X\oplus Y\oplus Z$, the set $\{W, X, Y, Z\}$ is 3-wise but not 4-wise independent.
- **Chebyshev** (pairwise independent indicators): variance $\sum p_i(1-p_i) < \mu$; $\Pr[X\ge(1+\delta)\mu] < 1/(\delta^2\mu)$. Gives the weak law of large numbers. $2k$-wise independence (and $\mu\ge1$): $O((1/(\delta^2\mu))^k)$.
- **Chernoff** (mutually independent indicators): $\mathrm{E}[\alpha^X]\le e^{(\alpha-1)\mu}$, then Markov with $\alpha = x/\mu$: $\Pr[X\ge x]\le e^{x-\mu}(\mu/x)^x$ ($x\ge\mu$), same for $\Pr[X\le x]$ ($x\le\mu$); simple forms $e^{-\delta^2\mu/3}$ and $e^{-\delta^2\mu/2}$ for $0<\delta<1$.
- **Coin flips:** Markov $\Theta(1)$, Chebyshev $O(1/N)$, Chernoff $e^{-\Theta(N)}$.
- **Treaps:** on each side of $k$ the ancestor indicators are independent, $\Pr = 1/j$ each; Chernoff + union bound: depth $\le 8\ln n$ with probability $> 1 - 2/n$ (Theorem 3); depth $> 2c\ln n$ with probability $\le 2/n^{c\ln c - c}$.
- The note ends with 6 exercises (why all $n-1$ indicators are not independent; descendants versus ancestors; how often a random-order scan updates its minimum; Pareto-optimal points; the depth of a heater; iterated variances of coin flips).
