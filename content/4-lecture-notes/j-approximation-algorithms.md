> [!abstract] In one breath
> When a problem is NP-hard, a polynomial-time algorithm can still promise a solution within a fixed factor of the best one: an **$\alpha$-approximation algorithm** returns, on every input, a solution whose cost is at most $\alpha$ times the optimum (for a minimization problem). Every proof compares the algorithm with a **lower bound** on the optimum — the longest job and the average load, a matching, a linear-programming relaxation, a minimum spanning tree. The classic results: greedy load balancing within 2 (3/2 after sorting); greedy vertex cover within $O(\log n)$ and greedy set cover within $1 + \ln|X|$; vertex cover within 2 by taking both ends of a maximal matching, and weighted vertex cover within 2 by rounding a linear program; the traveling salesman within 2 (tree doubling) and 3/2 (Christofides) when distances obey the triangle inequality, but not within any polynomial-time computable factor $f(n)$ without it unless $\textsf{P} = \textsf{NP}$; $k$-center clustering within 2. It ends with **approximation schemes**, which get within $1+\varepsilon$ for every $\varepsilon > 0$, and an **FPTAS** for subset sum, polynomial in both $n$ and $1/\varepsilon$.

## J.1 Load balancing

> [!definition] Definition — makespan, load balancing
> There are $n$ jobs with running times $T[1\,..\,n]\ge 0$ and $m$ identical machines. An **assignment** is an array $A[1\,..\,n]$, where $A[j] = i$ puts job $j$ on machine $i$. Its **makespan** is the time the busiest machine needs:
> $$
> \textit{makespan}(A) = \max_i \sum_{j:\,A[j] = i} T[j].
> $$
> The **load balancing** problem asks for an assignment with the smallest makespan.

It is NP-hard: an array $T$ can be split into two halves of equal sum (Partition) if and only if two machines can reach makespan $\sum_j T[j]/2$. A slightly more complicated reduction from 3Partition shows it is NP-hard even with all numbers written in unary (strongly NP-hard, in the sense of [[2-algorithms/12-np-hardness#caveat-reductor|§12.12]]). A dynamic program over the vectors of machine loads solves it in $O(nM^m)$ time, where $M$ is the optimal makespan — exponential in $m$.

The natural greedy algorithm puts each job, in input order, on the machine that is free earliest.

```text
GreedyLoadBalance(T[1 .. n], m):
    for i ← 1 to m
        Total[i] ← 0
    for j ← 1 to n
        mini ← arg min_i Total[i]          ⟨⟨least-loaded machine⟩⟩
        A[j] ← mini
        Total[mini] ← Total[mini] + T[j]
    return A[1 .. n]
```

As written it takes $O(nm)$ time; with the loads in a min-heap, $O(n\log m)$. (The note returns $A[1\,..\,m]$; the assignment has $n$ entries.)

> [!key] Theorem J.1
> The makespan of the assignment computed by GreedyLoadBalance is at most twice the optimal makespan $\mathrm{OPT}$.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Two lower bounds hold for **every** assignment, hence for the optimal one: some machine runs the longest job, and the busiest machine runs at least the average load,
> $$
> \mathrm{OPT}\ \ge\ \max_j T[j] \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathrm{OPT}\ \ge\ \frac1m\sum_{j=1}^n T[j].
> $$
> Let machine $i$ have the largest final load, and let $j$ be the last job assigned to it. By the first bound, $T[j]\le\mathrm{OPT}$. When $j$ arrived, machine $i$ had the smallest load, $\textit{Total}[i] - T[j]$; loads only grow, so this is at most every final load $\textit{Total}[k]$, hence at most their average $\frac1m\sum_j T[j]\le\mathrm{OPT}$ by the second bound. So the makespan $\textit{Total}[i] = (\textit{Total}[i] - T[j]) + T[j]\le 2\cdot\mathrm{OPT}$. $\square$

GreedyLoadBalance is an **online** algorithm: it decides about each job without seeing the later ones, as a real scheduler must. When all jobs are known in advance (**offline**), sorting them first helps.

```text
SortedGreedyLoadBalance(T[1 .. n], m):
    sort T in decreasing order
    return GreedyLoadBalance(T, m)
```

> [!key] Theorem J.2
> The makespan of the assignment computed by SortedGreedyLoadBalance is at most $\frac32$ times the optimal makespan.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Let $i$ be the busiest machine and $j$ the last job assigned to it. If $i$ received only one job, its load is $T[j]\le\mathrm{OPT}$ and the schedule is optimal. Otherwise $j\ge m+1$: the $m$ longest jobs go to $m$ different machines. As in Theorem J.1, $\textit{Total}[i] - T[j]\le\mathrm{OPT}$. In every schedule, the optimal one included, two of the $m+1$ longest jobs share a machine (pigeonhole); call the two that share one in the optimal schedule $k$ and $\ell$, so $T[k] + T[\ell]\le\mathrm{OPT}$. The jobs are sorted and $k,\ell\le m+1\le j$, so
> $$
> T[j]\ \le\ T[m+1]\ \le\ \min\{T[k], T[\ell]\}\ \le\ \mathrm{OPT}/2,
> $$
> and the makespan is at most $\mathrm{OPT} + \mathrm{OPT}/2$. $\square$

Neither analysis is tight. The exact worst-case ratios, stated in the note without proof, are $2 - 1/m$ for GreedyLoadBalance and $\frac43 - \frac1{3m}$ for SortedGreedyLoadBalance (Graham's bounds); both are attained by small instances, and the first is the note's Exercise 1.

> [!trap] Load-balancing traps
> - **The proof never computes OPT.** It compares the greedy makespan with two quantities that every schedule must exceed. That is the method of the whole note.
> - **Which job $j$:** the *last* job on the *busiest* machine — only for that job is the load underneath it known to be the minimum at the time it was placed.
> - **Sorting is in decreasing order**; sorted increasingly, the longest job comes last, which is the worst case for the first proof.

## J.2 Generalities

> [!definition] Definition — approximation algorithm, approximation factor
> For an optimization problem, let $\mathrm{OPT}(X)$ be the value of an optimal solution for input $X$ and $A(X)$ the value of the solution computed by algorithm $A$. Then $A$ is an **$\alpha(n)$-approximation algorithm** if, for every input $X$ of size $n$,
> $$
> \frac{\mathrm{OPT}(X)}{A(X)}\le\alpha(n) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{A(X)}{\mathrm{OPT}(X)}\le\alpha(n).
> $$
> $\alpha(n)\ge 1$ is the **approximation factor** (or ratio). For a **minimization** problem $A(X)\ge\mathrm{OPT}(X)$, so only the second inequality has content; for a **maximization** problem, only the first. A 1-approximation algorithm is exact.

(The note writes "maximization" in both sentences; the first is about minimization problems.)

The general recipe is the one Theorem J.1 used. Since an optimal solution of an NP-hard problem cannot be characterized, find an intermediate quantity $f(X)$ that is both a **lower bound** on the optimal cost and an **upper bound** on the algorithm's cost, up to constants: if $\mathrm{OPT}(X)\ge f(X)/2$ and $A(X)\le 5f(X)$ for some function $f$, then $A$ is a 10-approximation. Choosing $f$ is the art.

## J.3 Greedy vertex cover

A **vertex cover** of a graph is a set of vertices that touches every edge; finding the smallest is NP-hard ([[2-algorithms/12-np-hardness#129-clique-and-vertex-cover-from-independent-set|§12.9]]). (The note's first sentence says "vertex color".) The obvious greedy heuristic takes a vertex of largest degree, deletes it with its edges, and repeats.

```text
GreedyVertexCover(G):
    C ← ∅
    while G has at least one edge
        v ← a vertex of maximum degree in G
        G ← G \ v                          ⟨⟨delete v and its edges⟩⟩
        C ← C ∪ {v}
    return C
```

It does not always find a minimum cover (that would prove $\textsf{P} = \textsf{NP}$), but it is not far off.

> [!key] Theorem J.3
> GreedyVertexCover is an $O(\log n)$-approximation algorithm, for $n$-vertex graphs.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Let $G_0 = G$, let $G_i$ be the graph after $i$ iterations, and let $d_i$ be the degree of the vertex removed in iteration $i$ — the maximum degree in $G_{i-1}$. Write $|G_i|$ for the number of edges of $G_i$, and let $C^*$ be a minimum vertex cover of $G$, with $\mathrm{OPT}$ vertices. $C^*$ also covers $G_{i-1}$, so its vertices' degrees in $G_{i-1}$ add up to at least $|G_{i-1}|$; some vertex has degree at least $|G_{i-1}|/\mathrm{OPT}$, hence $d_i\ge|G_{i-1}|/\mathrm{OPT}$. For $i\le\mathrm{OPT}$, $|G_{i-1}|\ge|G_{\mathrm{OPT}}|$, so
> $$
> \sum_{i=1}^{\mathrm{OPT}} d_i\ \ge\ \sum_{i=1}^{\mathrm{OPT}}\frac{|G_{i-1}|}{\mathrm{OPT}}\ \ge\ |G_{\mathrm{OPT}}|\ =\ |G| - \sum_{i=1}^{\mathrm{OPT}} d_i,
> $$
> that is, the first $\mathrm{OPT}$ iterations delete at least half of the edges (if the loop runs that long). The remaining graph has a vertex cover of size at most $\mathrm{OPT}$, so the same holds for every later block of $\mathrm{OPT}$ iterations. After $k$ blocks at most $|G|/2^k$ edges remain, which is less than one edge once $k = \lfloor\lg|G|\rfloor + 1$. So the algorithm stops after at most $\mathrm{OPT}\,(\lfloor\lg|G|\rfloor + 1)\le\mathrm{OPT}\,(2\lg n + 1)$ iterations, and $|C| = O(\mathrm{OPT}\log n)$. $\square$

(The note bounds the number of iterations by $\mathrm{OPT}\lg|G|$, which fails for a graph with a single edge; the extra block above repairs it. It also writes $G_i$ for $G_{i-1}$ once.) GreedyVertexCover is the greedy set-cover algorithm of §J.4 applied to the edges, so the sharper bound proved there applies: at most $H(\Delta)\cdot\mathrm{OPT}\le(1 + \ln\Delta)\,\mathrm{OPT}$, where $\Delta$ is the maximum degree and $H(k) = 1 + \frac12 + \cdots + \frac1k$.

**No better ratio.** The note asserts that a recursive construction gives $n$-vertex graphs on which the greedy cover has size $\Omega(\mathrm{OPT}\log n)$, and leaves the details as Exercise 2.

## J.4 Set cover and hitting set

> [!definition] Definition — set system, set cover, hitting set
> A **set system** $(X, \mathcal F)$ is a finite ground set $X$ and a family $\mathcal F$ of subsets of $X$. A **set cover** is a subfamily of $\mathcal F$ whose union is $X$; a **hitting set** is a subset of $X$ that meets every set in $\mathcal F$. The problems ask for the smallest of each.

A graph is a set system in two ways. With the vertices as ground set and each edge a two-element set, a vertex cover is a **hitting set**. With the edges as ground set and each vertex standing for the set of edges at it, a vertex cover is a **set cover**. The greedy vertex-cover algorithm generalizes both ways:

```text
GreedySetCover(X, F):
    C ← ∅
    while X ≠ ∅
        S ← arg max_{S ∈ F} |S ∩ X|           ⟨⟨most uncovered elements⟩⟩
        X ← X \ S
        C ← C ∪ {S}
    return C
```

```text
GreedyHittingSet(X, F):
    H ← ∅
    while F ≠ ∅
        x ← arg max_{x ∈ X} |{S ∈ F : x ∈ S}|  ⟨⟨in most unhit sets⟩⟩
        F ← F \ {S ∈ F : x ∈ S}
        H ← H ∪ {x}
    return H
```

> [!key] Approximation ratios of the greedy algorithms
> GreedySetCover returns at most $H(\max_{S\in\mathcal F}|S|)\cdot\mathrm{OPT}\le(1 + \ln|X|)\,\mathrm{OPT}$ sets. GreedyHittingSet returns at most $H(d)\cdot\mathrm{OPT}\le(1 + \ln|\mathcal F|)\,\mathrm{OPT}$ elements, where $d$ is the largest number of sets containing one element. So set cover is approximated within a logarithm of the size of the **ground set**, hitting set within a logarithm of the size of the **family**. Up to lower-order terms, no polynomial-time algorithm does better unless $\textsf{P} = \textsf{NP}$.

**The note swaps $|X|$ and $|\mathcal F|$ here** (and again at the end of §J.7): it claims $O(\log|\mathcal F|)$ for set cover and $O(\log|X|)$ for hitting set. The figure is a counterexample to the first claim: with $k$ blocks, greedy takes $k$ sets where 2 suffice, a ratio of $k/2$ with only $|\mathcal F| = k + 2$ sets.

> [!proof]- Proof of the set-cover bound (click to expand)
> This is the standard "pricing" proof (the note has only a placeholder for it). When greedy picks a set that covers $k$ new elements, charge each of those elements $1/k$; the total charge is the number of sets greedy picks. Fix a set $S$ of an optimal cover and list its elements in the order greedy covers them. When the $r$-th from last of them is covered, $S$ itself still has at least $r$ uncovered elements, so the set greedy picks covers at least $r$ new ones and the charge is at most $1/r$. Hence the elements of $S$ pay at most $1 + \frac12 + \cdots + \frac1{|S|} = H(|S|)$. Every element lies in some set of the optimal cover, so greedy picks at most $\sum_{S} H(|S|)\le\mathrm{OPT}\cdot H(\max|S|)$ sets, and $H(k)\le 1 + \ln k$. The hitting-set bound is the same statement for the dual system below. $\square$

**Duality.** For $x\in X$ let $x^* = \{S\in\mathcal F : x\in S\}$, and let $X^* = \{x^* : x\in X\}$ (the note writes $x\in S$). The **dual** set system $(\mathcal F, X^*)$ has the sets as ground set. A family $C\subseteq\mathcal F$ covers $X$ exactly when it meets every $x^*$: set covers of $(X,\mathcal F)$ are the hitting sets of the dual, and vice versa; the dual of the dual is the original system again, up to renaming, provided no two elements lie in exactly the same sets (otherwise $x^* = y^*$ and $X^*$ is smaller than $X$; the note claims it for every set system). The two greedy algorithms are the same algorithm on dual systems.

## J.5 Vertex cover redux

Greed is not the best idea here. A "stupid" heuristic does better:

```text
DumbVertexCover(G):
    C ← ∅
    while G has at least one edge
        uv ← any edge in G
        G ← G \ {u, v}                     ⟨⟨delete both ends and their edges⟩⟩
        C ← C ∪ {u, v}
    return C
```

> [!key] DumbVertexCover is a 2-approximation
> The edges chosen by the loop share no endpoints — each choice deletes both its ends — so they form a **matching**, and $C$ consists of the $2|M|$ endpoints of that matching $M$. **Every** vertex cover contains an endpoint of each matching edge, all different, so $\mathrm{OPT}\ge|M| = |C|/2$. And $C$ is a vertex cover, because the loop stops only when no edge is left.

The lower bound is now a matching, not a degree count. Surprisingly, no polynomial-time algorithm with ratio $2-\varepsilon$, for a constant $\varepsilon > 0$, is known for vertex cover. The same idea gives a hitting-set algorithm whose ratio is the size of the largest set in $\mathcal F$: repeatedly take **all** elements of some set that has not been hit yet; every hitting set needs a different element from each set taken.

## J.6 Lightest vertex cover: LP rounding

Now each vertex $v$ has a weight $w(v)\ge 0$, and we want a vertex cover $C$ of minimum total weight $w(C) = \sum_{v\in C}w(v)$. Both heuristics above can be arbitrarily bad: on the path $a\,b\,c$ with $w(a) = w(c) = 1$ and $w(b) = 10^{100}$, the lightest cover $\{a, c\}$ weighs 2, greedy takes $b$ (weight $10^{100}$), and DumbVertexCover takes $b$ and one endpoint ($10^{100} + 1$).

The problem is an **integer linear program** (ILP):
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{minimize}\quad & \sum_v w(v)\,x(v)\\
\text{subject to}\quad & x(u) + x(v)\ge 1 \quad\text{for every edge } uv,\\
& x(v)\in\{0,1\} \quad\text{for every vertex } v.
\end{aligned}
$$
Its **linear relaxation** (LP) is the same program with $x(v)\in\{0,1\}$ replaced by $0\le x(v)\le 1$. Integer solutions are exactly the indicator vectors of vertex covers, so the ILP's optimum is $\mathrm{OPT}$. (Most NP-hard problems of [[2-algorithms/12-np-hardness|Chapter 12]] can be written as integer programs, so integer linear programming is NP-hard.) The LP can be solved in polynomial time (notes [[4-lecture-notes/h-linear-programming|H]] and [[4-lecture-notes/i-linear-programming-algorithms|I]]); let $x^*$ be an optimal **fractional solution** and $\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}} = \sum_v w(v)\,x^*(v)$ its value.

> [!key] LP rounding is a 2-approximation for lightest vertex cover
> Solve the LP and **round**: $x'(v) = 1$ if $x^*(v)\ge\frac12$, and $x'(v) = 0$ otherwise. Then $x'$ is a vertex cover, and
> $$
> \sum_v w(v)\,x'(v)\ \le\ 2\sum_v w(v)\,x^*(v)\ =\ 2\,\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}\ \le\ 2\,\mathrm{OPT}.
> $$
> **Lower bound:** every vertex cover is feasible for the LP, so $\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}\le\mathrm{OPT}$. **Cover:** for each edge $uv$, $x^*(u) + x^*(v)\ge 1$ forces $\max\{x^*(u), x^*(v)\}\ge\frac12$. **Cost:** $x'(v)\le 2x^*(v)$ for every vertex.

> [!example] Example — the 5-cycle, and why 2 is the limit of this lower bound
> On the cycle $C_5$ with unit weights, adding the five constraints gives $2\sum_v x(v)\ge 5$, so $\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}\ge\frac52$, attained only by $x^*\equiv\frac12$. Rounding takes all five vertices: weight $5 = 2\,\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}$, while $\mathrm{OPT} = 3$. On the complete graph $K_n$ the LP value is again $n/2$ (all $\frac12$) but $\mathrm{OPT} = n - 1$, so $\mathrm{OPT}/\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}\to 2$: no algorithm whose analysis compares with $\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}$ can prove a ratio below 2.

## J.7 Randomized LP rounding

The fractional values can also be used as probabilities: independently for each vertex, set $x'(v) = 1$ with probability $x^*(v)$. (The random choices are the coin flips of the rounding; the LP solution $x^*$ is fixed.) By linearity of expectation, $\mathrm{E}\bigl[\sum_v w(v)\,x'(v)\bigr] = \widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}\le\mathrm{OPT}$, but $x'$ need not be a cover: an edge $uv$ is missed with probability
$$
\Pr[x'(u) = 0\land x'(v) = 0] = (1 - x^*(u))(1 - x^*(v))\ \le\ \tfrac14,
$$
because under the constraint $x^*(u) + x^*(v)\ge 1$ the product is **maximized** at $x^*(u) = x^*(v) = \frac12$. So each edge is covered with probability at least $\frac34$, and in expectation at least $\frac34$ of the edges are covered. (The note writes the constraint as $x^*(u) = x^*(v)\ge 1$ and says the product is "minimized" there; its minimum is 0.)

To get a cover, take the union of several independent roundings:

```text
ApproxLightestVertexCover(G):
    C ← ∅
    x* ← optimal fractional solution of the LP
    for i ← 1 to 2 lg n
        for all vertices v
            with probability x*(v)
                C ← C ∪ {v}
    return C
```

(The note adds $\{x\}$ instead of $\{v\}$.) Equivalently, with $N = 2\lg n$, each vertex joins $C$ independently with probability $1 - (1 - x^*(v))^N$.
- **Cover, with high probability.** An edge stays uncovered with probability $(1 - x^*(u))^N(1 - x^*(v))^N\le 4^{-N} = n^{-4}$. There are fewer than $n^2/2$ edges, so by the union bound $C$ is a vertex cover with probability at least $1 - 1/(2n^2)$ (the note says $1 - 1/n^2$, which is weaker and also true).
- **Weight, in expectation.** $1 - (1-x)^N\le Nx$, so $\mathrm{E}[w(C)]\le N\cdot\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}\le 2\lg n\cdot\mathrm{OPT}$. The note says "exactly $N\cdot\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}$"; it is strictly less whenever some $x^*(v) > 0$ — on $C_5$ with $N = 2$, $\mathrm{E}[w(C)] = 5\cdot\frac34 = \frac{15}4 < 5$.

To **guarantee** a cover, keep running rounds until every edge is covered: with high probability that takes $O(\log n)$ rounds, and the expected weight is still $O(\log n)\cdot\mathrm{OPT}$ — the same ratio as the greedy algorithm, in expectation, and much worse than deterministic rounding. The same strategy works for weighted **set cover**, with expected weight $O(\log|X|)$ times the optimum (the note writes $O(\log|\mathcal F|)$, with the same swap as in §J.4), and its dual for weighted hitting set, $O(\log|\mathcal F|)$. The note then has an unfinished placeholder — "derandomization: greedy set cover with prices, with a proof via LP duality" — and nothing more.

## J.8 Traveling salesman: the bad news

The **traveling salesman problem** (TSP) asks for the shortest Hamiltonian cycle in a weighted undirected graph; without loss of generality the graph is the complete graph $K_n$, and the input is its $\binom n2$ edge lengths. Recall that deciding whether a graph has a Hamiltonian cycle is NP-hard ([[2-algorithms/12-np-hardness#1211-hamiltonian-cycle|§12.11]], [[concepts/hamiltonian-cycle]]).

**TSP is NP-hard.** Given an arbitrary graph $G$ with $n$ vertices, give the edge $e$ of $K_n$ length $\ell(e) = 1$ if $e$ is an edge of $G$ and $2$ otherwise. If $G$ has a Hamiltonian cycle, that cycle is a tour of length $n$; otherwise every tour uses a non-edge of $G$ and has length at least $n + 1$. The lengths are computed in $O(n^2)$ time, so a polynomial-time TSP algorithm would decide HamCycle. (These lengths satisfy the triangle inequality, $2\le 1 + 1$, so TSP is NP-hard even in the metric case of §J.9.)

**Approximating TSP is NP-hard.** Spread the two values apart. With non-edges of length $n + 1$, a Hamiltonian $G$ gives a tour of length $n$, and otherwise every tour has length at least $(n - 1) + (n + 1) = 2n$. So a polynomial-time algorithm with ratio **less than 2** would decide HamCycle. (The note says this rules out factor 2 itself. It does not: a 2-approximation may return a tour of length exactly $2n$ for a Hamiltonian $G$ — the 5-cycle plus one chord has one, a Hamiltonian path through the chord closed by a non-edge — and $2n$ is also the optimum for the non-Hamiltonian path on 5 vertices. Non-edges of length $n + 2$ settle factor 2.)

> [!key] Theorem J.4
> Let $f(n)\ge 1$ be computable in time polynomial in $n$. If $\textsf{P}\ne\textsf{NP}$, no polynomial-time algorithm approximates the traveling salesman problem on arbitrary weighted graphs within a factor $f(n)$.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> **Reduction** from HamCycle. Given an arbitrary graph $G$ with $n$ vertices, give each edge of $K_n$ length 1 if it is an edge of $G$ and $n\,f(n) + 1$ otherwise. Since $f(n)$ is computed in polynomial time, the number has polynomially many bits and the instance is built in polynomial time.
>
> **⇒.** If $G$ has a Hamiltonian cycle, the optimal tour has length $n$, so an $f(n)$-approximation returns a tour of length at most $n f(n)$.
>
> **⇐.** If $G$ has no Hamiltonian cycle, every tour contains a non-edge, so every tour — including the one returned — has length at least $(n-1) + n f(n) + 1 > n f(n)$.
>
> So comparing the returned length with $n f(n)$ decides HamCycle in polynomial time, which implies $\textsf{P} = \textsf{NP}$. $\square$

## J.9 Traveling salesman: the good news

A common special case can be approximated: the lengths satisfy the **triangle inequality**
$$
\ell(u,w)\ \le\ \ell(u,v) + \ell(v,w)\qquad\text{for all vertices } u, v, w,
$$
as for points in the plane with Euclidean distances. This is **metric TSP**; the lengths in Theorem J.4's proof violate it. The triangle inequality makes **shortcuts** safe: replacing a walk $u\to v_1\to\cdots\to v_k\to w$ by the single edge $u\to w$ never makes it longer.

**Tree doubling.** Compute a minimum spanning tree $T$ ([[2-algorithms/07-minimum-spanning-trees#74-jarníks-prims-algorithm|Jarník's algorithm]] with an array, $O(n^2)$ time on $K_n$; the note quotes $O(n^2\log n)$, also enough), number the vertices in the order a depth-first traversal of $T$ first reaches them (preorder), and return the cycle that visits them in that order.

> [!key] Theorem J.5
> For lengths satisfying the triangle inequality, visiting the vertices in depth-first order of a minimum spanning tree gives a tour of length at most $2\cdot\mathrm{OPT}$.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Let $\mathrm{MST}$ be the length of $T$ and $A$ the length of the returned tour. **Lower bound:** deleting one edge of an optimal tour leaves a Hamiltonian path, which is a spanning tree, so $\mathrm{MST}\le\mathrm{OPT}$ (the note prints $\mathrm{MST}\ge\mathrm{OPT}$). **Upper bound:** the closed walk that follows the depth-first traversal around $T$ uses every tree edge twice, so its length is $2\cdot\mathrm{MST}$; the returned tour is that walk with every repeated vertex skipped, and by the triangle inequality each shortcut is no longer than the part of the walk it replaces. So $A\le 2\cdot\mathrm{MST}\le 2\cdot\mathrm{OPT}$. $\square$

**Christofides' algorithm** (1976) improves the factor to $\frac32$ by adding fewer edges to $T$. The walk around $T$ doubles every edge only to make all degrees even; it is enough to fix the vertices of odd degree.
1. Compute a minimum spanning tree $T$.
2. Let $O$ be the set of vertices of odd degree in $T$; $|O|$ is even (the degrees add up to $2(n-1)$).
3. Compute a **minimum-cost perfect matching** $M$ on $O$: pairs of vertices of $O$, each vertex in exactly one pair, of minimum total length. (The note says this is done with maximum-flow techniques. Flows find matchings only in bipartite graphs; on the complete graph on $O$ the tool is Edmonds' blossom algorithm, in $O(n^3)$ time.)
4. In the multigraph $T\cup M$ (an edge in both appears twice) every degree is even and the graph is connected, so it has an **Euler circuit**, a closed walk using every edge once; find one in $O(n)$ time by a variant of depth-first search ([[2-algorithms/05-basic-graph-algorithms-exercises#exercise-55--euler-tours|Exercise 5.5]]).
5. Return the vertices in the order the Euler circuit first reaches them.

> [!key] Theorem J.6
> For lengths satisfying the triangle inequality, Christofides' algorithm returns a tour of length at most $\frac32\,\mathrm{OPT}$.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Let $\mathrm{MOM}$ be the length of the minimum odd-vertex matching $M$. The Euler circuit has length $\mathrm{MST} + \mathrm{MOM}$, and shortcutting does not lengthen it, so $A\le\mathrm{MST} + \mathrm{MOM}$. As before, $\mathrm{MST}\le\mathrm{OPT}$. For the matching: shortcutting an optimal tour past the vertices not in $O$ gives a cycle through $O$ of length at most $\mathrm{OPT}$. It has an even number of edges; coloring them alternately splits it into two perfect matchings of $O$, the shorter of which has length at most $\mathrm{OPT}/2$, and both have length at least $\mathrm{MOM}$. So $\mathrm{MOM}\le\mathrm{OPT}/2$ and $A\le\frac32\,\mathrm{OPT}$. $\square$

**Both bounds are tight** (examples not in the note). Tree doubling: on $n$ points equally spaced around a cycle (distances measured along the cycle), the minimum spanning tree can be a path; a depth-first traversal from its middle vertex visits one half, jumps back across, and visits the other half, a tour of length $2n - 2$ against $\mathrm{OPT} = n$. Christofides: on a strip of $2k-1$ triangles — a bottom row $b_0,\dots,b_k$ and a top row $t_0,\dots,t_{k-1}$, zigzag edges of length 1, edges along the rows of length $1 + \epsilon$, distances measured in this graph — the minimum spanning tree is the zigzag path of length $2k$, its only odd vertices are its ends $b_0$, $b_k$, at distance $k(1+\epsilon)$, and the tour is $2k + k(1 + \epsilon)$, while going out along one row and back along the other costs $2 + (2k - 1)(1 + \epsilon)$: the ratio tends to $\frac32$.

> [!trap] TSP traps
> - **No triangle inequality, no guarantee**: unless $\textsf{P} = \textsf{NP}$, Theorem J.4 forbids every factor $f(n)$ computable in polynomial time. Tree doubling on a non-metric instance can exceed $2\cdot\mathrm{OPT}$, because a shortcut can be long.
> - **$\mathrm{MST}\le\mathrm{OPT}$**, not the other way: a tour minus one edge is a spanning tree.
> - **Christofides matches only the odd-degree vertices**, with a minimum-cost *perfect* matching on them — a general-graph matching, not a flow problem.

The note (2018) calls Christofides' algorithm the best known for metric TSP; in 2020 Karlin, Klein and Oveis Gharan improved the factor to $\frac32 - 10^{-36}$. For **graphic** TSP — shortest-path distances in an unweighted graph — the best factor known is $\frac75$ (Sebő and Vygen).

## J.10 k-center clustering

> [!definition] Definition — $k$-center clustering
> Given a set $P = \{p_1,\dots,p_n\}$ of points in the plane (or in any metric space) and an integer $k$, choose $k$ **centers** $C = \{c_1,\dots,c_k\}$ minimizing
> $$
> \textit{cost}(C) = \max_i\,\min_j\,|p_ic_j|,
> $$
> the largest distance from a point to its nearest center. Each point belongs to the **cluster** of its nearest center; the cost is the largest cluster radius — the smallest $r$ such that $k$ disks of radius $r$ cover $P$.

The problem is NP-hard, in the plane even to approximate within a factor of about 1.8. **Gonzalez's** greedy algorithm (1985) gets within 2: start with any input point as the first center, and repeatedly make the point farthest from all current centers the next one. Below, $d_i$ is the distance from $p_i$ to its nearest center so far and $r_j$ the cluster radius after $j$ centers; the last iteration computes $r_k$ and one extra center $c_{k+1}$.

```text
GonzalezKCenter(P, k):
    for i ← 1 to n
        d_i ← ∞
    c_1 ← p_1
    for j ← 1 to k
        r_j ← 0
        for i ← 1 to n
            d_i ← min{d_i, |p_i c_j|}
            if r_j < d_i
                r_j ← d_i;  c_{j+1} ← p_i
    return {c_1, c_2, ..., c_k}
```

It runs in $O(nk)$ time; Feder and Greene compute the same clustering in $O(n\log k)$ time for points in $\mathbb R^d$.

> [!key] Theorem J.7
> GonzalezKCenter computes a 2-approximation of the optimal $k$-center clustering: $r_k\le 2\cdot\mathrm{OPT}$.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> The cost of $\{c_1,\dots,c_k\}$ is $r_k$, the distance from $c_{k+1}$ to its nearest center. Each $c_j$ was chosen at distance $r_{j-1}$ from its nearest earlier center, hence at least $r_{j-1}$ from every earlier center, and the radii never increase ($r_1\ge r_2\ge\cdots\ge r_k$: adding a center can only shorten distances), so the $k+1$ points $c_1,\dots,c_{k+1}$ are pairwise at distance at least $r_k$. The optimal clustering has only $k$ clusters, so two of these points, $c_i$ and $c_j$, lie in the same optimal cluster, within $\mathrm{OPT}$ of its center; by the triangle inequality $r_k\le|c_ic_j|\le 2\cdot\mathrm{OPT}$. $\square$

The proof uses only the triangle inequality, so the theorem holds in every metric space — and, like the TSP bound, it compares with a quantity that every solution must respect: $k+1$ points pairwise far apart cannot be covered by $k$ small disks.

## J.11 Approximation schemes

This section is ♥ in the note. An **approximation scheme** takes an instance and a number $\varepsilon > 0$ and returns a $(1+\varepsilon)$-approximation. For $k$-center in the plane a factor of 1.8 is already NP-hard, so unless $\textsf{P} = \textsf{NP}$ a scheme cannot run in polynomial time; but for small $k$ it can still beat every exact algorithm. Three phases:
1. Run GonzalezKCenter: its cost $r$ satisfies $r^*\le r\le 2r^*$, where $r^*$ is the optimal cost. $O(nk)$ time.
2. Lay a grid of square cells of side $\delta = \varepsilon r/(2\sqrt2)$ over the plane, and let $Q\subseteq P$ contain one point of each non-empty cell (hash the rounded coordinates $(\lfloor x/\delta\rfloor, \lfloor y/\delta\rfloor)$: $O(n)$ time, or $O(n\log n)$ by sorting). Each of the $k$ optimal disks of radius $r^*$ meets $O((r^*/\delta)^2) = O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ cells (the note prints $O(r^*/\delta^2)$), so $|Q| = O(k/\varepsilon^2)$.
3. Compute an **optimal** $k$-center clustering of $Q$ and return its centers.

**Quality.** The optimal radius $\tilde r$ for $Q$ is at most $r^*$, since the optimal disks for $P$ also cover $Q$. Every point of $P$ shares a cell with a point of $Q$ and is within the cell's diagonal $\delta\sqrt2 = \varepsilon r/2$ of it, so growing $Q$'s disks by $\delta\sqrt2$ covers $P$: the cost is at most $\tilde r + \varepsilon r/2\le r^* + \varepsilon r^* = (1+\varepsilon)\,r^*$.

**Time.** With the fastest exact algorithm known, $n^{O(\sqrt k)}$ time (Hwang, Lee and Chang), phase 3 takes $(k/\varepsilon^2)^{O(\sqrt k)}$, for a total of $O(nk + (k/\varepsilon^2)^{O(\sqrt k)})$: linear in $n$ when $k$ and $\varepsilon$ are constants, but exponential in the input size when $k$ grows (say $k = \sqrt n$).

## J.12 An FPTAS for subset sum

This section is ♥ in the note.

> [!definition] Definition — PTAS, FPTAS
> An approximation scheme is a **polynomial-time approximation scheme (PTAS)** if for every fixed $\varepsilon > 0$ its running time is polynomial in $n$; it is a **fully polynomial-time approximation scheme (FPTAS)** if the running time is polynomial in both $n$ and $1/\varepsilon$. Running time $O(n^2/\varepsilon^2)$: an FPTAS. $O(n^{1/\varepsilon^6})$: a PTAS, not an FPTAS. The $k$-center scheme of §J.11: not even a PTAS (exponential in $\sqrt k$, and $k$ is part of the input).

**SubsetSum** — is there a subset of the positive integers $X[1\,..\,n]$ that sums to $t$? — is NP-hard ([[2-algorithms/12-np-hardness#1212-subset-sum-from-vertex-cover|§12.12]]), and a yes/no answer cannot be approximated. The optimization version can: find the subset with the **largest sum that is at most $t$**. The dynamic program of [[2-algorithms/03-dynamic-programming#38-subset-sum|§3.8]] solves it in $O(nt)$ time, pseudo-polynomial. A different exact algorithm keeps the set $S_i$ of all subset sums of the first $i$ numbers that are at most $t$:

```text
SubsetSum(X[1 .. n], t):
    S_0 ← {0}
    for i ← 1 to n
        S_i ← S_{i−1} ∪ (S_{i−1} + X[i])       ⟨⟨S + x = {s + x : s ∈ S}⟩⟩
        remove all elements of S_i bigger than t
    return max S_n
```

With each $S_i$ in a sorted array, iteration $i$ is a merge in $O(|S_{i-1}|)$ time. Since $|S_i|\le 2^i$ and $|S_i|\le t+1$, the total is $O(\min\{2^n, nt\})$ — and less when many subsets share a sum. The FPTAS keeps the sets small by **merging nearly equal sums**: sort $X$ first, and after each step keep a sum only if it exceeds the last kept one by a factor more than $1 + \delta$.

```text
Filter(Z[1 .. k], δ):
    Sort(Z)
    j ← 1
    Y[1] ← Z[1]
    for i ← 2 to k
        if Z[i] > (1 + δ) · Y[j]               ⟨⟨keep only clear increases⟩⟩
            j ← j + 1
            Y[j] ← Z[i]
    return Y[1 .. j]
```

```text
ApproxSubsetSum(X[1 .. n], t, ε):
    Sort(X)                                    ⟨⟨increasing order⟩⟩
    R_0 ← {0}
    for i ← 1 to n
        R_i ← R_{i−1} ∪ (R_{i−1} + X[i])
        R_i ← Filter(R_i, ε/2n)
        remove all elements of R_i bigger than t
    return max R_n
```

(The note initializes $Y[j]\leftarrow Z[i]$ before $i$ is defined, and names ApproxSubsetSum's second parameter $k$ although the body uses $t$.)

> [!key] Theorem J.8
> For $0 < \varepsilon\le 1$, ApproxSubsetSum returns a $(1+\varepsilon)$-approximation of the optimal subset sum: $r^*\le s^*\le(1+\varepsilon)\,r^*$, where $s^* = \max S_n$ and $r^* = \max R_n$. In particular $r^*\ge s^*/(1+\varepsilon)\ge(1-\varepsilon)\,s^*$.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Let $\delta = \varepsilon/2n$ and $x_i$ the $i$-th smallest number. **Claim** (by induction on $i$): for every $s\in S_i$ there is an $r\in R_i$ with $r\le s\le r(1+\delta)^i$. (The note's boxed claim has $(1 + \varepsilon n/2)^i$; the proof and its use need $(1 + \varepsilon/2n)^i$.) For $i = 0$ both sets are $\{0\}$. Let $s\in S_i$.
>
> *Case 1, $s\in S_{i-1}$.* By induction some $r'\in R_{i-1}$ has $r'\le s\le r'(1+\delta)^{i-1}$. If $r'$ survives into $R_i$, done. Otherwise Filter dropped it because of a kept $r$ with $r\le r'\le r(1+\delta)$ (and $r\le t$), so $r\le s\le r(1+\delta)^{i-1}(1+\delta)$.
>
> *Case 2, $s\in S_{i-1} + x_i$.* By induction some $r'\in R_{i-1}$ has $r'\le s - x_i\le r'(1+\delta)^{i-1}$. If $r' + x_i$ survives, done (it is at most $s\le t$). Otherwise a kept $r$ has $r\le r' + x_i\le r(1+\delta)$, so $r'\le r(1+\delta) - x_i$ and
> $$
> s\ \le\ r'(1+\delta)^{i-1} + x_i\ \le\ r(1+\delta)^i - x_i\bigl((1+\delta)^{i-1} - 1\bigr)\ \le\ r(1+\delta)^i.
> $$
> (The note's chain passes through $(r - x_i)(1+\delta)^i + x_i$, which does not follow from $r' + x_i\le r(1+\delta)$: for $\delta = \frac1{10}$, $i = 2$, $x_i = 5$, $r = 20$, $r' = 17$, the left side is $23.7$ and that bound $23.15$. The chain above repairs it.)
>
> Now $R_n\subseteq S_n$ gives $r^*\le s^*$, and the claim gives a kept $r\le r^*$ with $s^*\le r(1+\varepsilon/2n)^n\le r^*e^{\varepsilon/2}\le(1+\varepsilon)\,r^*$, using $1 + x\le e^x$ and $e^x\le 1 + 2x$ for $0\le x\le 1$. $\square$

> [!key] Theorem J.9
> ApproxSubsetSum runs in $O((n^3\log n)/\varepsilon)$ time.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Each iteration merges, filters and truncates sorted arrays in $O(|R_{i-1}|)$ time, so the total is $O(\sum_i|R_i|)$. Because $X$ is sorted increasingly, the elements of $R_{i-1} + x_i$ lie between $x_i$ and $i\cdot x_i$. After Filter, consecutive kept sums differ by a factor more than $1+\delta$; so if $p\ge 2$ elements of $R_{i-1} + x_i$ survive, the largest is more than $(1+\delta)^{p-1}$ times the smallest, and both lie in $[x_i, i\,x_i]$, so $(1+\delta)^{p-1} < i$. Hence at most $\lfloor\log_{1+\delta} i\rfloor + 1$ elements of $R_{i-1} + x_i$ survive. With $\ln(1+\delta)\ge\delta/2$ for $\delta\le 1$,
> $$
> |R_i|\ \le\ |R_{i-1}| + \log_{1+\delta} n + 1\ \le\ |R_{i-1}| + \frac{2\ln n}{\delta} + 1\ =\ |R_{i-1}| + \frac{4n\ln n}{\varepsilon} + 1,
> $$
> so $|R_i| = O((n^2\log n)/\varepsilon)$ and the total time is $O((n^3\log n)/\varepsilon)$. $\square$
>
> (The note counts $\lceil\log_{1+\delta} i\rceil$ survivors, which is 0 for $i = 1$, where one survives; its reason, one survivor per interval $[(1+\delta)^k, (1+\delta)^{k+1})$, alone would allow $\lfloor\log_{1+\delta} i\rfloor + 2$, since $[x_i, i\,x_i]$ can meet that many intervals. It also writes "=" where "≤" is meant, and simplifies $2\ln n/\delta$ to $n\ln n/\varepsilon$ instead of $4n\ln n/\varepsilon$. The bound $O((n^3\log n)/\varepsilon)$ is unaffected.)

So subset sum, NP-hard and only weakly so, has an FPTAS: polynomial in $n$ and $1/\varepsilon$, independent of the size of the numbers.

## Summary

- An $\alpha$-approximation algorithm runs in polynomial time and is within a factor $\alpha$ of the optimum on **every** input. The proof compares the algorithm with a lower bound on $\mathrm{OPT}$ (for minimization) that every solution must respect.
- Load balancing: greedy $\le 2\,\mathrm{OPT}$ (largest job, average load); sorted greedy $\le\frac32\,\mathrm{OPT}$ (two of the $m+1$ largest jobs share a machine). Exact ratios $2 - \frac1m$ and $\frac43 - \frac1{3m}$.
- Vertex cover: max-degree greedy $O(\log n)$ — more precisely at most $H(\Delta)\cdot\mathrm{OPT}$; both ends of a maximal matching, 2 (every cover needs one end of each matching edge); weighted, LP rounding at $\frac12$, 2 ($\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}\le\mathrm{OPT}$, $x'\le 2x^*$); randomized rounding, $O(\log n)$ in expectation.
- Greedy set cover: $H(\max|S|)\le 1 + \ln|X|$ — logarithmic in the ground set, not in $|\mathcal F|$; greedy hitting set: $1 + \ln|\mathcal F|$. Hitting sets are set covers of the dual system.
- General TSP: no polynomial-time $f(n)$-approximation, for any $f$ computable in polynomial time, unless $\textsf{P} = \textsf{NP}$ (non-edges of length $nf(n) + 1$). Metric TSP: tree doubling 2 ($\mathrm{MST}\le\mathrm{OPT}$, walk $= 2\,\mathrm{MST}$, shortcuts); Christofides $\frac32$ (plus a matching of the odd vertices, $\mathrm{MOM}\le\mathrm{OPT}/2$).
- $k$-center: Gonzalez's farthest-point greedy, $O(nk)$ time, ratio 2 ($k+1$ centers pairwise $\ge r_k$ apart).
- PTAS: polynomial for each fixed $\varepsilon$; FPTAS: polynomial in $n$ and $1/\varepsilon$. Subset sum: keep all subset sums, merging those within a factor $1 + \varepsilon/2n$: $(1+\varepsilon)$-approximation in $O((n^3\log n)/\varepsilon)$ time.
- The note ends with 18 exercises (load balancing, vertex cover heuristics, stabbing, routing, packing, partition, coloring, knapsack, bin packing, max $k$-cut, Hamiltonian paths, scheduling, nearest-neighbor TSP).
