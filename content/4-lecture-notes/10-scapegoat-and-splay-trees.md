> [!abstract] In one breath
> A binary search tree answers queries in time proportional to its height, so it must be kept balanced; this note keeps it balanced **lazily** and pays for the work by [[concepts/amortized-analysis|amortization]]. **Global rebuilding** handles deletions: mark deleted nodes, and rebuild the whole tree once half of it is marked. **Partial rebuilding** handles insertions: when a subtree's height exceeds $\alpha\lg$ of its size, rebuild that subtree perfectly balanced — Lemma 1 shows such a subtree has absorbed a constant fraction of its size in insertions since it was last built. **Scapegoat trees** combine the two: $O(\log n)$ worst-case searches and $O(\log n)$ amortized updates with $O(1)$ extra space. **Splay trees** instead move every accessed node to the root by **rotations** in pairs (zig-zags and roller-coasters); with the potential $\Phi = \sum_v\lg\text{size}(v)$, the **Access Lemma** bounds the amortized cost of a splay by $1 + 3\lg n$, so every operation takes $O(\log n)$ amortized time. With weights the same lemma gives static optimality and finger properties; whether splay trees are dynamically optimal is open.

## 10.1 Definitions

(The note opens with an editorial remark to move this introduction elsewhere.) Standard binary-search-tree vocabulary and the usual search, insertion and deletion algorithms are assumed. The note works with full binary trees whose internal nodes hold the keys and whose leaves are null pointers. The **depth** of a node is its distance from the root, its **height** the distance to the farthest leaf below it, and its **size** the number of nodes in its subtree.

> [!definition] Convention on this page
> $\text{size}(v)$ = the number of keys in $v$'s subtree; $\text{height}(v)$ = the number of edges on the longest downward path from $v$ to a key (a single key has height 0, an empty tree $-1$). A search for a key visits at most $\text{height} + 1$ nodes. (Counting the null leaves as nodes instead — size $2k + 1$ and one more level — changes only constants below, not the arguments.)

A tree of height $h$ has at most $2^{h+1} - 1$ keys, so a tree with $n$ keys has height at least $\lfloor\lg n\rfloor$, that is, at least $\lceil\lg(n+1)\rceil$ levels. A **perfectly balanced** tree attains this: every subtree splits its remaining keys as evenly as possible (both halves have at most $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ keys), so searches take $O(\log n)$ time. But a malicious sequence of insertions and deletions can turn any tree into a path, with $\Theta(n)$-time searches; balanced trees prevent this by periodic restructuring. AVL trees, red-black trees, B-trees, weight-balanced trees, treaps, skip lists and many others guarantee $O(\log n)$ time per operation — some in the worst case, some amortized, some expected. This note presents three with **amortized** guarantees: lazy weight-balanced trees (Overmars, early 1980s) and scapegoat trees (Andersson 1989; Galperin and Rivest 1993, independently), which rebuild, and splay trees (Sleator and Tarjan), which rotate.

## 10.2 Lazy deletions: global rebuilding

Start from a perfectly balanced tree and allow only searches and deletions. To delete a key, find its node and **mark** it instead of removing it. Searches still use marked nodes to navigate but report a marked key as absent. The tree stays balanced, but its height now depends on how many keys were *ever* stored, so add one rule:

> [!key] Global rebuilding rule
> As soon as half the nodes of the tree are marked, rebuild a perfectly balanced tree containing only the unmarked keys.

**Searches: $O(\log n)$ worst case.** Let $n$ be the number of unmarked keys. Fewer than half of the $N$ nodes are marked, so $N < 2n$, and a search in the perfectly balanced tree on $N$ nodes visits at most $\lfloor\lg N\rfloor + 1\le\lceil\lg n\rceil + 1$ nodes. (The note says at most $\lg n + 1$ keys, which is slightly too small: five nodes, two of them marked, leave $n = 3$ unmarked keys in a tree of 3 levels, and $3 > \lg 3 + 1\approx 2.58$.)

**Deletions: $O(\log n)$ amortized.** A tree can be rebuilt perfectly balanced in time linear in its size (how is one of the note's exercises). When a rebuild happens, at least half of the $N$ nodes of the old tree are marked: that is $m\ge N/2$ deletions since the previous rebuild, and both the old tree ($N\le 2m$ nodes) and the new one (at most $m$ keys) are paid for by a tax of \$3 per deletion. So rebuilding costs $O(1)$ amortized per deletion, on top of the $O(\log n)$ search for the node to mark.

## 10.3 Insertions: partial rebuilding

Now allow only searches and insertions. A new key cannot be "half-inserted" — it must be findable — so instead we insert normally and **rebuild subtrees that have become too unbalanced**. Fix a constant $\alpha > 1$, and let every node store its height and size.

> [!key] Partial rebuilding rule
> After inserting a node, walk back up the search path updating heights and sizes. At a node $v$ with
> $$
> \text{height}(v) > \alpha\lg\text{size}(v),
> $$
> rebuild $v$'s subtree into a perfectly balanced tree, in $O(\text{size}(v))$ time.

With this rule every node satisfies $\text{height}(v)\le\alpha\lg\text{size}(v)$ after every insertion; in particular the tree has height at most $\alpha\lg n$, so **searches take $O(\log n)$ worst-case time**. An insertion may rebuild the whole tree, $\Theta(n)$ time. At most one subtree is rebuilt per insertion, as the note states: rebuilding the lowest violating node repairs all its ancestors. The amortized analysis rests on the **imbalance**
$$
\text{Imbal}(v) = \bigl\lvert\text{size}(\text{left}(v)) - \text{size}(\text{right}(v))\bigr\rvert.
$$
In a perfectly balanced tree $\text{Imbal}(v)\le 1$ at every node, so right after $v$'s subtree is (re)built $\text{Imbal}(v)\le 1$; and each insertion into $v$'s subtree changes $\text{Imbal}(v)$ by exactly 1.

> [!key] Lemma 1 (with its constant)
> Just before $v$'s subtree is rebuilt, $\text{Imbal}(v) > c\cdot\text{size}(v)$, where $c = 2^{1-1/\alpha} - 1 > 0$. (For $\alpha = 3/2$, $c\approx 0.26$; for $\alpha = 2$, $c\approx 0.41$.)

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Say the new key went into $v$'s left subtree $L$, and let $R$ be the right subtree and $s = \text{size}(v) = \text{size}(L) + \text{size}(R) + 1$. Before the insertion $v$ satisfied the rule, $\text{height}(v)\le\alpha\lg(s - 1) < \alpha\lg s$; now it violates it, so its height grew, and it grew through $L$: $\text{height}(v) = \text{height}(L) + 1$. The subtree $L$ satisfies the rule — it never violated it, or it was just rebuilt (only the lowest violator is rebuilt, and $v$ is the first one met). Hence
> $$
> \alpha\lg s < \text{height}(v) = \text{height}(L) + 1\le\alpha\lg\text{size}(L) + 1,
> $$
> so $\lg s - \lg\text{size}(L) < 1/\alpha$, that is, $\text{size}(L) > s/2^{1/\alpha}$, and therefore $\text{size}(R) = s - 1 - \text{size}(L) < (1 - 2^{-1/\alpha})\,s - 1$. Subtracting,
> $$
> \text{Imbal}(v)\ge\text{size}(L) - \text{size}(R) > \bigl(2\cdot 2^{-1/\alpha} - 1\bigr)s + 1 = c\,s + 1.
> $$
> Since $\alpha > 1$, $2^{1-1/\alpha} > 1$ and $c > 0$. $\square$
>
> (The note writes the last step as $\text{Imbal}(v)\ge\text{size}(L) - \text{size}(R) - 1 > (2/2^{1/\alpha} - 1)\,s$, which is also true. Its proof does not say why $\text{height}(v)\le\text{height}(L) + 1$; the reason is the first two sentences above.)

**Amortized $O(\log n)$ insertions.** Since $\text{Imbal}(v)$ was at most 1 when $v$'s subtree was last built and moves by 1 per insertion, more than $c\cdot\text{size}(v)$ keys have been inserted into $v$'s subtree since then. Spread the rebuilding cost $\text{size}(v)$ over those insertions: each pays less than $1/c$. A new key lies in the subtrees of at most $\alpha\lg n + 1$ nodes, so it pays $O(\log n)$ in all; precisely, the total rebuilding work of any sequence of insertions is at most $1/c$ times the sum of the depths of the inserted keys.

## 10.4 Scapegoat (lazy height-balanced) trees

**Scapegoat trees** support all three operations by combining the two techniques: partial rebuilding after insertions and global rebuilding after deletions. Searches take $O(\log n)$ worst-case time, insertions and deletions $O(\log n)$ amortized. The note leaves out "a few small technical details"; one consistent choice is: marked nodes stay in place and count in the sizes, partial rebuilds keep them, and only a global rebuild — when half of all nodes are marked — removes them. Then $N < 2n$ nodes hold $n$ keys, and a search visits at most $\alpha\lg N + 1 < \alpha(\lg n + 1) + 1$ nodes.

**$O(1)$ extra space.** Once the analysis is done, the structure can be simplified: at most one subtree, the **scapegoat**, is rebuilt per insertion, and instead of storing a size and a height at every node it suffices to keep three integers — the size of the whole tree, its height, and the number of marked nodes. When an insertion unbalances the tree, compute the sizes of the nodes on the search path from the new leaf upward, each from its child's size plus a traversal of the sibling subtree, stopping at the scapegoat. These traversals are disjoint and lie inside the scapegoat's subtree, so they cost exactly its size — the same as rebuilding it. Unlike almost all other balanced trees, scapegoat trees therefore need only $O(1)$ extra space.

## 10.5 Rotations, double rotations, and splaying

A **rotation** at a node $x$ with parent $y$ makes $x$ the parent of $y$, moving $x$ up one level and $y$ down one, and keeps the in-order sequence. It changes $O(1)$ pointers.

Rotations are used in pairs. A **zig-zag** at $x$ — when $x$ and its parent are children on opposite sides — is two rotations at $x$ in opposite directions. A **roller-coaster** (usually called **zig-zig**) at $x$ — both on the same side — is a rotation at $x$'s parent followed by a rotation at $x$, both in the same direction. Either one moves $x$ up two levels and leaves its parent's depth unchanged; the grandparent moves down one level in a zig-zag and two in a roller-coaster.

To **splay** a node $x$, apply double rotations at $x$ until $x$ is the root or a child of the root, and then, if needed, one single rotation. Splaying takes time proportional to the depth of $x$ before the splay: exactly $\text{depth}(x)$ rotations.

## 10.6 Splay trees

A **splay tree** is a binary search tree that splays every node it touches:

- **Search**: find the key by the usual algorithm — or, if it is absent, its predecessor or successor, the last node visited — and splay that node.
- **Insert**: insert a new node by the usual algorithm and splay it.
- **Delete**: find and splay the node $x$, and remove it; this leaves a left subtree $L$ (keys smaller than $x$) and a right subtree $R$. Find the largest key $w$ of $L$ (the predecessor of $x$), splay it within $L$ — now it has no right child — and make $R$ its right subtree.

Each operation walks down to a constant number of nodes and splays them, and each walk is as long as the splay that follows it (one more node than rotations). So it suffices to bound the amortized number of rotations of a splay.

**The potential.** Define the **rank** $r(v) = \lg\text{size}(v)$ and $\Phi = \sum_v r(v)$. The amortized cost of a rotation step is the number of rotations **plus the increase** $\Phi' - \Phi$. A perfectly balanced tree has $\Phi = \Theta(n)$; a path has $\Phi = \lg n! = \Theta(n\log n)$. (The note's §10.6 uses $\lfloor\lg\text{size}(v)\rfloor$ and says "plus the drop in potential"; see the trap below.)

> [!key] The Access Lemma
> Let $r$ and $r'$ be ranks just before and after one step of a splay at $x$. A single rotation has amortized cost at most $1 + 3\bigl(r'(x) - r(x)\bigr)$; a double rotation has amortized cost at most $3\bigl(r'(x) - r(x)\bigr)$.

Adding up the steps of a splay, the intermediate ranks of $x$ cancel, and at most one step is single:
$$
\text{amortized cost of splaying } x\;\le\; 1 + 3\bigl(r(\text{root}) - r(x)\bigr)\;\le\; 1 + 3\lg n .
$$
(The note concludes "at most $3\lg n - 1$"; the sum gives $1 + 3\lg n$ — for a one-node tree the splay costs $0 > 3\lg 1 - 1$.) The proof of the lemma is in §10.7, for weighted sizes.

**From splays to operations.** Inserting a leaf also raises the potential: the sizes along its search path grow by one, which adds at most $\lg n$ to $\Phi$. Deleting removes the root's rank, and attaching $R$ under $w$ raises $w$'s rank by at most $\lg n$. Counting this way: a search costs at most $1 + 3\lg n$, an insertion at most $1 + 4\lg n$, a deletion at most $2 + 6\lg n$ rotations, amortized. Starting from an empty tree, $\Phi_0 = 0\le\Phi$, so **any sequence of $m$ operations on a splay tree of at most $n$ keys takes $O(m\log n)$ time** — even though a single operation can take $\Theta(n)$ (search for the smallest key after inserting the keys in increasing order).

> [!example] Example — the note's Figure 4
> In the figure, $x$ starts at depth 7, and the splay takes 7 rotations: a roller-coaster, two zig-zags and a single rotation. The old search path $b, d, m, k, f, h, j$ goes from depths $0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$ to $1, 2, 1, 2, 3, 5, 4$. The potential $\sum_v\lg\text{size}(v)$ of the 15-node tree drops from 20.95 to 19.40, so the amortized cost is $7 - 1.55 = 5.45$, below the Access Lemma's bound $1 + 3(\lg 15 - \lg 1) = 12.72$.

> [!trap] Ranks must not be rounded down
> With the note's §10.6 ranks $\lfloor\lg\text{size}(v)\rfloor$ and a cost of 2 per double rotation, the Access Lemma is **false**: a roller-coaster in which the subtrees of $x, y, z$ have sizes $(8, 9, 13)$ before and $(13, 8, 4)$ after costs 2 rotations but lowers $\sum\lfloor\lg\text{size}\rfloor$ by only 1 (from $3 + 3 + 3$ to $3 + 3 + 2$), an amortized cost of 1 against the bound $3(\lfloor\lg 13\rfloor - \lfloor\lg 8\rfloor) = 0$; whole splays can exceed $1 + 3(\lfloor\lg n\rfloor - \text{rank}(x))$ too. Use real ranks $\lg\text{size}(v)$ (as §10.7 and Sleator and Tarjan do). The amortized cost is the actual cost **plus the increase** $\Phi' - \Phi$, not plus the drop.

## 10.7 Other optimality properties

(★ in the note: optional.) Give every node an arbitrary **positive** weight $w(v)$, used only in the analysis — the algorithm does not change. Redefine $s(v) = w(v) + s(\text{left}(v)) + s(\text{right}(v))$, the total weight of $v$'s subtree, $r(v) = \lg s(v)$ and $\Phi = \sum_v r(v)$; unit weights give §10.6. (The note allows non-negative weights, but a zero-weight leaf would have rank $\lg 0 = -\infty$.)

> [!key] Generalized Access Lemma
> For any positive weights, a single rotation at $x$ has amortized cost at most $1 + 3\bigl(r'(x) - r(x)\bigr)$ and a double rotation at most $3\bigl(r'(x) - r(x)\bigr)$. Hence splaying $x$ costs at most $1 + 3\bigl(r(\text{root}) - r(x)\bigr)$ amortized.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand; Sleator and Tarjan's)
> Two facts are used throughout: after a step $x$ is the root of the subtree that its old ancestor ($y$ or $z$) used to root, so $r'(x)$ equals that ancestor's old rank and $r'(x)\ge r(x)$; and for $a, b > 0$, concavity of $\lg$ gives $\lg a + \lg b\le 2\lg\frac{a+b}{2}$.
>
> **Single rotation** ($x$ with parent $y$; only $x$ and $y$ change rank, and $r'(y)\le r(y)$):
> $$
> 1 + \Phi' - \Phi = 1 + r'(x) + r'(y) - r(x) - r(y)\le 1 + r'(x) - r(x)\le 1 + 3\bigl(r'(x) - r(x)\bigr).
> $$
> **Zig-zag** ($x$, parent $w$, grandparent $z$; only these change rank; $r(w)\ge r(x)$, $r'(x) = r(z)$, $s'(w) + s'(z)\le s'(x)$):
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> 2 + \Phi' - \Phi &= 2 + r'(w) + r'(x) + r'(z) - r(w) - r(x) - r(z)\\
> &\le 2 + r'(w) + r'(z) - 2r(x)\\
> &= 2 + \lg\frac{s'(w)}{s'(x)} + \lg\frac{s'(z)}{s'(x)} + 2\bigl(r'(x) - r(x)\bigr)\\
> &\le 2 + 2\lg\tfrac12 + 2\bigl(r'(x) - r(x)\bigr) = 2\bigl(r'(x) - r(x)\bigr)\le 3\bigl(r'(x) - r(x)\bigr).
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> **Roller-coaster** ($x$, parent $y$, grandparent $z$; only these change rank; $r(y)\ge r(x)$, $r'(y)\le r'(x) = r(z)$, $s(x) + s'(z)\le s'(x)$):
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> 2 + \Phi' - \Phi &= 2 + r'(x) + r'(y) + r'(z) - r(x) - r(y) - r(z)\\
> &\le 2 + r'(x) + r'(z) - 2r(x)\\
> &= 2 + \lg\frac{s(x)}{s'(x)} + \lg\frac{s'(z)}{s'(x)} + 3\bigl(r'(x) - r(x)\bigr)\\
> &\le 2 + 2\lg\tfrac12 + 3\bigl(r'(x) - r(x)\bigr) = 3\bigl(r'(x) - r(x)\bigr). \qquad\square
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> (Two slips in the note's version, p. 7: in the zig-zag its second line keeps an extra $r'(x)$ — "$2 + r'(w) + r'(x) + r'(z) - 2r(x)$" — which does not equal the next line; and in the roller-coaster the bracket justifies the second line by "$r(x)\ge r(y)$", which is backwards: $y$ is $x$'s parent, so $r(y)\ge r(x)$, and that is what the step needs.)

The bound depends on the weights only through ranks, so any choice of weights gives a valid bound for the *same* algorithm. With total weight $W$, splaying $x$ costs at most $1 + 3\lg(W/w(x))$ amortized, and over a whole sequence the potential can drop by at most $\sum_x\lg(W/w(x))$, which must be added to turn amortized into actual totals.

> [!key] Static optimality theorem
> If each node $x$ is accessed $t(x)\ge 1$ times and $T = \sum_x t(x)$, an access to $x$ costs $O\bigl(1 + \log(T/t(x))\bigr)$ amortized. (Weights $w(x) = t(x)$. The note writes $O(\log T - \log t(x))$; as for the static finger bound below, the constant term is needed.)

So the total $O\bigl(T + \sum_x t(x)\log(T/t(x))\bigr)$ is, by the note's account, within a constant factor of the best *static* search tree for those frequencies — the optimal binary search tree that dynamic programming computes in [[2-algorithms/03-dynamic-programming#39-optimal-binary-search-trees|§3.9]] — although the splay tree never learns the frequencies: frequently accessed keys drift toward the root.

> [!key] Static finger theorem
> For any fixed node $f$, an access to $x$ costs $O\bigl(1 + \log\text{dist}(f, x)\bigr)$ amortized, where $\text{dist}(f, x)$ is the number of keys between $f$ and $x$, both included.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Set $w(x) = 1/\text{dist}(f, x)^2$. Each distance $d\ge 2$ occurs at most twice (left and right of $f$) and $d = 1$ once, so $W\le\sum_{d\ge 1}2/d^2 = \pi^2/3$, and $r(x)\ge\lg w(x) = -2\lg\text{dist}(f, x)$. The splay bound $1 + 3\bigl(\lg W - r(x)\bigr)\le 1 + 3\lg(\pi^2/3) + 6\lg\text{dist}(f, x)$. $\square$ (The note writes $O(\lg\text{dist}(f, x))$, which is 0 for $x = f$; the constant term is needed.)

Stated without proof, with much harder proofs (each bound up to an additive constant, as above):

- **Working set theorem** (Sleator and Tarjan). An access to $x$ costs $O(\log D)$ amortized, where $D$ is the number of distinct keys accessed since the last access to $x$ ($D = n$ at the first access) — recently used keys are cheap.
- **Scanning theorem** (Tarjan). Splaying all nodes in increasing order, from any initial tree, takes $O(n)$ rotations in total.
- **Dynamic finger theorem** (Cole, Mishra, Schmidt and Siegel; Cole). Right after accessing $y$, an access to $x$ costs $O(\log\text{dist}(x, y))$ amortized — nearby keys are cheap.

## 10.8 Splay tree conjectures

(★ in the note: optional; the note says it follows Erik Demaine's lecture notes.)

- **Deque conjecture.** Use the splay tree as a double-ended queue: two fingers start at the two ends, and each step moves one of them one key left or right. Conjecture: $m$ such steps on an $n$-node tree cost $O(m + n)$. Sundar proved $O\bigl((m+n)\,\alpha(m+n)\bigr)$ (with $\alpha$ the inverse Ackermann function of [[concepts/union-find]]), and Pettie improved it to $O\bigl((m+n)\,\alpha^*(m+n)\bigr)$.
- **Traversal conjecture.** Accessing the keys in the preorder of any other binary search tree on the same keys costs $O(n)$ — a generalization of the scanning theorem.
- **Unified conjecture.** An access to $x$ costs $O\bigl(\log\min_y(D(y) + \text{dist}(x, y))\bigr)$, where $D(y)$ is the number of distinct keys accessed since the last access to $y$; it would imply both the working set and the dynamic finger theorems, and other structures are known to achieve it.
- **Dynamic optimality conjecture** — the most important one, and one of the main open problems on data structures: on every access sequence, splay trees are within a constant factor of the best binary search tree that knows the whole sequence in advance and may perform arbitrary rotations (cost = comparisons + rotations). No dynamically optimal binary search tree is known at all, even offline; the best known are $O(\log\log n)$-competitive (Tango trees, multi-splay trees, chain-splay trees), and a geometric reformulation of binary-search-tree executions as point sets is a promising approach.

## Summary

- **Global rebuilding:** mark deleted keys; rebuild when half the nodes are marked. Searches $O(\log n)$ worst case (at most $\lceil\lg n\rceil + 1$ nodes), deletions $O(1)$ amortized rebuilding cost.
- **Partial rebuilding** ($\alpha > 1$): after an insertion, rebuild the lowest node with $\text{height}(v) > \alpha\lg\text{size}(v)$. Height stays $\le\alpha\lg n$; Lemma 1: then $\text{Imbal}(v) > (2^{1-1/\alpha} - 1)\,\text{size}(v)$, so a constant fraction of the subtree was inserted since its last rebuild; insertions $O(\log n)$ amortized.
- **Scapegoat trees** = both: $O(\log n)$ worst-case search, $O(\log n)$ amortized update, one rebuild per insertion, $O(1)$ extra space.
- **Rotations** keep the in-order sequence; a **zig-zag** (opposite sides) and a **roller-coaster/zig-zig** (same side, parent first) lift $x$ by two levels. **Splaying** lifts $x$ to the root in $\text{depth}(x)$ rotations.
- **Splay trees** splay every accessed node. With $\Phi = \sum_v\lg\text{size}(v)$, the Access Lemma (single: $\le 1 + 3\Delta r(x)$; double: $\le 3\Delta r(x)$) gives $\le 1 + 3\lg n$ amortized per splay and $O(\log n)$ amortized per operation; a single operation can cost $\Theta(n)$.
- **Weights** (positive, analysis only) give static optimality ($O(1 + \log(T/t(x)))$), the static finger bound; the working set, scanning and dynamic finger theorems also hold; dynamic optimality is open.
- The note ends with 10 exercises (perfect balance and the single scapegoat, purging a dirty tree in linear time and little space, move-to-root, staircases, print-and-delete ranges, rank selection in augmented trees, secondary structures, strings with reversal, burnt pancakes, and the geometric view of binary search trees).
