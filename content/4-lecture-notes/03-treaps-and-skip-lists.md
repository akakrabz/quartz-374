> [!abstract] In one breath
> A **treap** is a binary tree whose nodes carry a search key and a priority: in-order it is a binary search tree on the keys, top-down it is a min-heap on the priorities. With distinct keys and priorities the shape is unique — it is the BST obtained by inserting the keys in order of increasing priority — and search, insertion and deletion (by **rotations**), split and join all take time proportional to the depth of one node. Choosing the priorities at random makes the treap a random BST: node $x_i$ is an ancestor of $x_k$ exactly when it has the smallest priority among $x_i,\dots,x_k$, which has probability $1/(\lvert k-i\rvert+1)$, so the expected depth of $x_k$ is $H_k + H_{n-k+1} - 2 < 2\ln n$ and every operation takes $O(\log n)$ expected time; the same calculation analyzes randomized quicksort. A **skip list** is a sorted linked list in which every element is copied up one more level for each heads in a row of fair coin flips; it has $O(\log n)$ levels with high probability (union bound), at most $\lceil\lg n\rceil + 1$ in expectation, and a search, run backwards, is a random walk that climbs one level per heads — $O(\log n)$ steps with high probability.

> [!note] Where this fits
> Both structures answer the question left open by the binary search trees of [[2-algorithms/02-backtracking#28-optimal-binary-search-trees|§2.8]] and [[2-algorithms/03-dynamic-programming#39-optimal-binary-search-trees|§3.9]]: how to keep a dynamic BST shallow without the case analysis of AVL or red-black trees. The analysis uses only indicator variables, linearity of expectation and harmonic numbers ([[4-lecture-notes/01-discrete-probability#122-expectation|note 1, §1.2.2]], [[concepts/discrete-probability]]), and §3.1.4 finishes the random-pivot analysis that [[2-algorithms/01-recursion#15-quicksort|§1.5]] leaves outside the book. [[4-lecture-notes/04-tail-inequalities|Note 4]] upgrades the expected bounds to high-probability bounds for the whole tree; [[4-lecture-notes/10-scapegoat-and-splay-trees|note 10]] gives deterministic, amortized alternatives. ## 3.1 Treaps

### 3.1.1 Definitions

> [!definition] Definition — treap
> A **treap** is a binary tree in which every node $v$ has a **search key** $\mathit{key}(v)$ and a **priority** $\mathit{priority}(v)$ such that the in-order sequence of keys is sorted (a binary search tree) and every node's priority is smaller than its children's (a min-heap). Assume all keys and all priorities are distinct.

> [!key] The shape of a treap is determined by its keys and priorities
> The node with the smallest priority must be the root (heap order); the nodes with smaller keys must form its left subtree and those with larger keys its right subtree (search order); and each subtree is a treap, determined by induction (the empty treap is the base case). Equivalently, **a treap is the BST obtained by inserting its nodes, in order of increasing priority, into an empty tree with the textbook insertion algorithm** (the note's Exercise 1).

A third picture: plot each node as the point (key, priority), smallest priority at the top. The root's "T" — a horizontal line through the top point and a vertical line down from it — has nothing above it and splits the other points into the two subtrees, which are split the same way. The structure was introduced by Vuillemin (1980) as the *Cartesian tree*; Seidel and Aragon (1989) used random priorities to build randomized search trees.

### 3.1.2 Treap operations

A **rotation** at a node $x$ with parent $y$ makes $x$ the parent of $y$, moving the middle subtree to the other side; it preserves the in-order sequence, moves $x$ up one level and $y$ down one level, and changes $O(1)$ pointers.

```text
TreapInsert(z):                      ⟨⟨z has a key and a priority⟩⟩
    insert z as a leaf by ordinary binary-search-tree insertion
    while z is not the root and priority(z) < priority(parent(z))
        rotate at z                  ⟨⟨z moves up one level⟩⟩

TreapDelete(z):
    while z is not a leaf
        c ← the child of z with smaller priority   ⟨⟨or its only child⟩⟩
        rotate at c                  ⟨⟨z moves down one level⟩⟩
    remove z
```

- **Search** is ordinary BST search: $O(\mathit{depth}(v))$ time for the node $v$ with the query key. An unsuccessful search ends at the query's in-order predecessor $v^-$ or successor $v^+$, so it costs $O(\mathit{depth}(v^-))$ or $O(\mathit{depth}(v^+))$.
- **Insertion** walks down to a leaf position — whose parent is $v^-$ or $v^+$ — and back up with at most that many rotations, each restoring heap order one level higher: about twice the cost of an unsuccessful search for the new key. Rotations never break search order, and the only heap violation is always between $z$ and its parent, so the result is the unique treap of the new set.
- **Deletion** is insertion run backwards: rotating at the child of smaller priority keeps heap order everywhere except at $z$; when $z$ is a leaf, cut it off. Same cost.
- **Split** at a pivot $\pi$ (not a key): insert a node with key $\pi$ and priority $-\infty$; it becomes the root, and its two subtrees are the treaps $T_<$ and $T_>$ of the keys below and above $\pi$. **Join** $T_<$ and $T_>$ (all keys of $T_<$ below those of $T_>$): make them the subtrees of a dummy root, rotate it down to a leaf as in TreapDelete, cut it off. Both cost as much as inserting the dummy.

> [!example] Example — inserting S with priority −1
> In the treap of the figure above, BST insertion puts S as the right child of R5 (its in-order predecessor), at depth 3. Its priority $-1$ is smaller than R's, T's and M's, so three rotations carry it to the root. Deleting S from the result reverses the sequence; splitting the original treap at a pivot between M and O gives $T_< = \{A,G,H,I,L,M\}$ and $T_> = \{O,R,T\}$, and joining them restores it.

Every operation costs $O(\text{depth of some node})$. In the worst case that is $\Theta(n)$ — if the priorities increase with the keys, the treap is a path.

### 3.1.3 Random priorities

A **randomized treap** gives each node an independent priority drawn uniformly from $[0,1]$ when it is inserted (continuous, so ties have probability 0; in practice random integers from a large range, with ties broken arbitrarily, behave the same). What is random is only the priorities — the keys and the sequence of operations are arbitrary but fixed in advance. Since the priorities are i.i.d., every one of the $n!$ priority orders is equally likely, and every node is equally likely to be the root.

Write $x_k$ for the node with the $k$th smallest key and $i\uparrow k$ ("$i$ above $k$") for "$x_i$ is a proper ancestor of $x_k$". The depth of a node is its number of proper ancestors, so by linearity of expectation
$$
\mathit{depth}(x_k) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\,[i\uparrow k],\qquad \mathrm{E}[\mathit{depth}(x_k)] = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\Pr[i\uparrow k].
$$
Let $X(i,k)$ be the set of nodes with keys from $x_i$ to $x_k$ (in either order of $i$ and $k$).

> [!key] Lemma 1
> For all $i\ne k$: $i\uparrow k$ if and only if $x_i$ has the smallest priority among the nodes of $X(i,k)$.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Induction on the size of the treap. If $x_i$ is the root, then $i\uparrow k$, and $x_i$ has the smallest priority of all. If $x_k$ is the root, then $x_i$ is not its ancestor, and $x_k\in X(i,k)$ has a smaller priority than $x_i$. If some other node $x_j$ is the root and $x_i$, $x_k$ lie in different subtrees, then $j$ is strictly between $i$ and $k$, neither is an ancestor of the other, and $x_j\in X(i,k)$ beats $x_i$. Otherwise both lie in the same subtree, a smaller treap containing all of $X(i,k)$, and the induction hypothesis applies. $\square$

Each of the $\lvert k-i\rvert+1$ nodes of $X(i,k)$ is equally likely to have the smallest priority, so
$$
\Pr[i\uparrow k] = \frac{[i\ne k]}{\lvert k-i\rvert+1}
$$
and
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{E}[\mathit{depth}(x_k)] &= \sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{k-i+1} + \sum_{i=k+1}^{n}\frac{1}{i-k+1}\\
&= \sum_{j=2}^{k}\frac1j + \sum_{j=2}^{n-k+1}\frac1j \;=\; H_k + H_{n-k+1} - 2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

> [!key] Expected depth in a randomized treap
> $\mathrm{E}[\mathit{depth}(x_k)] = H_k + H_{n-k+1} - 2 \;\le\; \ln k + \ln(n-k+1) \;\le\; 2\ln\tfrac{n+1}{2} \;<\; 2\ln n$ for $n\ge 2$, because $H_m - 1\le\ln m$. So **search, insertion, deletion, split and join take $O(\log n)$ expected time** on an $n$-node randomized treap.

The note's chain continues "$< \ln k + \ln(n-k+1) - 2 < 2\ln n - 2$" (p. 5). The first step is backwards: $H_m - 1 > \ln(m+1) - 1 > \ln m - 1$, so the left side is always *larger* than $\ln k + \ln(n-k+1) - 2$. The final bound $2\ln n - 2$ is false for $n\le 16$ (for $n = 2$, $\mathrm{E}[\mathit{depth}(x_1)] = 1/2$ while $2\ln 2 - 2 < 0$) and true from $n = 17$ on. The bound to remember is $2\ln n\approx 1.39\lg n$.

> [!example] Example — exact expected depths for n = 7
> Enumerating all $7! = 5040$ priority orders gives, for $k = 1,\dots,7$: $223/140\approx 1.59$, $39/20 = 1.95$, $127/60\approx 2.12$, $13/6\approx 2.17$, $127/60$, $39/20$, $223/140$ — exactly $H_k + H_{8-k} - 2$, deepest in the middle, all below $2\ln 7\approx 3.89$.

Since a treap is the BST obtained by inserting in order of priority, a randomized treap has the distribution of a BST built by inserting the keys **in random order**, and the same expected depths hold for such random BSTs, priorities or not.

### 3.1.4 Randomized quicksort (again!)

A third description of randomized quicksort (after the recursive one of [[2-algorithms/01-recursion#15-quicksort|§1.5]] and the iterative one of [[4-lecture-notes/02-nuts-and-bolts#27-iterative-analysis|note 2, §2.7]]):

```text
RandomizedQuicksort:
    T ← an empty binary search tree
    insert the keys into T in random order
    output the in-order sequence of keys in T
```

Each insertion takes $O(\log n)$ expected time, so the sort takes $O(n\log n)$ expected time. It *is* quicksort: the first key inserted plays the first pivot — every later key is compared with it on the way down — and the keys that go to its left and right subtrees are exactly the two partitions; by induction both versions make the same comparisons. Two keys $x_i, x_k$ are compared exactly when one is an ancestor of the other, so
$$
\Pr[x_i \text{ and } x_k \text{ are compared}] = \Pr[i\uparrow k] + \Pr[k\uparrow i] = \frac{2}{\lvert k-i\rvert+1},
$$
the probability met in the nuts-and-bolts analysis of [[4-lecture-notes/02-nuts-and-bolts#27-iterative-analysis|note 2]]. Summing (not in the note): the expected number of comparisons is $\sum_k \mathrm{E}[\mathit{depth}(x_k)] = 2(n+1)H_n - 4n < 2n\ln n\approx 1.39\,n\lg n$ for $n\ge2$, for every input.

## 3.2 Skip lists

Skip lists (Pugh, 1989) have the performance of balanced search trees with the structure of linked lists. Start with one sorted linked list, with sentinels $-\infty$ and $+\infty$ at the ends. Copy each element into a second sorted list, of **shortcuts**, independently with probability $1/2$, each copy pointing down to its original. To search for $x$, scan the shortcut list from $-\infty$ until $x$ or a larger key appears; if $x$ was not found, drop down from $w$, the last shortcut node below $x$, and scan the bottom list from there.

**Cost.** Count every node examined, including the node where a scan stops. For the $k$th smallest key, the first phase examines $-\infty$, the copied keys below $x_k$ (expected number $(k-1)/2$) and one more node: $(k-1)/2 + 2$ on average. The second phase is needed only if $x_k$ has no copy (probability $1/2$); it examines $w$, the uncopied keys between $w$ and $x_k$ — at least $j$ of them with probability $2^{-j}$ — and $x_k$, on average $2 + \sum_{j=1}^{k-1}2^{-j} = 3 - 2^{1-k}$ nodes. In total $k/2 + 3 - 2^{-k}$, at most $n/2 + 3$: one level of shortcuts saves a factor of about two. (The note's version of the second-phase count, "$1 + \sum_{k\ge0}2^{-k} = 2$" on p. 7, has a slip — that sum is 3 — and it concludes $n/2 + 2$; the difference is only in what is counted.)

### 3.2.1 Recursive random shortcuts

Add shortcuts to the shortcuts, recursively: for each element, flip a fair coin until it comes up tails, and make one more copy, one level higher, for every heads. Each level is a sorted linked list between sentinels; each node $v$ stores $\mathit{key}(v)$, $\mathit{right}(v)$ (the next node on its level) and $\mathit{down}(v)$ (its copy one level lower). In the note's figures the highest level holds only the two sentinels.

```text
SkipListFind(x, L):                  ⟨⟨L = the leftmost node of the top level⟩⟩
    v ← L
    while v ≠ Null and key(v) ≠ x
        if key(right(v)) > x
            v ← down(v)
        else
            v ← right(v)
    return v
```

On each level the search moves right as far as it can without passing $x$, then drops down; it returns the highest copy of $x$, or Null after failing on the bottom level.

### 3.2.2 Number of levels

The analysis does not depend on the key values; take the keys $1,\dots,n$. Let $L(x)$ be the number of levels above the bottom that contain $x$ — the number of heads before the first tails. With probability $1/2$ the first flip is tails and $L(x) = 0$; otherwise $L(x)$ is $1$ plus a fresh copy of itself: $\mathrm{E}[L(x)] = \tfrac12\cdot 0 + \tfrac12(1 + \mathrm{E}[L(x)])$, so $\mathrm{E}[L(x)] = 1$, and $\Pr[L(x)\ge\ell] = 2^{-\ell}$.

The search cost depends on the number of levels $L = \max_x L(x)$, and the expectation of a maximum is not the maximum of expectations. Instead the note proves a high-probability bound.

> [!definition] Definition — with high probability
> An event happens **with high probability** (w.h.p.) if its probability is at least $1 - 1/n^c$ for some constant $c\ge 1$; "$X = O(f(n))$ w.h.p." allows the hidden constant to depend on $c$.

$L\ge\ell$ exactly when $L(x)\ge\ell$ for at least one of the $n$ keys. By the **union bound** $\Pr[A\vee B]\le\Pr[A]+\Pr[B]$ ([[4-lecture-notes/01-discrete-probability#113-identities-and-inequalities|note 1, §1.1.3]]),
$$
\Pr[L\ge\ell]\;\le\;\sum_{x=1}^{n}\Pr[L(x)\ge\ell] = \frac{n}{2^\ell},\qquad\text{so}\qquad \Pr[L\ge c\lg n]\le\frac{1}{n^{c-1}}\ \text{ for every } c>1.
$$
**A skip list has $O(\log n)$ levels with high probability.** (Exactly, $\Pr[L\ge\ell] = 1-(1-2^{-\ell})^n$.)

The tail bound also bounds the expectation. Using $\mathrm{E}[L] = \sum_{\ell\ge1}\Pr[L\ge\ell]$ and bounding the first $\lceil\lg n\rceil$ terms by 1 and the rest by $n/2^\ell$:
$$
\mathrm{E}[L]\;\le\;\lceil\lg n\rceil + \sum_{\ell>\lceil\lg n\rceil}\frac{n}{2^\ell} = \lceil\lg n\rceil + \frac{n}{2^{\lceil\lg n\rceil}}\;\le\;\lceil\lg n\rceil + 1\;<\;\lg n + 2 .
$$
So a skip list has, in expectation, at most about one level more than a perfectly balanced one — an additive penalty, where a treap's expected depth is a constant *multiple* of $\lg n$. (The note writes $\mathrm{E}[L(x)]$ for $\mathrm{E}[L]$ and ends its computation with $\lg n + \sum_{i\ge1}2^{-i} = \lg n + 2$ (p. 9); the sum is 1, so for $n$ a power of 2 its own algebra gives $\lg n + 1$. The stated bound $\lg n + 2$ is true for all $n$.) The exact values are close to $\lg n + \tfrac13$: for $n = 1000$, $\mathrm{E}[L] = 10.30$ while $\lg n = 9.97$.

### 3.2.3 Logarithmic search time

**Backwards analysis.** Run the search in reverse, from the node it returns back to the top-left sentinel, pretending the lists also have $\mathit{up}$ and $\mathit{left}$ pointers (the note spells the procedure's name backwards, dniFtsiLpikS):

```text
SkipListFindBackwards(v):
    while level(v) ≠ the top level
        if up(v) exists
            v ← up(v)
        else
            v ← left(v)
```

This retraces the search path exactly: the forward search came down at the last node of each level that has a copy above. Every node visited has a copy above it with probability $1/2$, independently of everything visited before, so for the analysis the reverse walk is a **FlipWalk**: at each step flip a fair coin, go up on heads, left on tails (at the $-\infty$ sentinel the real walk always goes up, which only shortens it). The walk needs at most $L+1$ heads, and in expectation it flips as many tails as heads, so its expected length is about twice the number of levels.

To get a high-probability bound, fix $\ell = c\lg n$ and $t = 4\ell$. If the walk takes more than $t$ steps, then either $L\ge\ell$ (probability $\le 1/n^{c-1}$) or the first $t$ coin flips contain fewer than $\ell = t/4$ heads, which has probability less than $e^{-t/16} = n^{-c/(4\ln 2)}$ by a Chernoff bound ([[4-lecture-notes/04-tail-inequalities#46-coin-flips|§4.6]] of note 4). So **a search takes $O(\log n)$ time with high probability**, and also in expectation. (The note states this conclusion after the heads-equal-tails remark, without the tail bound.)

> [!note] An exact count (not in the note)
> With an empty top level as in the figure, the expected number of moves of SkipListFind for the $k$th smallest key is exactly $\mathrm{E}[L] + \mathrm{E}[L_k]$, where $L_k$ is the largest $L(x)$ among the $k$ smallest keys: $\mathrm{E}[L]$ down moves on average and, in expectation, one right move per level that the $k$ smallest keys reach. For the largest key it is $2\,\mathrm{E}[L]\le 2\lceil\lg n\rceil + 2$; for $n = 1000$, about 20.6 moves.

## Summary

- **Treap** = BST on keys + min-heap on priorities; with distinct priorities the shape is unique (= BST built by inserting in priority order).
- **Operations by rotations**: insert as a leaf and rotate up while the priority is smaller than the parent's; delete by rotating down at the child of smaller priority, then cut; split by inserting a $-\infty$ dummy; join by sinking a dummy root. Each costs $O(\text{depth of one node})$; worst case $\Theta(n)$.
- **Random priorities**: $i\uparrow k$ iff $x_i$ has the smallest priority among $x_i..x_k$ (Lemma 1), so $\Pr[i\uparrow k] = 1/(\lvert k-i\rvert+1)$ and $\mathrm{E}[\mathit{depth}(x_k)] = H_k + H_{n-k+1} - 2 < 2\ln n$: every operation $O(\log n)$ expected.
- **Randomized quicksort** = inserting the keys into a BST in random order; $x_i, x_k$ compared with probability $2/(\lvert k-i\rvert+1)$; $2(n+1)H_n - 4n$ comparisons expected.
- **Skip list**: each element promoted once per heads; $\Pr[L\ge c\lg n]\le n^{1-c}$ by the union bound, so $O(\log n)$ levels w.h.p.; $\mathrm{E}[L]\le\lceil\lg n\rceil + 1$.
- **Search** runs backwards as a coin-flip walk up and left: $O(\log n)$ w.h.p. and in expectation.
- The note ends with 10 exercises (treaps as insertion-order BSTs; expected numbers of descendants, leaves and spine nodes; "heaters", with random keys and given priorities; skip-list size, insertion, deletion and merging; skip-list trees; treaps with geometric or bit-by-bit priorities; randomized meldable heaps; adversaries that know the random choices).
