> [!abstract] In one breath
> **Problem 1** is short answers: three recurrences (one shrinking child is dominated by the root, $8B(n/2)$ by the leaves, and three pieces adding up to $n$ give $n\log n$) and the memoization plans for an interval recurrence ($O(n^3)$) and a recurrence whose second argument only doubles ($O(n\log n)$ entries). **Problem 2** is quadhopper: the handout's own puzzle defeats "always take the smallest number" (5 moves instead of 7), and $\textsf{MaxMoves}(i)$, the most moves from square $i$, is a suffix recurrence filled right to left in $O(n)$ time. **Problem 3** asks for the highest point of an $s$–$t$ walk of length at most $L$: a walk can visit $v$ exactly when $d_s(v) + d_t(v)\le L$, so two runs of Dijkstra and a scan solve it in $O(E\log V)$. **Problem 4** is text segmentation with skipping: $\textsf{MaxWords}(i)$ either skips $T[i]$ or starts a word there, $O(n^3)$ time with the $O(|w|)$ word test. **Problem 5** is the closest pair of points: split at the median $x$-coordinate, recurse on both halves, and let $\textsf{IWalkTheLine}$ find the best pair across the line, $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n) = O(n\log n)$.

## Problem 1 — Short answers

> [!question] Problem 1
> (a) Solve the recurrences $A(n) = A(5n/11) + O(\sqrt n)$, $\ B(n) = 8B(n/2) + O(n^2)$, $\ C(n) = C(n/2) + C(n/3) + C(n/6) + O(n)$.
>
> (b) Describe an appropriate memoization structure and evaluation order for each of the following (meaningless) recurrences, and state the running time of the resulting iterative algorithm. Compute $\textsf{Foo}(1, n)$, where
> $$
> \textsf{Foo}(i, k) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } i\ge k-1\\ \max\limits_{i<j<k}\big\{\textsf{Foo}(i, j) + \textsf{Foo}(j, k)\big\} + \displaystyle\sum_{j=i}^{k} A[j] & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
> $$
> and compute $\textsf{Bar}(n, 1)$, where
> $$
> \textsf{Bar}(i, s) = \begin{cases} \infty & \text{if } i < 0 \text{ or } s > n\\ 0 & \text{if } i = 0\\ \min\big\{\textsf{Bar}(i, 2s),\ X[i]\cdot s + \textsf{Bar}(i - s, s)\big\} & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
> $$

*Method: [[concepts/recursion-trees]], [[2-algorithms/01-recursion#17-recursion-trees|§1.7]] · memoization plans: [[concepts/memoization]], [[0-toolkit/08-review-algorithms#4-chapter-3--dynamic-programming|the subproblem-shape table]]*

### (a) Three recurrences

For each one, compare the sums of consecutive levels of the recursion tree.

> [!key] Answer (a)
> - $A(n) = O(\sqrt n)$. One child of size $\tfrac{5}{11}n$: level $i$ sums to $\big(\sqrt{5/11}\big)^i\sqrt n$, a decreasing geometric series, so the root dominates.
> - $B(n) = O(n^3)$. Level ratio $8/2^2 = 2 > 1$: the leaves dominate, and there are $8^{\lg n} = n^{\lg 8} = n^3$ of them.
> - $C(n) = O(n\log n)$. The fractions add up to $\tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac16 = 1$, so every level of the recursion tree sums to at most $n$ (the first $\log_6 n$ levels to exactly $n$), and there are at most $\lg n$ levels.

The bounds are $\Theta$ when the $O(\cdot)$ term is exact, which is how the exam means it.

### (b) Two memoization plans

**Foo.** The recurrence is defined on intervals $1\le i\le k\le n$, and $\textsf{Foo}(i, k)$ reads $\textsf{Foo}(i, j)$ with $j < k$ (same $i$, smaller $k$) and $\textsf{Foo}(j, k)$ with $j > i$ (larger $i$, same $k$).

> [!key] Answer (b), Foo
> **Memoization:** an array $\textsf{Foo}[1\,..\,n, 1\,..\,n]$, of which only the entries with $i\le k$ are used. **Evaluation order:** $i$ from $n$ down to 1 in the outer loop, $k$ from $i$ up to $n$ in the inner loop (equivalently, all intervals by increasing length $k - i$). **Time:** $O(n^2)$ entries, each a maximum over $O(n)$ values of $j$ plus a sum of $O(n)$ array entries, so $O(n)$ per entry and **$O(n^3)$** in all.

Prefix sums would make the $\sum A[j]$ term $O(1)$, but the $\max$ over $j$ still costs $O(n)$, so the bound stays $O(n^3)$.

**Bar.** Starting from $s = 1$, the second argument only ever doubles, so it takes the values $s = 2^e$ for $0\le e\le\lfloor\lg n\rfloor$ (any larger $s$ is a base case, $\infty$); the first argument stays in $0\,..\,n$ (below 0 is a base case). $\textsf{Bar}(i, 2^e)$ reads $\textsf{Bar}(i, 2^{e+1})$ (same $i$, larger $e$) and $\textsf{Bar}(i - 2^e, 2^e)$ (smaller $i$, same $e$).

> [!key] Answer (b), Bar
> **Memoization:** an array $\textsf{Bar}[0\,..\,n, 0\,..\,\lfloor\lg n\rfloor]$ whose entry $[i, e]$ holds $\textsf{Bar}(i, 2^e)$; the base cases $i < 0$ and $s > n$ are not stored but read as $\infty$. **Evaluation order:** $i$ from 0 up to $n$ in the outer loop, $e$ from $\lfloor\lg n\rfloor$ down to 0 in the inner loop (the loops can also be swapped: $e$ decreasing outside, $i$ increasing inside). **Time:** $(n+1)(\lfloor\lg n\rfloor + 1)$ entries, $O(1)$ each: **$O(n\log n)$**.

> [!trap] Two ways to lose the point
> An array $\textsf{Bar}[0\,..\,n, 1\,..\,n]$ indexed by $s$ itself is correct but wastes space and suggests $O(n^2)$ time; say that only powers of 2 occur. And in $\textsf{Foo}$, "$i$ increasing" in the outer loop reads $\textsf{Foo}(j, k)$ with $j > i$ before it is written.

> [!exam] How this is graded
> No standard rubric covers Problem 1. Give each recurrence with its level ratio and the case it falls in, and each memoization plan with the dependency that fixes the order — which loop is outer, which way each index runs. ## Problem 2 — Quadhopper

> [!question] Problem 2
> Quadhopper is a solitaire game played on a row of $n$ squares, each containing four positive integers. The player places a token on the leftmost square. On each move, the player chooses one of the numbers on the token's current square and moves the token that many squares to the right. The game ends when the token moves past the rightmost square; the object is to make as many moves as possible. The handout's example has 13 squares and shows a sequence of six moves (hops 4, 1, 3, 2, 2, 1), which is not the longest.
>
> (a) Prove that the obvious greedy strategy (always choose the smallest number) does not give the largest possible number of moves for every quadhopper puzzle.
>
> (b) Describe and analyze an efficient algorithm to find the largest possible number of legal moves for a given quadhopper puzzle.

*Template: [[0-toolkit/02-proof-templates#20-writing-up-a-dynamic-program|dynamic program]] · concepts: [[concepts/dynamic-programming]], [[concepts/memoization]] · greedy counterexamples: [[2-algorithms/03-dynamic-programming#35-warning-greed-is-stupid|§3.5]]*

Write $N[i, 1], \ldots, N[i, 4]$ for the four numbers on square $i$.

### (a) The greedy strategy fails

> [!key] Answer (a)
> Take the four squares $(1, 2, 2, 2)$, $(4, 4, 4, 4)$, $(1, 1, 1, 1)$, $(1, 1, 1, 1)$. Greedy hops 1 to square 2, then must hop 4, past the end: **2 moves**. Hopping 2 to square 3, then 1 to square 4, then 1 past the end makes **3 moves**. So greedy is not optimal.

The handout's own puzzle is a counterexample too: greedy visits squares 1, 2, 4, 7, 13 (hops 1, 2, 3, 6, 1) and makes 5 moves, but the handout already shows 6, and part (b) finds 7. Either counterexample is a complete answer — it needs no argument that the better sequence is optimal.

### (b) A dynamic program

Ask the backtracking question: which number does the first move use? Each choice leaves the same game, started from a square further right — so the subproblem is "the game started on square $i$".

> [!key] Answer (b)
> **Function.** For every integer $i\ge 1$, let $\textsf{MaxMoves}(i)$ be the largest number of moves in a game whose token starts on square $i$; in particular $\textsf{MaxMoves}(i) = 0$ for $i > n$, because the game is already over. We need $\textsf{MaxMoves}(1)$.
>
> **Recurrence.**
> $$
> \textsf{MaxMoves}(i) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } i > n\\ 1 + \max\limits_{1\le c\le 4}\textsf{MaxMoves}\big(i + N[i, c]\big) & \text{if } 1\le i\le n\end{cases}
> $$
> **Memoization.** An array $\textsf{MaxMoves}[1\,..\,n+1]$, where entry $n+1$ is 0 and stands for every square past the end: read $\textsf{MaxMoves}(i + N[i, c])$ as entry $\min\{i + N[i, c], n + 1\}$.
>
> **Evaluation order.** Every reference goes to a larger index, so fill $i$ from $n+1$ down to 1.
>
> **Time.** $n + 1$ entries, $O(1)$ work each (four candidates): **$O(n)$**.

Each move goes strictly right, so the squares form a dag and $\textsf{MaxMoves}$ is a longest path in it. To output the moves themselves, record which $c$ attains each maximum and follow those choices from square 1.

## Problem 3 — The highest point of a short walk

> [!question] Problem 3
> You walk from home to your office and want to reach the highest possible altitude on the way, while keeping the total length of the walk at most some threshold. The input is an undirected graph $G$ in which every vertex $v$ has a height $h(v)$ and every edge $e$ has a positive length $\ell(e)$, a start vertex $s$, a target vertex $t$ and a maximum length $L$. Describe and analyze an algorithm that returns the maximum height reachable by a walk from $s$ to $t$ in $G$ whose total length is at most $L$.

*Template: [[0-toolkit/02-proof-templates#22-solving-a-problem-by-graph-reduction|graph reduction]] · concepts: [[concepts/dijkstras-algorithm]], [[concepts/shortest-paths]] · the black box: [[2-algorithms/08-shortest-paths#86-best-first-dijkstras-algorithm|§8.6]]*

The height a walk reaches is the largest $h(v)$ over its vertices. So the question is: **which vertices can a walk from $s$ to $t$ of length at most $L$ visit?** Write $d_s(v)$ and $d_t(v)$ for the shortest-path distances in $G$ from $s$ and from $t$ to $v$ ($G$ is undirected, so $d_t(v)$ is also the distance from $v$ to $t$).

> [!key] Lemma
> *Some walk from $s$ to $t$ of length at most $L$ visits $v$ if and only if $d_s(v) + d_t(v)\le L$.*
>
> **Proof.** If a walk $W$ from $s$ to $t$ visits $v$, cut it at a visit of $v$: the part before is a walk from $s$ to $v$, of length at least $d_s(v)$, and the part after is a walk from $v$ to $t$, of length at least $d_t(v)$; so $L\ge\ell(W)\ge d_s(v) + d_t(v)$. Conversely, a shortest path from $s$ to $v$ followed by a shortest path from $v$ to $t$ is a walk from $s$ to $t$ through $v$ of length $d_s(v) + d_t(v)\le L$. $\square$

> [!key] Answer
> - **The graph.** $G$ itself: undirected, with the edge lengths $\ell(e) > 0$ as weights.
> - **The problem on it.** Single-source shortest paths from $s$ and from $t$, giving $d_s(v)$ and $d_t(v)$ for every vertex $v$. The answer is $\max\{h(v) \mid v\in V,\ d_s(v) + d_t(v)\le L\}$, or "no walk" if that set is empty (that is, if $d_s(t) > L$).
> - **The algorithm.** Dijkstra's algorithm from $s$ and from $t$ (black boxes — all lengths are positive), then one scan over the vertices.
> - **The time.** $O(E\log V)$ for the two runs of Dijkstra and $O(V)$ for the scan: **$O(E\log V)$**, where $V$ and $E$ are the numbers of vertices and edges of the input graph.

By the lemma, the set contains exactly the vertices that some admissible walk visits, and the best walk reaches the highest of them; to output that walk, follow the predecessor pointers of the two shortest-path trees from the best $v$ back to $s$ and to $t$.

> [!trap] Tempting wrong answers
> "Search from $s$ and stop when the length exceeds $L$" loses the length budget for the way back to $t$. "Try every path with depth-first search" is exponential. And breadth-first search ignores the lengths.

> [!exam] How this is graded
> Graph-reduction rubric ([[0-toolkit/06-how-exams-are-graded#13-graph-reductions|§13]]): **3** for the graph (here $G$ itself, say undirected and weighted by $\ell$), **3** for the problem — shortest paths from $s$ and from $t$, and the formula that turns them into the answer (the lemma is the 2 detail points), **2** for Dijkstra as a black box, **2** for $O(E\log V)$.

## Problem 4 — The longest verbal subsequence

> [!question] Problem 4
> A message in a foreign language is given as an array $T[1\,..\,n]$ of symbols, and a black box $\textsf{IsWord}(w)$ decides whether a string $w$ is a word in $O(|w|)$ time. The message cannot be split into words: extra symbols were inserted between words. A **verbal subsequence** of $T$ is a sequence of non-overlapping substrings of $T$, each of which is a word; its length is the number of words. Describe and analyze an algorithm to find the length of the longest verbal subsequence of $T$.
>
> For example, with English words, (STUDY, AM, ICE, TRAP, RAMBLE) and (DYNAMIC, EXTRA, PROGRAM) are verbal subsequences of STUDYNAMICEXTRAPROGRAMBLE, so the answer for that string is at least 5.

*Template: [[0-toolkit/02-proof-templates#20-writing-up-a-dynamic-program|dynamic program]] · the problem it generalizes: [[2-algorithms/03-dynamic-programming#33-interpunctio-verborum-redux|text segmentation, §3.3]] · concept: [[concepts/dynamic-programming]]*

As in text segmentation, ask about the first symbol of the rest of the string: either it is not part of any chosen word, or a chosen word starts there — and then that word is $T[i\,..\,j]$ for some $j$.

> [!key] Answer
> **Function.** For $1\le i\le n+1$, let $\textsf{MaxWords}(i)$ be the length of the longest verbal subsequence of the suffix $T[i\,..\,n]$. We need $\textsf{MaxWords}(1)$.
>
> **Recurrence.** $\textsf{MaxWords}(n+1) = 0$, and for $1\le i\le n$
> $$
> \textsf{MaxWords}(i) = \max\Big\{\textsf{MaxWords}(i+1),\ \max_{\substack{i\le j\le n\\ \textsf{IsWord}(T[i\,..\,j])}}\big(1 + \textsf{MaxWords}(j+1)\big)\Big\}
> $$
> (an inner $\max$ over no $j$ is ignored). The first option skips $T[i]$; the second makes $T[i\,..\,j]$ the first word.
>
> **Memoization.** An array $\textsf{MaxWords}[1\,..\,n+1]$.
>
> **Evaluation order.** $i$ from $n+1$ down to 1: every reference has a larger index.
>
> **Time.** Entry $i$ calls $\textsf{IsWord}(T[i\,..\,j])$ for every $j\ge i$, which costs $O(j - i + 1)$; the total is $\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i}^{n}O(j - i + 1) = O(n^3)$. Everything else is $O(n^2)$, so **$O(n^3)$** time.

With the handout's eight words (STUDY, AM, ICE, TRAP, RAMBLE, DYNAMIC, EXTRA, PROGRAM) as the whole dictionary, the algorithm returns 5 on STUDYNAMICEXTRAPROGRAMBLE.

> [!exam] How this is graded
> Dynamic-programming rubric ([[0-toolkit/06-how-exams-are-graded#12-dynamic-programming|§12]]): **3** for "$\textsf{MaxWords}(i)$ is the most non-overlapping words in the suffix $T[i\,..\,n]$", **4** for the recurrence — the skip option is the one that is easy to forget, and without it the algorithm only handles strings that split completely — **3** for the array, the order and $O(n^3)$. The time must count the cost of $\textsf{IsWord}$: "$O(n^2)$" counts calls, not time.

## Problem 5 — The closest pair of points

> [!question] Problem 5
> Find the closest pair of points in a set of $n$ points in the plane, given as an array $P[1\,..\,n]$ sorted by $x$-coordinate, with all $x$- and $y$-coordinates distinct. Two subroutines are available as black boxes:
> - $\textsf{IWalkTheLine}(P, a)$ returns, in $O(n)$ time for an $n$-point set $P$, the indices $i$ and $j$ of the closest pair of points with $P[i].x\le a < P[j].x$ — the closest pair that crosses the vertical line $x = a$;
> - $\textsf{Distance}(p, q)$ returns the Euclidean distance between $p$ and $q$ in $O(1)$ time.
>
> Describe and analyze an algorithm to compute the distance between the closest pair of points in $P$, using these subroutines as black boxes.

*Template: [[0-toolkit/02-proof-templates#18-writing-up-a-divide-and-conquer-algorithm|divide and conquer]] · concept: [[concepts/divide-and-conquer]] · the same shape: [[2-algorithms/01-recursion#14-mergesort|mergesort]]*

Split at a vertical line through the middle point. The closest pair lies entirely left of the line, entirely right of it, or crosses it — and the black box handles exactly the third case.

> [!key] Answer
> **What the recursive algorithm returns.** For $1\le lo\le hi\le n$, $\textsf{ClosestPair}(lo, hi)$ returns the smallest distance between two points of $P[lo\,..\,hi]$, or $\infty$ if $lo = hi$. We call $\textsf{ClosestPair}(1, n)$.
>
> **Base case.** If $lo = hi$, return $\infty$.
>
> **Divide.** Let $m = \lfloor(lo + hi)/2\rfloor$ and $a = P[m].x$. Because $P$ is sorted by $x$ and the $x$-coordinates are distinct, the points of $P[lo\,..\,m]$ are exactly those of $P[lo\,..\,hi]$ with $x\le a$, and the points of $P[m+1\,..\,hi]$ those with $x > a$. Recursively compute $\delta_L = \textsf{ClosestPair}(lo, m)$ and $\delta_R = \textsf{ClosestPair}(m+1, hi)$.
>
> **Conquer.** Let $(i, j) = \textsf{IWalkTheLine}(P[lo\,..\,hi], a)$, and return $\min\{\delta_L,\ \delta_R,\ \textsf{Distance}(P[i], P[j])\}$ (indices $i, j$ relative to the subarray passed in).
>
> **Time.** Apart from the two recursive calls, a call on $k$ points makes one call to $\textsf{IWalkTheLine}$ on $k$ points, $O(k)$ time, and $O(1)$ other work (pass the subarray as its two end indices, not as a copy). So
> $$
> T(n) = T(\lceil n/2\rceil) + T(\lfloor n/2\rfloor) + O(n),
> $$
> which is mergesort's recurrence: every level of the recursion tree costs $O(n)$ and there are $O(\log n)$ levels, so **$T(n) = O(n\log n)$**.

**Why it is correct** (by strong induction on $hi - lo$). Every pair of points of $P[lo\,..\,hi]$ has both points left of the line, both right of it, or one on each side. The recursive calls are correct by the induction hypothesis and cover the first two kinds; a pair with one point on each side is a pair with $P[i].x\le a < P[j].x$, and $\textsf{IWalkTheLine}$ returns the closest such pair. The minimum of the three is the closest pair overall.

> [!exam] How this is graded
> Recursive-algorithm rubric ([[0-toolkit/06-how-exams-are-graded#11-recursive-algorithms|§11]]): **3** for what $\textsf{ClosestPair}(lo, hi)$ returns, in terms of its parameters (not "split the points in half and recurse"); **1** for the base case; **2** for the two halves split at $a = P[m].x$; **2** for combining with $\textsf{IWalkTheLine}$ and the minimum; **2** for the recurrence and its solution, one point each. Re-implementing $\textsf{IWalkTheLine}$ is explicitly not wanted.
