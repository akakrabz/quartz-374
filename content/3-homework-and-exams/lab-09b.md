> [!abstract] In one breath
> All four problems reduce to a standard problem on a **dag** you build yourself. Problem 1: "$A$ finished ahead of $B$ in some game" becomes an edge $A\to B$, and a consistent ranking is exactly a topological order — topological sort returns one or finds a cycle that proves none exists, in $O(n+m)$ time. Problem 2: orient every teleport-way away from home; distance from home grows along every edge, so there is no cycle, and Judy's best tour is a **longest path in a dag** from $s$ to a neighbor of $s$, plus the teleport home: $O(n+m)$. Problem 3: Judy must also remember how many paid teleports she has used — put the count in the vertex: three copies of the dag, still a dag, still $O(n+m)$. Problem 4 is genuinely harder, because a path cannot remember which galaxies it has already counted. But a tour is three climbing chains, and if three tokens climb them together, **always moving the lowest token**, a token can re-enter a counted galaxy only when another token is standing on it right now. Tuples of token positions (plus where the first two chains must end) form a dag with $O(n^5)$ vertices, and its longest path is the answer, in $O(n^4 m)$ time; we do not know whether that can be improved.

## Problem 1 — A consistent overall ranking

> [!question] Problem 1
> A Snakes and Ladders competition has $n$ participants. Every game involves three players, who finish first, second and third. A player may take part in any number of games (possibly none), not necessarily the same number as the others. After $m$ games you must produce an overall ranking of all $n$ players, with one rule: if player $A$ finished ahead of player $B$ in at least one game, then $A$ must be ranked ahead of $B$ overall. Given the players and the result of each of the $m$ games, describe and analyze an algorithm that produces an overall ranking consistent with all the games, or correctly reports that no such ranking exists.

> [!hint]- Hint
> "$A$ must be ranked ahead of $B$" is a constraint between two players: make it a directed edge. Which orderings of a directed graph put every edge forward?

> [!solution]- Solution
> **The idea.** A game won by $a$, with $b$ second and $c$ third, demands $a$ ahead of $b$, $a$ ahead of $c$ and $b$ ahead of $c$. Draw every demand as an edge from the player who must be ahead. A ranking that respects all the demands is then an order of the vertices with every edge pointing forward — a **topological order** ([[2-algorithms/06-depth-first-search#63-topological-sort|§6.3]]), which exists exactly when the graph has no directed cycle.
>
> - **Vertices.** The $n$ players.
> - **Edges.** Directed: for every game with result $(a, b, c)$, the two edges $a\to b$ and $b\to c$. ($a\to c$ is not needed: an order with $a$ before $b$ and $b$ before $c$ has $a$ before $c$.) At most $2m$ edges. The graph may have cycles — that is the question.
> - **Values.** None.
> - **Relationship.** A ranking is consistent with the games if and only if it is a topological order of this graph. If it is consistent, every edge $a\to b$ or $b\to c$ comes from a game in which the tail finished ahead, so the edge points forward. Conversely, in a topological order every game $(a,b,c)$ has $a$ before $b$ before $c$, which settles all three of its pairs. So a consistent ranking exists if and only if the graph is a dag: a directed cycle $p_1\to p_2\to\cdots\to p_k\to p_1$ would need $p_1$ ranked ahead of itself.
> - **Algorithm.** Topological sort by depth-first search ([[2-algorithms/06-depth-first-search#63-topological-sort|§6.3]]: reverse postorder, with three vertex states so that an edge into an **active** vertex reveals a cycle, [[2-algorithms/06-depth-first-search#62-detecting-cycles|§6.2]]). If it finds a cycle, report that no consistent ranking exists; otherwise output its order, first place first.
> - **Time.** Building the graph scans the $m$ games once: $O(n+m)$. Topological sort takes $O(V+E) = O(n + m)$. In all, $O(n+m)$.
>
> Any other topological sort works too, for instance repeatedly removing a player with no incoming edge (a source).
>
> **Example.** Players $A$–$F$ and games $(A,B,C)$, $(B,D,E)$, $(F,A,D)$ give the edges $A\to B$, $B\to C$, $B\to D$, $D\to E$, $F\to A$, $A\to D$. Depth-first search in alphabetical order finishes $C, E, D, B, A, F$, so the ranking is $F, A, B, D, E, C$, and each game's order appears in it. Add a fourth game $(E, F, C)$: its edge $E\to F$ closes the cycle $F\to A\to B\to D\to E\to F$, and no consistent ranking exists.
>
> **Answer.** Edges $a\to b$ and $b\to c$ for every game $(a,b,c)$; a topological sort of this graph is a consistent ranking, and a cycle proves that none exists; $O(n+m)$ time.

## Problem 2 — Judy's tour

> [!question] Problem 2
> There are $n$ galaxies connected by $m$ intergalactic teleport-ways; each joins two galaxies and can be traversed in both directions. The company that runs them charges nothing for teleporting *farther* from one's home galaxy, but teleporting *toward* one's home galaxy is prohibitively expensive. Judy wants a sabbatical tour that visits as many galaxies as possible, starting at her home galaxy. To save money, she wants every teleport to take her away from her home galaxy, except for the very last teleport home. Describe and analyze an algorithm to compute the maximum number of galaxies Judy can visit. The input is an undirected graph $G$ with $n$ vertices and $m$ edges describing the teleport-ways, an integer $1\le s\le n$ identifying Judy's home galaxy, and an array $D[1\,..\,n]$ containing the distances of the galaxies from $s$. *(Handout's hint: longest paths in a directed acyclic graph can be computed in linear time; see §6.4 of the book.)*

> [!note] How we read Problems 2–4
> - **Away and toward.** A teleport along the teleport-way $uv$ from $u$ to $v$ goes **away** from home if $D[v] > D[u]$ and **toward** home if $D[v] < D[u]$. A teleport-way between two galaxies at the same distance is neither, and Judy never uses one: every teleport of her plan goes away from home, except the paid ones of Problems 3–4 and the last one.
> - **$D$ is distance in space, not in $G$.** We read $D[v]$ as the physical distance of galaxy $v$ from home, any number with $D[s] = 0 < D[v]$ for $v\ne s$. The teleport network says nothing about it, which is why it is part of the input. It cannot mean the number of teleports from $s$: for those breadth-first distances, $\lvert D[u] - D[v]\rvert\le 1$ along every teleport-way, so every away teleport raises $D$ by exactly 1; the last teleport home leaves from a neighbor of $s$, at distance 1, so Judy could never get past her first stop — every tour would be $s\to v\to s$, the answer would always be 2 (1 if $s$ has no neighbor), and the hint about longest paths would be pointless. All our algorithms work for every array $D$ with $D[s] = 0 < D[v]$, that degenerate one included.
> - **The last teleport** goes along a teleport-way into $s$, from a neighbor of $s$, and ends the tour: Judy arrives home once, at the end.
> - **What we count.** The galaxies of the tour, home counted once. A tour of $t$ teleports visits $t$ galaxies in this sense, because each teleport arrives at one and the last arrives home. If $s$ has no teleport-way, Judy cannot travel at all, and the answer is 1.

> [!hint]- Hint
> Orient every teleport-way away from home. Distance from home grows along every edge, so the directed graph has no cycle. What does Judy's tour look like in it?

> [!solution]- Solution
> **The idea.** Every free teleport increases $D$, so orient each teleport-way from its nearer end to its farther end. The resulting directed graph has no cycle: $D$ would have to increase all the way around. Judy's tour is a path in this graph that starts at $s$ and stops at some neighbor of $s$, from which she teleports home, and she wants as many galaxies on it as possible — a **longest path in a dag**, the model problem of [[2-algorithms/06-depth-first-search#64-memoization-and-dynamic-programming|§6.4]]. To give the path a single target, add a vertex $s'$ standing for "home again".
>
> - **Vertices.** The $n$ galaxies, plus one new vertex $s'$ that represents Judy back at home at the end of her tour: $n+1$ vertices.
> - **Edges.** Directed: $u\to v$ for every teleport-way $uv$ with $D[u] < D[v]$ (a free teleport away from home), and $v\to s'$ for every neighbor $v$ of $s$ (the last teleport home). This graph is a **dag**: $D$ strictly increases along every edge $u\to v$ between galaxies, and $s'$ has no outgoing edges.
> - **Values.** Every edge has length 1: one teleport.
> - **Relationship.** Tours with $t$ teleports correspond one-to-one to paths with $t$ edges from $s$ to $s'$: the tour $s = w_0\to w_1\to\cdots\to w_{t-1}\to s$, whose teleports before the last all go away from home, is the path $s\to w_1\to\cdots\to w_{t-1}\to s'$; conversely, a path from $s$ to $s'$ consists of away teleports followed by one edge $w_{t-1}\to s'$ from a neighbor of $s$. $D$ strictly increases along $w_0, \ldots, w_{t-1}$, so these $t$ galaxies are distinct, and the tour visits exactly $t$ galaxies. So the answer is the length of the longest path from $s$ to $s'$, or 1 if there is no such path (when $s$ has no neighbor).
> - **Algorithm.** Longest path in a dag ([[2-algorithms/06-depth-first-search#dynamic-programming-in-dags|§6.4]]) with target $s'$: $\textsf{LLP}(v)$ is the length of the longest path from $v$ to $s'$, $\textsf{LLP}(s') = 0$, $\textsf{LLP}(v) = \max\{1 + \textsf{LLP}(w) \mid v\to w\}$ with $\max\varnothing = -\infty$, evaluated by memoized depth-first search from $s$ (that is, in postorder). Return $\max\{1, \textsf{LLP}(s)\}$.
> - **Time.** The dag has $n+1$ vertices and at most $m + \deg(s)\le 2m$ edges; one pass over the adjacency lists, comparing $D$ values, builds it in $O(n+m)$ time. The longest path takes $O(V+E) = O(n+m)$. In all, $O(n+m)$.
>
> The topological order comes from the depth-first search; sorting the galaxies by $D$ would also give one, but sorting arbitrary numbers costs $O(n\log n)$, more than the linear time the hint promises.
>
> **Example.** In the figure, the free teleports are the ten teleport-ways oriented to the right: $s\to c$, $s\to h$, $b\to c$, $c\to g$, $g\to h$, $f\to g$, $d\to f$, $e\to h$, $a\to d$, $a\to h$; the neighbors of $s$ are $c$ and $h$, so the dag also has $c\to s'$ and $h\to s'$. Galaxies $a$, $b$, $d$, $e$, $f$ cannot be reached from $s$ at all, and the longest path from $s$ to $s'$ is $s\to c\to g\to h\to s'$: the answer is 4, for the tour $s\to c\to g\to h\to s$.
>
> **Answer.** The dag of away-teleports plus an edge $v\to s'$ from every neighbor $v$ of $s$; the answer is the longest path from $s$ to $s'$ (or 1 if $s$ has no neighbor); $O(n+m)$ time.

> [!trap] Traps in Problem 2
> - **Longest path is NP-hard in general graphs**; it is easy here only because orienting the teleport-ways away from home leaves no cycle. Building that dag is the whole reduction.
> - **Off by one.** The edge into $s'$ *is* the teleport home, so the path length already counts the home galaxy: the answer is $\textsf{LLP}(s)$, not $\textsf{LLP}(s) + 1$.
> - **"Try all paths from $s$ with depth-first search"** is exponential; the dynamic program visits every vertex and edge once.

## Problem 3 — Two paid teleports, every visit counts (harder)

> [!question] Problem 3
> Just before her tour, Judy wins the space lottery, which gives her just enough money for **two** teleports toward her home galaxy. Describe and analyze a new algorithm to compute the maximum number of galaxies Judy can visit; if she visits the same galaxy twice, that counts as two visits.

> [!note] How we read Problem 3
> The lottery pays for two teleports toward home **in addition** to the last teleport home, which Problem 2 already allowed. So a tour is a walk from $s$ back to $s$ whose teleports all go away from home, except at most two **paid** teleports toward home and the last one. A paid teleport lands on a galaxy other than $s$, since arriving home ends the tour. Visits are counted with multiplicity, so a tour of $t$ teleports makes $t$ visits (the last one at home). If you read the lottery as paying for two toward-home teleports *including* the last one, Judy has one paid teleport: replace 2 by $k = 1$ below; nothing else changes.

> [!hint]- Hint
> Judy now has to remember one more number as she travels: how many paid teleports she has used. Put that number in the vertex.

> [!solution]- Solution
> **The idea.** In Problem 2, the state of Judy's trip was just her galaxy. Now it is her galaxy *and* the number of paid teleports already used — the "state in the vertices" trick of [[2-algorithms/05-basic-graph-algorithms#state-in-the-vertices-walks-of-even-length|§5.7]]. Make three copies of the galaxies, one for each count 0, 1, 2. Free teleports stay in their copy and go away from home; paid teleports go toward home and move to the next copy. Within a copy $D$ grows along every edge, and between copies we only move forward, so the graph is still a dag and Problem 2's longest path applies unchanged. A path may pass the same galaxy in two different copies; that is a galaxy visited twice, which this problem counts twice.
>
> - **Vertices.** A vertex $(v, j)$ for every galaxy $v$ and every $j\in\{0, 1, 2\}$, representing "Judy is at $v$ and has used $j$ paid teleports"; plus $s'$, "Judy is home again": $3n+1$ vertices.
> - **Edges.** Directed:
>   - $(u, j)\to(v, j)$ for every teleport-way $uv$ with $D[u] < D[v]$ and every $j$ (a free teleport);
>   - $(u, j)\to(v, j+1)$ for every teleport-way $uv$ with $D[u] > D[v]$ and $v\ne s$, for $j\in\{0, 1\}$ (a paid teleport);
>   - $(v, j)\to s'$ for every neighbor $v$ of $s$ and every $j$ (the last teleport home).
>
>   It is a **dag**: along every edge between copies $j$ grows, and along every edge inside a copy $j$ stays and $D$ grows, so the pair $(j, D)$ increases lexicographically along every path; $s'$ has no outgoing edges.
> - **Values.** Every edge has length 1.
> - **Relationship.** Tours with $t$ teleports, at most two of them paid, correspond one-to-one to paths with $t$ edges from $(s, 0)$ to $s'$: follow the tour and record in the second coordinate how many paid teleports it has made so far. Conversely, the first coordinates of such a path spell a tour whose paid teleports are exactly its edges between copies — at most two, because $j\le 2$. The number of visits is $t$. So the answer is the length of the longest path from $(s, 0)$ to $s'$, or 1 if there is none.
> - **Algorithm.** Longest path in a dag ([[2-algorithms/06-depth-first-search#dynamic-programming-in-dags|§6.4]]), $\textsf{LLP}$ with target $s'$ as in Problem 2, evaluated by memoized depth-first search from $(s, 0)$. Return $\max\{1, \textsf{LLP}((s,0))\}$.
> - **Time.** $3n+1$ vertices and at most $3m + 2m + 3\deg(s)\le 8m$ edges, built in $O(n+m)$ time; the longest path takes $O(n+m)$. In all, $O(n+m)$. With $k$ paid teleports the same construction has $(k+1)n + 1$ vertices and $O((k+1)m)$ edges: $O((k+1)(n+m))$ time.
>
> **Example.** In the figure, two paid teleports allow 14 visits: $s\to c\to g\to h$, then the paid teleport $h\dashrightarrow a$, then $a\to d\to f\to g\to h$, the same paid teleport again, the same climb again, and home: $h$ is visited three times. With one paid teleport the best is $s\to c\to g\to h\dashrightarrow a\to d\to f\to g\to h\to s$, 9 visits.
>
> If a paid teleport could also take Judy home in the middle of her tour (after which she sets out again), add the edges $(v, j)\to(s, j+1)$ for every neighbor $v$ of $s$; the graph stays a dag and nothing else changes.
>
> **Answer.** Three copies of Problem 2's dag, linked by the paid teleports toward home; the longest path from $(s, 0)$ to $s'$; $O(n+m)$ time.

## Problem 4 — Counting distinct galaxies (harder, starred)

> [!question] Problem 4 (starred)
> Judy replies that she *can* see a whole galaxy in one visit, since they all look alike. Describe and analyze an algorithm to compute the maximum number of **distinct** galaxies Judy can visit, still with two paid teleports toward home. She may visit the same galaxy more than once, but only the first visit counts.

> [!hint]- Hint
> A tour is three climbing chains joined by two paid teleports, and its value is the number of galaxies on the chains together. Let three tokens climb the three chains *at the same time*, always moving the token on the lowest galaxy (smallest $D$). When can a token step onto a galaxy that has already been counted?

> [!solution]- Solution
> **Why Problem 3's graph fails.** A path in the layered dag cannot tell whether it has been to a galaxy before: in the example, its best path visits $h$ three times, 14 visits among 9 galaxies. Putting the set of visited galaxies into the vertex would make the graph exponential. We need a small summary of the past.
>
> **The shape of a tour.** A tour with exactly two paid teleports looks like
>
> $$
> s \leadsto x_1 \dashrightarrow y_1 \leadsto x_2 \dashrightarrow y_2 \leadsto x_3 \to s :
> $$
>
> three **chains** $P_0 = s\leadsto x_1$, $P_1 = y_1\leadsto x_2$ and $P_2 = y_2\leadsto x_3$, each a path of free teleports — a path in Problem 2's dag $H$ of away-teleports, so $D$ increases along it — joined by the paid teleports $x_1\dashrightarrow y_1$ and $x_2\dashrightarrow y_2$ (teleport-ways with $D[y_i] < D[x_i]$ and $y_i\ne s$), where $x_3$ is a neighbor of $s$. Its value is the number of galaxies on $P_0\cup P_1\cup P_2$, home included. A chain may be a single galaxy (two paid teleports in a row), and a tour with fewer paid teleports has fewer chains.
>
> **The sweep.** Number the galaxies $1, \ldots, n$ in increasing order of $D$, breaking ties arbitrarily, and call the number a galaxy's **rank**; every edge of $H$ goes from a smaller rank to a larger one. Put token $i$ on the first galaxy of chain $P_i$ and move the tokens up their chains one step at a time, **always moving the token on the lowest-ranked galaxy** — the **leader** (among tokens on the same galaxy, the one with the smallest index). A token at the end of its chain is removed when it leads. The point: a token only moves when no token is below it, so every galaxy the tokens have left behind lies below all of them. When the leader climbs, it lands above all of that, so **the galaxy it lands on has been counted before if and only if another token is standing on it right now**. The three current positions, plus where chains 0 and 1 must end (to connect to the paid teleports), are all we need to remember.
>
> - **Vertices.** A source $\sigma$, a sink $\tau$, and all tuples $(p_0, p_1, p_2, t_0, t_1)$ with $p_i\in V\cup\{\top\}$ and $t_0, t_1\in V$: token $i$ stands on galaxy $p_i$, or has finished its chain if $p_i = \top$; chains 0 and 1 must end at $t_0$ and $t_1$, the galaxies the two paid teleports leave from. That is $(n+1)^3n^2 + 2$ vertices. Token $i$ is **active** if $p_i\ne\top$; a tuple's **leader** is its active token on the lowest-ranked galaxy (smallest index among ties).
> - **Edges.** Directed. Out of a tuple whose leader is token $i$ on galaxy $u$:
>   - *finish:* if $i\in\{0,1\}$ and $u = t_i$, or $i = 2$ and $u$ is a neighbor of $s$, one edge to the same tuple with $p_i$ replaced by $\top$;
>   - *climb:* unless $i\in\{0,1\}$ and $u = t_i$, for every edge $u\to w$ of $H$, an edge to the same tuple with $p_i$ replaced by $w$;
>   - *start:* $\sigma\to(s, y_1, y_2, x_1, x_2)$ for every two paid teleports $x_1\dashrightarrow y_1$ and $x_2\dashrightarrow y_2$ (teleport-ways with $D[y] < D[x]$ and $y\ne s$; the same one may be used twice);
>   - *end:* $(\top, \top, \top, t_0, t_1)\to\tau$ for all $t_0, t_1$.
>
>   It is a **dag**: a finish or climb edge raises the rank of one token (count $\top$ as rank $n+1$) and changes nothing else, so the sum of the tokens' ranks grows along every such edge.
> - **Values.** Edge weights: a climb onto $w$ weighs 0 if another active token stands on $w$, and 1 otherwise; a start edge weighs the number of distinct galaxies among $s, y_1, y_2$; finish and end edges weigh 0.
> - **Relationship.** Paths from $\sigma$ to $\tau$ correspond to tours with exactly two paid teleports, and the weight of a path is the number of distinct galaxies of its tour (lemma below). So the longest path from $\sigma$ to $\tau$ is $A_2$, the best value with two paid teleports. The same construction with two tokens — tuples $(p_0, p_1, t_0)$, where chain 1 ends at a neighbor of $s$ — gives $A_1$ for one paid teleport, and Problem 2 gives $A_0$. The answer is $\max\{1, A_0, A_1, A_2\}$.
> - **Algorithm.** Longest path in a dag ([[2-algorithms/06-depth-first-search#dynamic-programming-in-dags|§6.4]]) from $\sigma$ to $\tau$, by memoized depth-first search from $\sigma$, with the tuples' values in a 5-dimensional array; the edges out of a tuple are generated from the adjacency lists when needed.
> - **Time.** There are $O(n^5)$ tuples. A tuple has at most $1 + \text{out-degree}_H(u)$ outgoing edges, $u$ its leader's galaxy; since the leader is one of three tokens, summing over all tuples gives at most $3n^2(n+1)^2m = O(n^4m)$ climb edges, plus $O(n^5)$ finish and end edges and at most $m^2$ start edges (each teleport-way is one paid teleport, from its farther to its nearer end). So $A_2$ takes $O(n^5 + n^4m)$ time and $O(n^5)$ space; $A_1$ takes $O(n^2(n+m))$ and $A_0$ $O(n+m)$. Discarding first the galaxies that are not connected to $s$ leaves at most $m+1$ galaxies, so the total is $O(n^4m)$.
>
> If a paid teleport may also land at home (the variant at the end of Problem 3), let $y_1$ and $y_2$ be $s$ as well in the start edges; nothing else changes.
>
> **Example.** In the figure, galaxies $a$, $b$ and $e$ have no neighbor closer to home, so Judy can only arrive there by a paid teleport, and with two paid teleports she misses one of them: the best is 8 of the 9 galaxies, for example $s\to c\to g\to h\dashrightarrow e\to h\dashrightarrow a\to d\to f\to g\to h\to s$. Its chains are $s\,c\,g\,h$, $e\,h$ and $a\,d\,f\,g\,h$, with $t_0 = t_1 = h$; ranks follow the alphabet ($s$ is 1, $a$ is 2, …, $h$ is 9). The start edge weighs $\lvert\{s, e, a\}\rvert = 3$, and the sweep then makes these moves:
>
> | move | leader | step | weight | tokens after |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | 1 | 0 | $s\to c$ | 1 | $c, e, a$ |
> | 2 | 2 | $a\to d$ | 1 | $c, e, d$ |
> | 3 | 0 | $c\to g$ | 1 | $g, e, d$ |
> | 4 | 2 | $d\to f$ | 1 | $g, e, f$ |
> | 5 | 1 | $e\to h$ | 1 | $g, h, f$ |
> | 6 | 2 | $f\to g$ | 0 | $g, h, g$ |
> | 7 | 0 | $g\to h$ | 0 | $h, h, g$ |
> | 8 | 2 | $g\to h$ | 0 | $h, h, h$ |
> | 9–11 | 0, 1, 2 | finish | 0 | $\top, \top, \top$ |
>
> Moves 6–8 weigh 0 because the leader lands where another token stands ($g$ holds token 0, then $h$ holds token 1), and the tokens finish because $h = t_0 = t_1$ and $h$ is a neighbor of $s$. Total $3 + 5 = 8$. With one paid teleport the best is 7, for example $s\to c\to g\to h\dashrightarrow a\to d\to f\to g\to h\to s$.
>
> **What we do not know.** The sweep has to remember up to five galaxies at once — three token positions and the two chain ends $t_0, t_1$ — and we found no argument that lets it forget one: in about one of every seven of our small random test instances, *every* best tour has a moment when three chains are in progress and both paid teleports are still pending. So we do not know whether $O(n^4m)$ can be improved. The construction works the same way for any fixed number $k$ of paid teleports ($k+1$ tokens, $k$ chain ends, $n^{O(k)}$ time); we have not studied the problem when $k$ is part of the input.
>
> **Answer.** Tuples of three token positions and two chain ends; each edge moves the lowest token one step up the away-dag, weighing 1 when it lands on a galaxy no other token occupies; the longest path from $\sigma$ to $\tau$, together with the cases of one and no paid teleport, is the answer, in $O(n^4m)$ time and $O(n^5)$ space.

> [!proof]- Lemma — paths of the token dag are tours, and their weights count distinct galaxies (click to expand)
> Fix a path that starts at $\sigma$. Call a galaxy **seen** if some token has stood on it so far, and **occupied** if an active token stands on it now.
>
> **Invariant: every galaxy that is seen but not occupied has smaller rank than every occupied galaxy.** Right after the start edge, the seen galaxies are exactly the occupied ones. A climb moves the leader from $u$, the lowest occupied galaxy, to $w$ with a larger rank. Afterwards every occupied galaxy is ranked at least as high as $u$, so the galaxies that were seen but unoccupied before, all ranked below $u$, stay below every occupied galaxy. The only galaxy that may newly become unoccupied is $u$ itself, and it is ranked below $w$ and below every other occupied galaxy (those are at or above $u$, and not $u$). A finish edge is the same without $w$. So the invariant survives every edge.
>
> **Counting.** When the leader climbs from $u$ to $w$, $w$ is ranked above $u$, the lowest occupied galaxy, so by the invariant $w$ is not seen-but-unoccupied: $w$ has been seen if and only if another active token occupies it — exactly when the climb weighs 0. So every edge weighs the number of galaxies it makes seen (the start edge sees $s, y_1, y_2$), and the weight of a path is the number of galaxies seen along it.
>
> **Paths give tours.** On a path from $\sigma$ to $\tau$, token 0 climbs a path of $H$ from $s$ that can only end at $t_0$ (it must finish there and cannot climb past it), token 1 one from $y_1$ to $t_1$, and token 2 one from $y_2$ to a neighbor $x_3$ of $s$. With the paid teleports $t_0\dashrightarrow y_1$ and $t_1\dashrightarrow y_2$ of the start edge and the last teleport $x_3\to s$, these chains form a tour with two paid teleports, and its galaxies are exactly the seen ones. The tour enters $s$ only at the end: $s$ has the smallest $D$, so no edge of $H$ enters it, and $y_1, y_2\ne s$.
>
> **Tours give paths.** Given a tour with chains $P_0, P_1, P_2$ as above, start with the edge $\sigma\to(s, y_1, y_2, x_1, x_2)$ and repeatedly let the leader take the next step of its own chain, or finish if it stands on the last galaxy of its chain. Every step is an edge of the token dag (a chain's last galaxy is $t_0$, $t_1$ or a neighbor of $s$, and a chain never passes its own end, because $D$ increases along it), so this is a path to $\tau$, and by the counting its weight is the number of galaxies on the chains.

> [!trap] Traps in Problem 4
> - **Only the lowest token may move.** If any token may move, a token can climb past a galaxy that a lower token reaches later, and the double count goes unnoticed; moving the *highest* token instead gives wrong answers on most random instances.
> - **Problem 3's answer capped at $n$ is not this answer.** In the example it is $\min\{14, 9\} = 9$, but only 8 galaxies can be visited.
> - **The chain ends must be remembered.** The paid teleport $x_1\dashrightarrow y_1$ connects the *end* of chain 0 with the *start* of chain 1, which the sweep reaches at different times; that is why $t_0$ and $t_1$ are in the tuple.

> [!exam] On the exam
> Problems 1–3 are exam-style graph reductions, graded by the [[0-toolkit/06-how-exams-are-graded#13-graph-reductions|graph-reduction rubric]]: the graph (3 points, with "directed" said explicitly), the problem on it — "longest path in this dag from … to …" with the off-by-one settled (3), the standard algorithm as a black box — topological sort or longest path in a dag, never a modified search (2), and the time in $n$ and $m$ (2). Saying *why* the graph is a dag is part of the edges. Problem 4 is well beyond exam level; its lesson for the exam is that a longest path counts steps, not distinct vertices.

Problems 2 and 4 are Exercise 6.22(a) and (b) of Erickson's *Algorithms*, where (b) is marked as particularly challenging; Problem 3 is the lab's intermediate version. `tools/checks/labs_09b.py` implements every algorithm exactly as stated here and compares it with exhaustive search — over all $n!$ rankings for Problem 1 (every input with 4 players and at most 2 games, and 1500 random inputs), and over all tours for Problems 2–4 (every graph with at most 4 galaxies with every $D$ in $\{1,2,3\}$, and 900 random connected graphs with 5–9 galaxies, with distances that tie, distinct real distances and breadth-first hop counts), for one and for two paid teleports and for the variant in which a paid teleport may land at home. It also checks the sizes of the constructed graphs, the degenerate hop-count case, that Problem 4's answer does not depend on how ties in $D$ are broken in the ranks, every number and tour of the examples, the sweep table, and the traps. The running-time bounds are proved above and only spot-checked by counting tuples and edges on random graphs.
