> [!abstract] In one breath
> Six questions about one puzzle, all answered the same way: build a graph whose vertices remember exactly what the rule needs, and run breadth-first search. Problem 1 (the book's Exercise 5.11) needs only the square the token is on: BFS in the graph of moves, $O(n^2)$. Alternating moves need the axis of the last move, "a multiple of 5" needs the number of moves mod 5, "no U-turns" needs the direction of the last move — 2, 5 and 4 copies of the board, still $O(n^2)$. Increasing lengths seem to need the length of the last move ($n^3$ vertices), but that length is the label of the square the token came from, so the rule can be checked on each edge instead: $O(n^2)$, and the graph is a dag. Palindromic lengths are checked from both ends at once: a vertex is a pair of squares, one for a token replaying the solution from the start and one for a token replaying it backward from the goal — $n^4$ vertices, $O(n^4)$ time. On the handout's maze the six answers are 8, none, 10, none, none, none.

> [!recipe] What every answer states — the handout's list
> Whenever a solution uses a standard graph algorithm, the handout requires the following (and recommends writing it as a bulleted list):
> - **Vertices** — what are they, and what does each vertex represent?
> - **Edges** — what are they, are they directed or undirected, and, if it matters to the algorithm, is the graph a dag?
> - **Values** — if the vertices or edges carry values, what are they?
> - **Relationship** — what is the precise relationship between the graph and the stated problem?
> - **Algorithm** — which standard graph algorithm, exactly how it is invoked, and exactly how its results are used?
> - **Running time** — of the entire algorithm, *including building the graph*, as a function of the original input's parameters?
>
> It also reminds you that the graph to search is not necessarily the one the input suggests — here the obvious graph of squares and moves answers only Problem 1. The list is the course's graph-reduction rubric in another order (graph 3 points, problem on the graph 3, algorithm 2, time 2: [[0-toolkit/06-how-exams-are-graded#13-graph-reductions|how exams are graded §13]]; template in [[0-toolkit/02-proof-templates#22-solving-a-problem-by-graph-reduction|proof templates §22]]). Every solution below uses exactly these six bullets.

> [!note] Conventions for all six problems
> - Squares are $(i,j)$ with $1\le i,j\le n$, row 1 at the top; the token starts on $(1,1)$ and must reach $(n,n)$. A **move** from $(i,j)$ goes $k = M[i,j]$ squares up, down, left or right, to $(i-k,j)$, $(i+k,j)$, $(i,j-k)$ or $(i,j+k)$, whichever lie on the board. Its **length** is $k$ — the label of the square it *starts* from — and its **direction** is up, down, left or right; up and down moves are **vertical**, left and right moves **horizontal**.
> - A **solution** is a sequence of moves taking the token from $(1,1)$ to $(n,n)$. It may pass through $(n,n)$ before its last move: nothing in the handout forbids it. If you read the rules as ending the game the moment the token arrives (the figure shows the goal as ★, without a number), give $(n,n)$ the label $n$, so that no move leaves it; every reduction below then works unchanged. On the handout's maze the answers are the same under both readings and for every label of the goal.
> - For $n = 1$ the token starts on the goal, and the empty sequence — 0 moves — is a valid solution of every variant. The reductions of Problems 1, 3 and 6 return 0 by themselves; for Problems 2, 4 and 5 return 0 directly and assume $n\ge 2$ in the construction.

## Problem 1 — Fewest moves

> [!question] Problem 1
> A **number maze** is an $n\times n$ grid of positive integers, given as an array $M[1\,..\,n, 1\,..\,n]$. A token starts on the top-left square, and the goal is to move it to the bottom-right square. In each turn the token moves up, down, left or right by exactly the number on its current square — from a square labeled 3, three squares in one of the four directions — and it may not leave the board. Describe and analyze an efficient algorithm that returns the minimum number of moves needed to solve a given number maze, or correctly reports that the maze has no solution. For the $5\times 5$ maze of the handout (★ marks the goal)
> $$
> \begin{matrix} 3 & 5 & 7 & 4 & 6\\ 5 & 3 & 1 & 5 & 3\\ 2 & 8 & 3 & 1 & 4\\ 4 & 5 & 7 & 2 & 3\\ 3 & 1 & 3 & 2 & \bigstar \end{matrix}
> $$
> the algorithm should return 8, the length of the solution right 3, down 4, left 2, right 1, up 3, left 1, right 3, down 3 — which is in fact the only shortest solution.

> [!hint]- Hint
> One vertex per square, one edge per legal move. Is a move from $p$ to $q$ always matched by a move from $q$ to $p$?

> [!solution]- Solution
> **The idea.** A position of the game is nothing but the square the token is on, and a move takes it from one square to another — so the game *is* a graph, and a solution with $m$ moves is a walk with $m$ edges from the start to the goal. Fewest moves means fewest edges, which is what breadth-first search computes. This is the book's Exercise 5.11, solved the same way on [[2-algorithms/05-basic-graph-algorithms-exercises#exercise-511--number-maze|the Chapter 5 exercises page]], with a figure of the shortest solution and of every square's BFS distance.
>
> - **Vertices.** The $n^2$ squares $(i,j)$, $1\le i,j\le n$. Vertex $(i,j)$ represents "the token is on square $(i,j)$".
> - **Edges.** A **directed** edge $(i,j)\to(i',j')$ for every move from $(i,j)$ to $(i',j')$: to $(i\pm k, j)$ and $(i, j\pm k)$ with $k = M[i,j]$, when they lie on the board — at most four edges out of each vertex. Directed, because a move's length is the label of the square it starts from, so the reverse move usually does not exist. The graph is usually not a dag; BFS does not care.
> - **Values.** None: the graph is unweighted, since every move counts once.
> - **Relationship.** Moves are edges, so the sequences of $m$ moves from $(1,1)$ to $(n,n)$ are exactly the walks with $m$ edges from $(1,1)$ to $(n,n)$. The answer is the **shortest-path distance** (number of edges) from $(1,1)$ to $(n,n)$, and the maze has no solution exactly when $(n,n)$ is not reachable from $(1,1)$.
> - **Algorithm.** Breadth-first search from $(1,1)$, used as a black box: it returns the distance of every vertex reachable from $(1,1)$. Return the distance of $(n,n)$, or "no solution" if the search does not reach it. BFS is the cheapest standard algorithm for fewest edges: whatever-first search answers only reachability, and Dijkstra's algorithm would spend $O(E\log V)$ time on weights that are all 1 (the rubric takes a point for a slower algorithm than necessary).
> - **Running time.** The graph has $V = n^2$ vertices and $E\le 4n^2$ edges and is built in $O(n^2)$ time by listing the four candidate moves of each square; BFS runs in $O(V + E) = O(n^2)$ time. Total: $O(n^2)$, linear in the size of the input.
>
> On the handout's maze BFS returns 8; no other sequence of 8 moves reaches the goal.
>
> **Answer.** BFS from $(1,1)$ in the directed graph of moves; $O(n^2)$ time.

> [!intuition] Problems 2–6: put the memory into the vertices
> In each variant the same moves exist, but whether a move may come next — or whether the token may stop — depends on the moves made so far. A search cannot be told to remember things; instead, make each vertex a pair (square, *memory*), where the memory is the least information about the past that decides which moves may follow and whether the solution may end here. The edges are the moves the memory allows, and each one updates the memory. Then the walks from the start vertex are exactly the valid move sequences, and BFS still counts moves. The graph has one copy of the board per value of the memory, so the memory must be small. This is the parity trick of [[2-algorithms/05-basic-graph-algorithms#state-in-the-vertices-walks-of-even-length|§5.7 (walks of even length)]], also used in [[2-algorithms/05-basic-graph-algorithms-exercises#exercise-514--walks-of-length-divisible-by-3|Ex. 5.14]] and [[2-algorithms/05-basic-graph-algorithms-exercises#exercise-515--no-three-edges-of-one-color-in-a-row|Ex. 5.15]]; see [[concepts/graph-reductions]].
>
> | problem | rule | memory besides the square | vertices | time |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | 1 | none | nothing | $n^2$ | $O(n^2)$ |
> | 2 | alternate horizontal and vertical | the axis of the last move | $2n^2 + 1$ | $O(n^2)$ |
> | 3 | a multiple of 5 moves | the number of moves mod 5 | $5n^2$ | $O(n^2)$ |
> | 4 | no U-turns | the direction of the last move | $4n^2 + 1$ | $O(n^2)$ |
> | 5 | increasing lengths | nothing: the rule is checked on each edge (or the last length: $n^3$ vertices) | $n^2 + 1$ | $O(n^2)$ |
> | 6 | palindromic lengths | the square of a second token that replays the solution backward from the goal | $n^4$ | $O(n^4)$ |
>
> The "$+1$" is an extra start vertex (before the first move there is no last move) or, in Problem 5, an extra end vertex.

## Problem 2 — Alternating horizontal and vertical moves

> [!question] Problem 2
> Now a sequence of moves is **valid** only if its moves alternate between horizontal and vertical: it never contains two horizontal moves in a row or two vertical moves in a row. Describe and analyze an algorithm that returns the minimum number of moves in a valid solution of a given number maze, or correctly reports that no valid solution exists. (The handout points out that the 8-move solution of the example is not valid for this or any of the following variants.)

> [!hint]- Hint
> Which moves are allowed next depends on one bit of the past. Which bit? And what is that bit before the first move?

> [!solution]- Solution
> **The idea.** Whether a move is allowed depends only on whether the previous move was horizontal or vertical. So make two copies of every square — "the token arrived here by a horizontal move" and "… by a vertical move" — and let the edges out of each copy be only the moves of the other kind. Before the first move there is no previous move, and every move is allowed, so the start gets a vertex of its own. (It cannot be one of the two copies of $(1,1)$: each copy allows moves of one kind only, and giving one of them every move would break the rule when the token comes back to $(1,1)$ later. Running BFS from each of the two copies and keeping the smaller distance works too.)
>
> - **Vertices.** A start vertex $s$, meaning "the token is on $(1,1)$ and has not moved yet", and the $2n^2$ triples $(i,j,a)$ with $1\le i,j\le n$ and $a\in\{\textsf{horiz}, \textsf{vert}\}$, meaning "the token is on $(i,j)$ and its last move was horizontal (resp. vertical)".
> - **Edges.** **Directed.** For every vertical move from $(i,j)$ to $(i',j')$, an edge $(i,j,\textsf{horiz})\to(i',j',\textsf{vert})$; for every horizontal move from $(i,j)$ to $(i',j')$, an edge $(i,j,\textsf{vert})\to(i',j',\textsf{horiz})$; and for every move from $(1,1)$ to $(i',j')$, an edge $s\to(i',j',a)$, where $a$ is the kind of that move. Whether the graph is a dag does not matter (it usually is not).
> - **Values.** None.
> - **Relationship.** By induction on $m$, the walks with $m$ edges from $s$ are exactly the alternating sequences of $m$ moves from $(1,1)$, and each ends at $(i,j,a)$, where $(i,j)$ is the token's final square and $a$ the kind of its last move: every edge is a move, the first move is unrestricted, and the edges out of $(i,j,a)$ are exactly the moves that may follow a move of kind $a$. So the answer is the **shortest-path distance** from $s$ to the nearer of $(n,n,\textsf{horiz})$ and $(n,n,\textsf{vert})$; if neither is reachable from $s$, there is no valid solution.
> - **Algorithm.** Breadth-first search from $s$; return $\min\{\mathit{dist}(n,n,\textsf{horiz}), \mathit{dist}(n,n,\textsf{vert})\}$ over those that are reached, or "no valid solution" if neither is. BFS because the graph is unweighted and we want fewest edges.
> - **Running time.** $V = 2n^2 + 1$. At most two edges leave each vertex (a square has at most two moves of each kind, and from the corner $(1,1)$ only the moves right and down can stay on the board), so $E\le 4n^2 + 2$. Building the graph takes $O(1)$ time per vertex, and BFS $O(V+E)$: $O(n^2)$ in all.
>
> On the handout's maze there is **no valid solution**: every solution contains a left move into $(2,2)$ followed by right 3 — two horizontal moves in a row ([[#the-handouts-maze-under-all-six-rules|why]]).
>
> **Answer.** BFS from $s$ in the graph of (square, axis of the last move) pairs; $O(n^2)$ time.

> [!trap] Modifying BFS instead of the graph
> Running BFS on the squares themselves and skipping every move of the same kind as the move that first reached the current square is **wrong**: BFS marks each square once, but a square may have to be visited twice, once after a horizontal and once after a vertical move. In the $3\times 3$ maze whose labels are all 1 except $M[3,2] = 2$, such a search (trying moves in the order up, down, left, right) first reaches the center by down, right; from there only vertical moves are allowed: up returns to the marked square $(1,2)$, and down reaches $(3,2)$, whose label 2 allows no horizontal move. The search stops without reaching the goal and reports "no solution" — but right, down, right, down is a valid solution of 4 moves. The rubric gives full credit to a modified search only when it is unmistakably correct; put the state into the vertices instead.

## Problem 3 — A multiple of 5 moves

> [!question] Problem 3
> Now a sequence of moves is **valid** only if its length — the number of moves — is a multiple of 5. Describe and analyze an algorithm that returns the minimum number of moves in a valid solution of a given number maze, or correctly reports that no valid solution exists.

> [!hint]- Hint
> Remember the number of moves so far — but only modulo 5.

> [!solution]- Solution
> **The idea.** Only the number of moves made so far matters, and only modulo 5. Take five copies of the board, one per remainder, and let every move lead from layer $r$ to layer $r+1$, and from layer 4 back to layer 0. A walk from layer 0 that ends in layer 0 has a multiple of 5 edges. This is the even-walk construction of [[2-algorithms/05-basic-graph-algorithms#state-in-the-vertices-walks-of-even-length|§5.7]] with five layers instead of two (and of [[2-algorithms/05-basic-graph-algorithms-exercises#exercise-514--walks-of-length-divisible-by-3|Ex. 5.14]] with three).
>
> - **Vertices.** The $5n^2$ triples $(i,j,r)$ with $1\le i,j\le n$ and $r\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}$, meaning "the token is on $(i,j)$ after a number of moves congruent to $r$ modulo 5".
> - **Edges.** **Directed.** For every move from $(i,j)$ to $(i',j')$ and every $r$, an edge $(i,j,r)\to\big(i',j',(r+1)\bmod 5\big)$. Whether the graph is a dag does not matter (it usually is not).
> - **Values.** None.
> - **Relationship.** Every sequence of $m$ moves from $(1,1)$ corresponds to exactly one walk with $m$ edges from $(1,1,0)$, which ends at $(i,j,m\bmod 5)$, where $(i,j)$ is the token's final square — the third coordinate counts the edges modulo 5 — and every walk from $(1,1,0)$ arises this way. So the valid solutions are exactly the walks from $(1,1,0)$ to $(n,n,0)$, and the answer is the **shortest-path distance** from $(1,1,0)$ to $(n,n,0)$; if $(n,n,0)$ is not reachable, there is no valid solution. (For $n = 1$ the distance is 0, which is right: 0 is a multiple of 5.)
> - **Algorithm.** Breadth-first search from $(1,1,0)$; return the distance of $(n,n,0)$, or "no valid solution" if it is not reached.
> - **Running time.** $V = 5n^2$ and $E\le 4V = 20n^2$; building and searching take $O(V+E) = O(n^2)$ time.
>
> On the handout's maze the answer is **10**: no solution has exactly 5 moves, and right 3, down 4, left 2, right 1, up 3, left 1, right 3, **left 3, right 3**, down 3 — the 8-move solution with a detour — has 10.
>
> **Answer.** BFS from $(1,1,0)$ to $(n,n,0)$ in five layered copies of the move graph; $O(n^2)$ time.

> [!trap] Rounding up is wrong
> The answer is not "the answer of Problem 1, rounded up to a multiple of 5". In the $2\times 2$ maze whose labels are all 1, every move changes $i + j$ by one, so every solution has an even number of moves: the shortest has 2, there is none with 5, and the answer to Problem 3 is 10. A valid solution may not exist at all even when the maze is solvable.

## Problem 4 — No U-turns (harder)

> [!question] Problem 4
> Now a sequence of moves is **valid** only if no two adjacent moves go in opposite directions — it contains no U-turn (up then down, down then up, left then right, or right then left). Describe and analyze an algorithm that returns the minimum number of moves in a valid solution of a given number maze, or correctly reports that no valid solution exists.

> [!hint]- Hint
> The forbidden pattern spans two consecutive moves. What must a vertex remember about the previous one?

> [!solution]- Solution
> **The idea.** The same as Problem 2 with a finer memory: whether a move is allowed depends on the *direction* of the previous move, one of four. So make four copies of every square, one per direction of arrival, and let the edges out of the copy "arrived moving in direction $d$" be every move except the one in the opposite direction. Again the start, with no previous move, gets its own vertex.
>
> Write $\textsf{opp}(d)$ for the direction opposite to $d$: $\textsf{opp}(\textsf{up}) = \textsf{down}$, $\textsf{opp}(\textsf{left}) = \textsf{right}$, and vice versa.
>
> - **Vertices.** A start vertex $s$, meaning "the token is on $(1,1)$ and has not moved yet", and the $4n^2$ triples $(i,j,d)$ with $1\le i,j\le n$ and $d\in\{\textsf{up},\textsf{down},\textsf{left},\textsf{right}\}$, meaning "the token is on $(i,j)$ and its last move went in direction $d$".
> - **Edges.** **Directed.** For every move from $(i,j)$ to $(i',j')$ in direction $d'$ and every direction $d\ne\textsf{opp}(d')$, an edge $(i,j,d)\to(i',j',d')$; and for every move from $(1,1)$ to $(i',j')$ in direction $d'$, an edge $s\to(i',j',d')$. Whether the graph is a dag does not matter (it usually is not).
> - **Values.** None.
> - **Relationship.** By induction on $m$, the walks with $m$ edges from $s$ are exactly the U-turn-free sequences of $m$ moves from $(1,1)$, each ending at $(i,j,d)$ with $(i,j)$ the final square and $d$ the direction of the last move: the first move is unrestricted, and the edges out of $(i,j,d)$ are exactly the moves that may follow a move in direction $d$. So the answer is the **shortest-path distance** from $s$ to the nearest of the four vertices $(n,n,d)$; if none is reachable, there is no valid solution.
> - **Algorithm.** Breadth-first search from $s$; return the smallest distance among the reached vertices $(n,n,\textsf{up})$, $(n,n,\textsf{down})$, $(n,n,\textsf{left})$, $(n,n,\textsf{right})$, or "no valid solution" if none is reached.
> - **Running time.** $V = 4n^2 + 1$. At most three edges leave each vertex (at most four moves, minus the U-turn; $s$ has at most two), so $E\le 3V = 12n^2 + 3$. Building and searching: $O(V+E) = O(n^2)$ time.
>
> On the handout's maze there is **no valid solution**: every solution contains a left move into $(2,2)$ followed by right 3 — a U-turn ([[#the-handouts-maze-under-all-six-rules|why]]).
>
> **Answer.** BFS from $s$ in the graph of (square, direction of the last move) pairs; $O(n^2)$ time.

## Problem 5 — Increasing move lengths (harder)

> [!question] Problem 5
> Now a sequence of moves is **valid** only if each move is longer than the previous move (if any): the sequence of move lengths is strictly increasing. Describe and analyze an algorithm that returns the minimum number of moves in a valid solution of a given number maze, or correctly reports that no valid solution exists.

> [!hint]- Hint
> The rule needs the length of the last move. Where does that length come from — and can the rule be checked at the moment a move is made?

> [!solution]- Solution
> **The idea.** The first thought is to remember the length $\ell$ of the last move: vertices $(i,j,\ell)$, with an edge for every move from $(i,j)$ whose length $M[i,j]$ exceeds $\ell$. That works (see the end), but it has $n^3$ vertices. Look closer: the length of the last move is the label of the square the token came from, and the length of the next move is the label of the square it is on. So the rule says: a move from $p$ to $q$ may be followed by another move only if $M[q] > M[p]$ — a condition on the move $p\to q$ itself. Keep only those moves as edges, with one exception: the **last** move of a solution is followed by nothing, so it may enter the goal whatever the labels. To tell a last move apart from a move that passes through the goal, last moves lead to a separate end vertex $t$.
>
> - **Vertices.** The $n^2$ squares $(i,j)$ and one end vertex $t$. Square $(i,j)$ means "the token is on $(i,j)$ and may move again": it arrived by a move shorter than $M[i,j]$, or it has not moved yet. Vertex $t$ means "the token has made its last move, into $(n,n)$".
> - **Edges.** **Directed.** An edge $(i,j)\to(i',j')$ for every move from $(i,j)$ to $(i',j')$ with $M[i',j'] > M[i,j]$ (a move that a longer move can follow), and an edge $(i,j)\to t$ for every move from $(i,j)$ to $(n,n)$ (a final move). The graph **is a dag**: labels strictly increase along every edge between squares, and no edge leaves $t$. BFS does not need this, but it bounds the answer: the lengths of a valid solution strictly increase and lie between 1 and $n-1$ (a longer move leaves the board), so a valid solution has at most $n-1$ moves.
> - **Values.** None.
> - **Relationship.** A solution $p_0 = (1,1), p_1, \dots, p_m = (n,n)$ with $m\ge 1$ has move lengths $M[p_0], M[p_1], \dots, M[p_{m-1}]$, so it is valid if and only if $M[p_0] < M[p_1] < \cdots < M[p_{m-1}]$ — that is, if and only if each of its first $m-1$ moves is an edge between squares; its last move, into $(n,n)$, is always an edge to $t$. So the valid solutions with $m$ moves correspond one-to-one to the walks $p_0\to p_1\to\cdots\to p_{m-1}\to t$ with $m$ edges, and the answer is the **shortest-path distance** from $(1,1)$ to $t$; if $t$ is not reachable, there is no valid solution.
> - **Algorithm.** Breadth-first search from $(1,1)$; return the distance of $t$, or "no valid solution" if it is not reached. **Is BFS still the right tool?** Yes: fewest edges in an unweighted graph is exactly its job, in $O(V+E)$ time. That the graph is a dag allows an alternative — dynamic programming over the squares in increasing order of label, which is a topological order (with $t$ last; capping the labels at $n$, which changes no edge, counting sort finds it in $O(n^2)$ time) — but that also takes $O(V+E)$ time, so nothing is gained; Dijkstra's algorithm would be slower than necessary.
> - **Running time.** $V = n^2 + 1$. At most four edges leave each square toward squares, and at most $2(n-1)$ edges enter $t$ (a move into the goal starts in the last row or column), so $E\le 4n^2 + 2n$. Building and searching: $O(n^2)$ time.
>
> **The direct version.** Vertices $(i,j,\ell)$ for $1\le i,j\le n$ and $0\le\ell\le n-1$ ("on $(i,j)$, the last move had length $\ell$"; $\ell = 0$ before the first move), and an edge $(i,j,\ell)\to(i',j',M[i,j])$ for every move from $(i,j)$ to $(i',j')$ with $M[i,j] > \ell$. The answer is the smallest distance from $(1,1,0)$ to a vertex $(n,n,\ell)$. Lengths above $n-1$ never occur, because such a move leaves the board. This graph is also a dag ($\ell$ increases along every edge) and is correct too, but it has $n^3$ vertices and up to $4n^3$ edges: $O(n^3)$ time, a factor $n$ slower.
>
> On the handout's maze there is **no valid solution**: the first move has length 3 and lands on a square labeled 4, and no move longer than 4 fits on a $5\times 5$ board, so a valid solution has at most 2 moves — but every solution has at least 8.
>
> **Answer.** BFS from $(1,1)$ to $t$ in the dag of moves $p\to q$ with $M[q] > M[p]$, plus the final moves into the goal; $O(n^2)$ time (the direct (square, last length) graph gives $O(n^3)$).

## Problem 6 — Palindromic move lengths (harder)

> [!question] Problem 6
> Finally, a sequence of moves is **valid** only if its sequence of move lengths is a palindrome — equal to its own reversal. Describe and analyze an algorithm that returns the minimum number of moves in a valid solution of a given number maze, or correctly reports that no valid solution exists.

> [!hint]- Hint
> A palindrome can be checked from both ends at once: the first length against the last, the second against the second-to-last, … Walk two tokens.

> [!solution]- Solution
> **The idea.** No small summary of the lengths so far decides whether a solution can still become a palindrome, so a single token cannot carry the memory. But a palindrome is checked from the outside in — first length against last, second against second-to-last — so build the solution from both ends at once. A **front token** starts on $(1,1)$ and makes the solution's moves in order; a **back token** starts on $(n,n)$ and makes them in reverse order, from the end. They move in lockstep, and at every step the two moves must have the same length. After $h$ steps each they have produced the first $h$ and the last $h$ moves. If the solution has $2h$ moves, the tokens now stand on the same square; if it has $2h+1$, one middle move joins them, and that move, paired with itself, may have any length. The state is the pair of squares — nothing else needs remembering, because each pair of lengths is checked when it is made.
>
> A back step from $b$ to $b'$ means that the solution contains the move from $b'$ to $b$: $b'$ lies $k$ squares from $b$ in some direction and is labeled $k$.
>
> - **Vertices.** The $n^4$ ordered pairs $(f,b)$ of squares. Vertex $(f,b)$ means: for some $h$, there is a sequence of $h$ moves from $(1,1)$ to $f$ and a sequence of $h$ moves from $b$ to $(n,n)$ whose lengths, the first read forward and the second backward, agree — the first and last $h$ moves of a palindromic solution in the making.
> - **Edges.** **Directed.** An edge $(f,b)\to(f',b')$ whenever there is a move from $f$ to $f'$ and a move from $b'$ to $b$ of the **same length**: $M[b'] = M[f]$. Whether the graph is a dag does not matter (it usually is not).
> - **Values.** None. (The extra middle move of an odd solution is accounted for when the result is read off.)
> - **Relationship.** Let $p_0 = (1,1), \dots, p_m = (n,n)$ be a solution with move lengths $\ell_k = M[p_k]$; it is valid iff $\ell_k = \ell_{m-1-k}$ for every $k$. With $h = \lfloor m/2\rfloor$, the sequence $(p_0, p_m)\to(p_1, p_{m-1})\to\cdots\to(p_h, p_{m-h})$ is a walk with $h$ edges from $\big((1,1),(n,n)\big)$: step $k$ pairs the front move from $p_k$ to $p_{k+1}$ (length $\ell_k$) with the back move from $p_{m-k-1}$ to $p_{m-k}$ (length $\ell_{m-1-k}$), and these are equal. If $m = 2h$ the walk ends at a vertex $(f,f)$; if $m = 2h+1$ it ends at $(p_h, p_{h+1})$, and a move goes from $p_h$ to $p_{h+1}$. Conversely, a walk with $d$ edges from $\big((1,1),(n,n)\big)$ to a vertex $(f,f)$ gives a valid solution with $2d$ moves (the front token's moves, then the back token's moves in reverse), and a walk with $d$ edges to a vertex $(f,b)$ such that a move goes from $f$ to $b$ gives one with $2d+1$ moves (that middle move in between). Hence, writing $\mathit{dist}$ for the **shortest-path distance** from $\big((1,1),(n,n)\big)$, the answer is the smallest of the numbers $2\,\mathit{dist}(f,f)$ and $2\,\mathit{dist}(f,b)+1$, taken over the reachable vertices $(f,f)$ and over the reachable vertices $(f,b)$ such that a move goes from $f$ to $b$; if there are no such vertices, there is no valid solution.
> - **Algorithm.** Breadth-first search once, from $\big((1,1),(n,n)\big)$; then scan all reached vertices and take the minimum above. (It is not a single shortest-path query to a set of targets, because the two kinds of endings are worth $2d$ and $2d+1$ moves; the scan costs $O(n^4)$, no more than the search.)
> - **Running time.** $V = n^4$. From $(f,b)$, the front token has at most 4 moves; for a front move of length $k$, the squares $b'$ with a move of length $k$ into $b$ are among the four squares at distance $k$ from $b$, so there are at most 4. Hence at most 16 edges leave each vertex, $E\le 16n^4$, and the graph is built in $O(1)$ time per vertex. BFS takes $O(V+E) = O(n^4)$, and the final scan $O(1)$ per vertex (is $b$ among the at most four squares a move from $f$ reaches?). Total: $O(n^4)$.
>
> On the handout's maze there is **no valid solution**: every solution starts with lengths 3, 4 and ends with lengths 3, 3, and has at least 8 moves, so its second length (4) would have to equal its second-to-last (3) ([[#the-handouts-maze-under-all-six-rules|why]]).
>
> **Answer.** BFS from $\big((1,1),(n,n)\big)$ in the graph of (front square, back square) pairs whose two moves per step have equal lengths, then the minimum of $2\,\mathit{dist}(f,f)$ and $2\,\mathit{dist}(f,b)+1$ over a final move from $f$ to $b$; $O(n^4)$ time.

## The handout's maze under all six rules

| problem | rule | answer | why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | none | 8 | right 3, down 4, left 2, right 1, up 3, left 1, right 3, down 3 — the only solution with 8 moves |
| 2 | alternate horizontal and vertical | none | every solution contains a left move followed by right 3 |
| 3 | a multiple of 5 moves | 10 | no solution has 5 moves; the 8-move one with the detour left 3, right 3 before its last move has 10 |
| 4 | no U-turns | none | every solution contains a left move followed by right 3, a U-turn |
| 5 | increasing lengths | none | lengths 3, 4, and then nothing longer than 4 fits on the board |
| 6 | palindromic lengths | none | the lengths start 3, 4 and end 3, 3 |

Four facts about the maze give every "none". The only move into the goal is down 3 from $(2,5)$; the only move into $(2,5)$ is right 3 from $(2,2)$ (or up 3 from the goal, if the goal's label were 3); both moves into $(2,2)$ go left — left 1 from $(2,3)$ and left 3 from $(2,5)$; and the only moves out of $(1,1)$ are right 3 and down 3, to $(1,4)$ and $(4,1)$, both labeled 4. The token's first visit to $(2,5)$ cannot come from the goal, so it comes by right 3 from $(2,2)$, right after a left move, because $(2,2)$ is not the start: every solution contains a left move followed by right 3, which rules out Problems 2 and 4. Every solution ends with right 3 or up 3 followed by down 3, so its lengths end 3, 3, and it starts with lengths 3, 4: that rules out Problem 6, and Problem 5 too, since no move longer than 4 fits on the board while every solution has at least 8 moves.
