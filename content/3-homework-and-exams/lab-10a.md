> [!abstract] In one breath
> All three problems are solved by cutting a shortest walk at the one place where it is special and handing each piece to a standard algorithm. Problem 1: without negative cycles a shortest walk can be taken to be a path, so it crosses the negative edge $u\to v$ at most once; delete that edge, run Dijkstra from $s$ and from $v$, and return $\min\{d_s(t),\ d_s(u) + w(u\to v) + d_v(t)\}$, in $O(E\log V)$ time. Or run the course's Dijkstra — which puts a vertex back into the queue when its distance drops — on the graph as it is: it is correct whenever there is no negative cycle, and with one negative edge the extracted keys drop at most once, so every vertex leaves the queue at most twice and the time is still $O(E\log V)$; but $t$'s distance need not be final the first time $t$ leaves the queue. Problem 2: you both leave now and the first to arrive waits, so meeting at city $t$ happens at time $\max\{\delta(p,t), \delta(q,t)\}$; Dijkstra from $p$ and from $q$ and a scan over the cities, $O(E\log V)$. Problem 3: a looped tree has fewer than $3n/2$ edges, so Dijkstra takes $O(n\log n)$; but a shortest walk passes through the root at most once, and splitting the root into a source and a sink turns the looped tree into a dag — two runs of DagSSSP, $O(n)$, or in closed form $D(v) - D(u)$ if $u = v$ or $u$ is an ancestor of $v$, and $L(u) + D(v)$ otherwise.

> [!note] Notation and the black boxes
> - $G = (V, E)$ is directed with edge weights $w$; $\delta_G(x, y)$, or $\delta(x,y)$, is the length of a shortest walk from $x$ to $y$ ($\infty$ if there is none). $G - e$ is $G$ without the edge $e$.
> - **Dijkstra** is the version of the book ([[2-algorithms/08-shortest-paths#86-best-first-dijkstras-algorithm|§8.6]], [[concepts/dijkstras-algorithm]]): after relaxing a tense edge into $y$, it calls DecreaseKey if $y$ is in the priority queue and Insert otherwise — also when $y$ has left the queue before. Kept as a heap with stale copies — push a new entry instead of DecreaseKey, and skip a popped entry whose key exceeds the vertex's current distance — it is the same algorithm. It is an instance of Ford's algorithm, so it ends with $\mathit{dist}(x) = \delta(s,x)$ for every $x$ whenever no negative cycle is reachable from $s$; with non-negative weights it extracts every vertex once and runs in $O(E\log V)$ time with a binary heap. **NonnegativeDijkstra** — or any implementation that never scans an extracted vertex again — is wrong as soon as one edge is negative.
> - **DagSSSP** ([[2-algorithms/08-shortest-paths#85-directed-acyclic-graphs-depth-first-search|§8.5]]): relax the out-edges of every vertex in topological order; any weights, dags only, $O(V+E)$.
> - Every solution is written in the four parts of the [[0-toolkit/06-how-exams-are-graded#13-graph-reductions|graph-reduction rubric]] ([[concepts/graph-reductions]]): the graph, the problem on it, the algorithm as a black box and how its output is used, and the time in the input's parameters.

## Problem 1 — One negative edge

> [!question] Problem 1
> Describe and analyze an algorithm that computes the shortest path from a vertex $s$ to a vertex $t$ in a directed graph with weighted edges in which exactly **one** edge $u\to v$ has negative weight. Assume that the graph has no negative cycles. *(Hint from the handout: modify the input graph and run Dijkstra's algorithm. Alternatively, **don't** modify the input graph, but run Dijkstra's algorithm anyway.)*

This is the book's Exercise 8.2(a); the [[2-algorithms/08-shortest-paths-exercises#exercise-82--a-few-negative-edges|Chapter 8 exercises page]] solves it the first way, and also says what to report when the graph might have a negative cycle. Both of the handout's ways are worked out below, on the example of the figure further down.

> [!hint]- Hint
> A shortest walk crosses $u\to v$ at most once. Cut it there: before the edge and after it, every weight is non-negative.

> [!solution]- Solution 1 — delete the negative edge, run Dijkstra twice
> **The idea.** Dijkstra's algorithm is in danger only because of the one edge $u\to v$. A shortest path from $s$ to $t$ either avoids that edge — then it is a path in $G - (u\to v)$, where every weight is non-negative — or crosses it exactly once, and then it consists of a path from $s$ to $u$, the edge, and a path from $v$ to $t$, the two paths avoiding the edge. Dijkstra computes the best pieces; then compare the two options.
>
> **Why $u\to v$ is used at most once.** A walk that crosses $u\to v$ twice contains, between the two crossings, a closed walk $v\leadsto u\to v$. A closed walk splits into cycles, and no cycle is negative, so it weighs at least 0; cutting it out leaves a walk from $s$ to $t$ that is no longer and crosses $u\to v$ once fewer. So some shortest walk crosses it at most once — and every shortest *path* (no repeated vertex) does, because it visits $u$ at most once. A shortest walk exists whenever $s$ reaches $t$, because there is no negative cycle ([[2-algorithms/08-shortest-paths#82-negative-edges|§8.2]]).
>
> - **The graph.** $G^+ = G - (u\to v)$: the same vertices, every edge of $G$ except $u\to v$ (found by one scan of the edges if it is not given), with the same weights; **directed**. All its weights are non-negative.
> - **The problem on it.** Single-source shortest paths in $G^+$ from $s$, giving $d_s(x) = \delta_{G^+}(s, x)$, and from $v$, giving $d_v(x) = \delta_{G^+}(v, x)$, for every vertex $x$. The answer is $\delta_G(s, t) = \min\{d_s(t),\ d_s(u) + w(u\to v) + d_v(t)\}$: the better of avoiding $u\to v$ and crossing it once (proof below).
> - **The algorithm.** Dijkstra in $G^+$ from $s$ and from $v$ (black boxes), then the formula. **The path itself:** if $d_s(t)\le d_s(u) + w(u\to v) + d_v(t)$, the predecessor path of $t$ in the first run; otherwise the predecessor path of $u$ in the first run, then $u\to v$, then the predecessor path of $t$ in the second run. With ties going to $d_s(t)$ the result never repeats a vertex: each piece is a path, and a vertex $z$ on both pieces would let us cut out the closed walk $z\leadsto u\to v\leadsto z$, of weight at least 0, leaving a walk from $s$ to $t$ in $G^+$ that is no longer — so $d_s(t)$ would be at most the second term, and the first would have been chosen.
> - **The time.** Building $G^+$ takes $O(V+E)$ time, the two runs of Dijkstra $O(E\log V)$, the formula and the path $O(V)$: **$O(E\log V)$** in all — instead of Bellman–Ford's $O(VE)$.
>
> **Why the formula is right.** Each term is $\infty$ or the length of a walk in $G$ from $s$ to $t$ — a path of $G^+$, or a path of $G^+$ to $u$, the edge $u\to v$ and a path of $G^+$ from $v$ — so $\delta_G(s,t)$ is at most the minimum. Conversely, if $s$ reaches $t$, take a shortest path $P$ from $s$ to $t$ in $G$. If $P$ avoids $u\to v$, it lies in $G^+$ and $w(P)\ge d_s(t)$. Otherwise it crosses $u\to v$ exactly once, its part before the edge is a path in $G^+$ from $s$ to $u$ and its part after is a path in $G^+$ from $v$ to $t$, so $w(P)\ge d_s(u) + w(u\to v) + d_v(t)$. Either way $\delta_G(s,t)$ is at least the minimum. If $s$ does not reach $t$, both terms are $\infty$.
>
> Dijkstra from $t$ in the reversal of $G^+$ works as well as Dijkstra from $v$: it gives $\delta_{G^+}(x, t)$ for every $x$, as in [[2-algorithms/08-shortest-paths-exercises#exercise-85--reinserting-one-edge|Ex. 8.5]]. The same two runs also confirm the assumption: every cycle through $u\to v$ is that edge plus a path from $v$ to $u$ in $G^+$, so $G$ has no negative cycle exactly when $d_v(u) + w(u\to v)\ge 0$.
>
> **One run instead of two.** Make two copies of $G^+$, with vertices $(x, 0)$ for "before crossing $u\to v$" and $(x, 1)$ for "after", copy every edge of $G^+$ into both layers, and add one **directed** edge $(u,0)\to(v,1)$ of weight **0**. Every path from $(s,0)$ to $(t,1)$ crosses this edge exactly once, so giving it weight 0 instead of $w(u\to v)$ shortens all of them by the same amount, and
> $$
> \delta_G(s,t) = \min\bigl\{\,\delta_H\bigl((s,0),(t,0)\bigr),\ \ \delta_H\bigl((s,0),(t,1)\bigr) + w(u\to v)\,\bigr\}
> $$
> from **one** run of Dijkstra in this graph $H$, which has $2V$ vertices and $2E - 1$ edges, all non-negative: $O(E\log V)$. (Shifting a weight is safe here only because every path being compared crosses the edge equally often; adding a constant to *every* edge is not — see the trap below.) It is the two-layer case of the layered graph of [[2-algorithms/08-shortest-paths-exercises#exercise-82--a-few-negative-edges|Ex. 8.2(b)]].
>
> On the example of the figure: $d_s(t) = 6$ (along $s\,a\,b\,t$) and $d_s(u) + w(u\to v) + d_v(t) = 7 - 5 + 3 = 5$, so $\delta(s,t) = 5$, along $s\,u\,v\,b\,t$.
>
> **Answer.** Dijkstra from $s$ and from $v$ in $G - (u\to v)$, then $\delta(s,t) = \min\{d_s(t),\ d_s(u) + w(u\to v) + d_v(t)\}$ and the matching path; $O(E\log V)$ time.

> [!solution]- Solution 2 — run Dijkstra on the graph as it is
> **The idea.** The re-inserting Dijkstra never needed non-negative weights to be *correct*: it is Ford's algorithm, which stops with the right distances whenever no negative cycle exists ([[2-algorithms/08-shortest-paths#83-the-only-sssp-algorithm|§8.3]]). Non-negative weights were needed only for the *running time* — they guarantee that the extracted keys never decrease, so no vertex is extracted twice. With one negative edge the keys can decrease only once: the run is an ordinary Dijkstra run in $G^+$ until $u$ is extracted; relaxing $u\to v$ may then give $v$ a key below everything extracted so far — the one drop — and from there on the run is again an ordinary non-negative run. Each of the two stretches extracts every vertex at most once.
>
> - **The graph.** $G$ itself, unchanged.
> - **The problem on it.** Single-source shortest paths from $s$; report $\mathit{dist}(t)$ and the predecessor path of $t$.
> - **The algorithm.** Dijkstra from $s$ — the re-inserting version (black box: correct because there is no negative cycle).
> - **The time.** $O(E\log V)$, by the claims below: at most $2V$ ExtractMin operations, and at most $2E$ relaxations, each followed by one Insert or DecreaseKey.
>
> Number the extractions $1, 2, \ldots$: the $i$th extracts $x_i$ with key $k_i$.
>
> 1. **Until $u$ is extracted, the run is a run of Dijkstra in $G^+$**, because the only missing edge leaves $u$ and is looked at only when $u$ is scanned. In $G^+$ every weight is non-negative, and then a vertex's distance is final when it is extracted ([[2-algorithms/08-shortest-paths#no-negative-edges|§8.6]]); so $u$ is extracted with $\mathit{dist}(u) = \delta_{G^+}(s,u)$, and that equals $\delta_G(s,u)$: a shortest path to $u$ visits $u$ only at its end, so it does not use the edge leaving $u$.
> 2. **$u$ is extracted at most once.** At every moment $\mathit{dist}(u)$ is the length of some walk from $s$ to $u$ (Ford's Claim 1), so it is never below $\delta_G(s,u)$ — it never drops after $u$'s first extraction, and $u$ is never inserted again.
> 3. **The keys drop at most once: $k_{i+1} < k_i$ only if $x_i = u$ and $x_{i+1} = v$.** As in Lemma 8.3: if the key of $x_{i+1}$ was set while $x_i$ was scanned, then $k_{i+1} = k_i + w(x_i\to x_{i+1})$, which is at least $k_i$ unless that edge is $u\to v$; otherwise $x_{i+1}$ was already in the queue when $x_i$ was extracted, with a key at least $k_i$, and nothing changed it. By claim 2 this happens at most once, so the keys form at most two non-decreasing stretches.
> 4. **Each vertex is extracted at most twice.** As in Lemma 8.4: between two extractions of $x$, its key must drop strictly (that is what puts it back into the queue), so two extractions of $x$ cannot lie in the same non-decreasing stretch.
> 5. **Every extraction after $u$'s is final:** those extractions form one non-decreasing stretch, so a vertex extracted then is never extracted again — its distance never drops again — and at the end its distance is correct.
>
> Hence at most $2V$ ExtractMin operations; a vertex's out-edges are scanned once per extraction, so at most $2E$ relaxations, each followed by one Insert or DecreaseKey; with a binary heap every operation takes $O(\log V)$ time: $O(E\log V)$, Dijkstra's usual bound with a factor 2 (as usual assuming $E\ge V-1$; in general $O(V + E\log V)$). The bound "twice" is attained: below, $b$ and $t$ leave the queue twice.
>
> **What is not guaranteed.**
> - **A vertex's first extraction need not be final**, so the usual shortcut "stop when $t$ is extracted" is wrong — unless $t$ is extracted after $u$ (claim 5). Vertices do not leave the queue in order of distance either.
> - **The re-inserting version is required.** NonnegativeDijkstra, and every implementation with a "finished" set that is never scanned again, lowers $\mathit{dist}(b)$ to 2 in the example but never rescans $b$, and reports $\mathit{dist}(t) = 6$.
> - **The bound is for one negative edge.** With many negative edges the same algorithm stays correct but can take exponential time (the Shier–Witzgall graphs of [[2-algorithms/08-shortest-paths#negative-edges|§8.6]]); with a negative cycle reachable from $s$ it never stops.
>
> **The run on the example of the figure** (ties never occur):
>
> | step | ExtractMin | relaxations | queue afterwards |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 1 | $s$, 0 | Insert $a$ 1, $u$ 7 | $a{:}\,1$, $u{:}\,7$ |
> | 2 | $a$, 1 | Insert $b$ 3, $t$ 7 | $b{:}\,3$, $t{:}\,7$, $u{:}\,7$ |
> | 3 | $b$, 3 | DecreaseKey $t$ to 6 | $t{:}\,6$, $u{:}\,7$ |
> | 4 | $t$, 6 | none ($t\to a$: $6 + 2 > 1$) | $u{:}\,7$ |
> | 5 | $u$, 7 | Insert $v$ 2 | $v{:}\,2$ |
> | 6 | $v$, 2 | $\mathit{dist}(b)$: $3\to 2$, Insert $b$ again | $b{:}\,2$ |
> | 7 | $b$, 2 | $\mathit{dist}(t)$: $6\to 5$, Insert $t$ again | $t{:}\,5$ |
> | 8 | $t$, 5 | none ($5 + 2 > 1$) | empty |
>
> The keys $0, 1, 3, 6, 7$ and then $2, 2, 5$ form two non-decreasing stretches; the drop is from $u$ (7) to $v$ (2). The run makes 8 Insert, 1 DecreaseKey and 8 ExtractMin operations, $u$ leaves the queue once, $b$ and $t$ twice; stopping at step 4 would report 6.
>
> **Answer.** The re-inserting Dijkstra from $s$ on $G$ unchanged: correct because there is no negative cycle, every vertex extracted at most twice (the tail $u$ once), $O(E\log V)$ time — but $t$'s distance is final only at an extraction after $u$'s, or at the end.

## Problem 2 — Where to meet

> [!question] Problem 2
> You have just found your best friend from elementary school online, and you both want to meet as soon as possible. You live in two different cities, far apart; you agree to meet at an intermediate city, and then you **simultaneously** hop in your cars and drive toward it. Where exactly should you meet? The input is a weighted graph $G = (V, E)$ whose vertices are cities and whose edges are roads directly connecting two cities; each edge $e$ has a weight $w(e)$, the time needed to travel between its two cities. You are also given a vertex $p$, your starting city, and a vertex $q$, your friend's. Describe and analyze an algorithm that finds the target vertex $t$ that lets you and your friend meet as soon as possible, assuming both of you leave home right now.

This is the book's Exercise 8.14 ([[2-algorithms/08-shortest-paths-exercises#exercise-814--where-to-meet|solved on the exercises page]]).

> [!hint]- Hint
> If you agree on city $t$, at what time do you meet? Not when the first of you arrives.

> [!solution]- Solution
> **The idea.** Fix a candidate city $t$. You cannot be at $t$ before time $\delta(p, t)$, and you are there at exactly that time if you drive a shortest route; your friend arrives at $\delta(q, t)$. Whoever arrives first waits, so the meeting takes place at
> $$
> T(t) = \max\bigl\{\delta(p, t),\ \delta(q, t)\bigr\},
> $$
> the arrival of the later one. All we need are the distances from $p$ and from $q$ to every city — two single-source problems — and then a city with the smallest $T(t)$.
>
> **Reading the problem.** The roads are undirected and the times are non-negative. You must meet **at a city**: the problem asks for a target vertex, fixed before you leave. Meeting somewhere along a road could be earlier — with a single road of length 2 between $p$ and $q$, both cities give time 2, the middle of the road gives 1 — but that is not what is asked.
>
> - **The graph.** $G$ itself, undirected, with weights $w(e)\ge 0$. For Dijkstra every road $xy$ becomes the two directed edges $x\to y$ and $y\to x$ of weight $w(xy)$ — safe because no weight is negative ([[2-algorithms/08-shortest-paths#82-negative-edges|§8.2]]).
> - **The problem on it.** Single-source shortest paths from $p$ and from $q$, giving $d_p(x) = \delta(p, x)$ and $d_q(x) = \delta(q, x)$ for every city $x$. The answer is a city $t$ minimizing $\max\{d_p(t), d_q(t)\}$: as argued above, that maximum is exactly the earliest time at which the two of you can be together at $t$. If the maximum is $\infty$ for every city, no city is reachable by both of you (you live in different components) and you cannot meet.
> - **The algorithm.** Dijkstra from $p$ and from $q$ (black boxes); then one scan over all cities, computing $\max\{d_p(t), d_q(t)\}$ and keeping a city where it is smallest.
> - **The time.** Two runs of Dijkstra, $O(E\log V)$, and a scan, $O(V)$: $O(V + E\log V)$, which is $O(E\log V)$ for a connected road network.
>
> **One-way roads** would change nothing: both distances are distances *from* $p$ and $q$, so Dijkstra runs from $p$ and from $q$ in the directed graph itself (no reversal needed).
>
> **Answer.** Dijkstra from $p$ and from $q$, then the city $t$ minimizing $\max\{\delta(p,t), \delta(q,t)\}$; $O(E\log V)$ time.

> [!trap] Two wrong targets
> - **Minimizing $\delta(p,t) + \delta(q,t)$** minimizes the total driving, not the meeting time. On the path $p - a - q$ with two roads of length 1, the sum is 2 at all three cities, but only $a$ gives meeting time 1.
> - **The city nearest the middle of a shortest route between $p$ and $q$.** The best city need not lie on any shortest $p$–$q$ path (figure). What is true: $T(t)\ge\delta(p,q)/2$ for every $t$, by the triangle inequality $\delta(p,q)\le\delta(p,t) + \delta(t,q)$.

## Problem 3 — Looped trees (harder)

> [!question] Problem 3
> A **looped tree** is a weighted, directed graph built from a binary tree, with its edges directed from parent to child (as in the handout's figure), by adding an edge from every leaf back to the root. Every edge has a non-negative weight. The handout's example (with our vertex names, as in the figure below and on the exercises page) has root $r$ with children $a$ (edge weight 5) and $b$ (8); $a$ has children $c$ (17) and $d$ (0); $c$ has the child $e$ (23); $b$ has the child $f$ (1); $f$ has children $g$ (9) and $h$ (14); the loop edges are $e\to r$ (4), $d\to r$ (16), $g\to r$ (7) and $h\to r$ (42).
>
> (a) How much time would Dijkstra's algorithm require to compute the shortest path between two vertices $u$ and $v$ in a looped tree with $n$ nodes?
>
> (b) Describe and analyze a faster algorithm.

This is the book's Exercise 8.1, and the handout's tree is the book's ([[2-algorithms/08-shortest-paths-exercises#exercise-81--looped-trees|solved and drawn on the exercises page]]).

### (a) Dijkstra's algorithm

> [!hint]- Hint
> Count the edges: how many leaves can a binary tree with $n$ nodes have?

> [!solution]- Solution
> The looped tree has $V = n$ vertices. A binary tree with $n$ nodes has $n - 1$ edges, and the looped tree adds one edge per leaf. If $\ell$ nodes are leaves, $n_1$ have one child and $n_2$ two, then counting the tree edges by their tails gives $n - 1 = n_1 + 2n_2$, and with $n = \ell + n_1 + n_2$ this says $\ell = n_2 + 1$; so $n\ge\ell + n_2 = 2\ell - 1$, that is $\ell\le(n+1)/2$. Hence
> $$
> E = n - 1 + \ell \le \frac{3n - 1}{2} = O(n),
> $$
> with equality exactly for full binary trees, where no node has a single child (the handout's tree is not full: $n = 9$, 4 leaves, 12 edges). All weights are non-negative, so Dijkstra from $u$ is correct, may stop as soon as $v$ is extracted, and in the worst case takes $O(E\log V) = O(n\log n)$ time with a binary heap — a Fibonacci heap's $O(E + V\log V)$ is no better here. Nothing in Dijkstra's algorithm takes advantage of the shape of the graph.
>
> **Answer.** $O(E\log V) = O(n\log n)$, because $E\le(3n-1)/2$.

### (b) A linear-time algorithm

> [!hint]- Hint
> From $u$ every edge leads down, until a leaf's loop edge jumps to the root; from the root, tree edges lead down to everything. How many times does a shortest walk need to pass through the root?

> [!solution]- Solution
> **The idea.** Write $r$ for the root and $T_u$ for the subtree of $u$ ($u$ and its descendants). Inside $T_u$ every edge goes down, so the only way out of $T_u$ is a leaf's loop edge, straight into $r$; and from $r$ the tree paths go down to every vertex. So a shortest walk from $u$ to $v$ is either the tree path down from $u$ to $v$ — possible only if $v$ is in $T_u$ — or goes down from $u$ to some leaf, takes its loop edge to $r$, and goes down the tree path from $r$ to $v$. It never needs to visit $r$ twice, because weights are non-negative. The root is the only place where the walk "turns around", so split it in two: a copy $r_{\text{out}}$ that keeps the edges to the root's children and a copy $r_{\text{in}}$ that receives the loop edges. That removes every cycle, and in a dag DagSSSP needs only linear time.
>
> - **The graph.** $H$: vertices are the $n - 1$ non-root nodes, $r_{\text{out}}$ and $r_{\text{in}}$. Edges: every tree edge $x\to y$, with $r_{\text{out}}$ in place of $r$ when $x = r$, and every loop edge $\ell\to r_{\text{in}}$ for each leaf $\ell$; same weights; **directed**. $H$ is a **dag**: tree edges go one level down, and $r_{\text{in}}$ has no outgoing edges. A preorder of the tree (with $r_{\text{out}}$ first) followed by $r_{\text{in}}$ is a topological order. $H$ has $n + 1$ vertices and the same $E\le(3n-1)/2$ edges.
> - **The problem on it.** If $u = v$ the answer is 0. Otherwise let $u' = r_{\text{out}}$ if $u = r$ and $u' = u$ otherwise, and $v' = r_{\text{in}}$ if $v = r$ and $v' = v$ otherwise. The answer is $\delta(u, v) = \min\{\delta_H(u', v'),\ \delta_H(u', r_{\text{in}}) + \delta_H(r_{\text{out}}, v')\}$: a shortest walk is either a path of $H$ or passes through the root once (proof below).
> - **The algorithm.** DagSSSP in $H$ from $u'$ and from $r_{\text{out}}$ (black boxes), then the formula.
> - **The time.** Building $H$ and its topological order takes $O(n)$ time, and each run of DagSSSP $O(V + E) = O(n)$: **$O(n)$** in all.
>
> **Why the formula is right.** Merging $r_{\text{out}}$ and $r_{\text{in}}$ back into $r$ turns every path of $H$ into a walk of the looped tree with the same length, and a path to $r_{\text{in}}$ followed by a path from $r_{\text{out}}$ into one walk through $r$; so both terms are lengths of walks from $u$ to $v$ (or $\infty$), and $\delta(u,v)$ is at most the minimum. Conversely, $v$ is reachable from $u$ (down to any leaf, back to $r$, down to $v$), so take a shortest walk $P$ from $u$ to $v$ with no repeated vertex (cutting out a closed walk never makes it longer). If $r$ is not an inner vertex of $P$, then $P$, read with $r_{\text{out}}$ at its start if $u = r$ and $r_{\text{in}}$ at its end if $v = r$, is a path in $H$ from $u'$ to $v'$. If $r$ is an inner vertex of $P$, it occurs once; the part of $P$ up to $r$ ends with a loop edge and is a path in $H$ from $u$ to $r_{\text{in}}$, and the part after $r$ is a path in $H$ from $r_{\text{out}}$ to $v$. Either way $w(P)$ is at least the minimum.
>
> **The same answer in closed form.** In $H$, the only paths from a node $x$ go down the tree, possibly ending with a loop edge. So $\delta_H(x, y)$ is the length of the tree path when $y$ is in $T_x$ and $\infty$ otherwise, $\delta_H(r_{\text{out}}, y) = D(y)$ and $\delta_H(x, r_{\text{in}}) = L(x)$, where
> - $D(y)$ is the length of the tree path from $r$ down to $y$: $D(r) = 0$ and $D(y) = D(x) + w(x\to y)$ for a child $y$ of $x$;
> - $L(x)$ is the cheapest way from $x$ back to $r$ — down to a leaf of $T_x$, then its loop edge: $L(x) = w(x\to r)$ if $x$ is a leaf, and $L(x) = \min_y\{w(x\to y) + L(y)\}$ over the children $y$ of $x$ otherwise.
>
> The formula becomes
> $$
> \delta(u, v) = \begin{cases} D(v) - D(u) & \text{if } u \text{ is an ancestor of } v \text{ (or } u = v\text{)},\\ L(u) + D(v) & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
> $$
> the formula of [[2-algorithms/08-shortest-paths-exercises#exercise-81--looped-trees|Ex. 8.1(b)]]. (When $u$ is an ancestor of $v$, the tree path always wins: $L(u) + D(v)\ge D(v)\ge D(v) - D(u)$.) It covers the corner cases: $\delta(r, v) = D(v)$, $\delta(u, r) = L(u)$ for $u\ne r$, $\delta(u, u) = 0$. Computed directly: walk up from $v$ along parent pointers, adding the weights; if you meet $u$, the sum so far is the answer; otherwise the sum is $D(v)$, and one postorder traversal of $T_u$ evaluates the recurrence for $L(u)$. That is $O(n)$ time — $O(\text{depth}(v) + \lvert T_u\rvert)$, to be precise. For many queries, compute $D$, $L$ and pre- and postorder numbers for all nodes once, in $O(n)$ time; then each query takes $O(1)$ time ($u = v$ or $u$ is an ancestor of $v$ iff $u$ comes no later than $v$ in preorder and no earlier in postorder).
>
> **On the handout's tree:**
>
> | node | $r$ | $a$ | $b$ | $c$ | $d$ | $e$ | $f$ | $g$ | $h$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | $D$ | 0 | 5 | 8 | 22 | 5 | 45 | 9 | 18 | 23 |
> | $L$ | 21 | 16 | 17 | 27 | 16 | 4 | 16 | 7 | 42 |
>
> $\delta(b, d) = L(b) + D(d) = 17 + 5 = 22$, along $b\,f\,g\,r\,a\,d$ (figure); $\delta(a, e) = D(e) - D(a) = 45 - 5 = 40$, the tree path (through the root it would cost $L(a) + D(e) = 61$); $\delta(c, a) = L(c) + D(a) = 27 + 5 = 32$.
>
> **Linear time is the best possible for one query.** If $v$ is not in $T_u$, the answer depends on the loop edge of every leaf of $T_u$: with all tree edges of weight 0 and all loop edges of weight 1, $L(u) = 1$, and lowering any single loop edge below $u$ to 0 makes $L(u) = 0$. So an algorithm that never looks at one of those edges can be fooled, and $T_u$ can contain about $n/2$ leaves.
>
> **If the looped tree is given only as a directed graph.** With at least two leaves, the root is the only vertex whose in-degree is not 1 (its in-degree is the number of leaves); with one leaf the looped tree is a single directed cycle, and any vertex can serve as the root. The leaves are the non-root vertices with an edge into the root, and parent pointers come from the other edges: $O(n)$ time.
>
> **Answer.** $\delta(u,v) = D(v) - D(u)$ if $u = v$ or $u$ is an ancestor of $v$, and $L(u) + D(v)$ otherwise — two runs of DagSSSP in the looped tree with its root split into a source and a sink, or a walk up from $v$ plus a postorder traversal of $T_u$: $O(n)$ time, and $O(1)$ per query after $O(n)$ preprocessing.

> The tree edges point down, so a walk cannot climb to a common ancestor: the only edges that go up are the loop edges, and they jump straight to the root.
