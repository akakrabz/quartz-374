> [!definition] Definition — context-free grammar
> A **context-free grammar** $G$ has a finite set $\Sigma$ of **terminals**, a finite set $\Gamma$ of **nonterminals** disjoint from $\Sigma$, a **start** nonterminal $S$, and finitely many **rules** $A\to\omega$ with $A\in\Gamma$ and $\omega\in(\Sigma\cup\Gamma)^*$. Applying a rule is one derivation step, $xAz\rightsquigarrow x\omega z$; $\rightsquigarrow^*$ means zero or more steps. Each nonterminal has a language $L(A) = \{w\in\Sigma^* \mid A\rightsquigarrow^* w\}$, and $L(G) = L(S)$. A language is **context-free** if some grammar generates it.

**Where it comes from.** A grammar is a recursive definition: each nonterminal names a language, and each rule is one way to build its strings by concatenating smaller pieces, with $\mid$ for union. Regular expressions are the special case that recurses only through the star — union, concatenation, and $S\to\varepsilon\mid S_1S$ for star — so every regular language is context-free. Recursion adds matching, as in $C\to\mathtt{0}C\mathtt{1}\mid\varepsilon$ for $\{\mathtt{0}^n\mathtt{1}^n\}$, which no DFA can do.

> [!template] Template — proving $L(G) = L$
> **$L\subseteq L(G)$.** Let $w\in L$ be arbitrary, and assume every shorter string of $L$ is generated (or induct on a parameter such as $n$). Split $w$ into pieces that match the right side of one rule, then derive $w$ with that rule and the hypothesis for the pieces. \
> **$L(G)\subseteq L$.** Let $w\in L(G)$ be arbitrary and fix a shortest derivation of $w$; assume every string with a shorter derivation is in $L$. Case on the first rule of the derivation, and apply the hypothesis to the substrings derived from the nonterminals on its right side. \
> **Several nonterminals?** State one claim per nonterminal and prove them all together (mutual induction).

> [!trap]
> - $\varepsilon$-rules break "the pieces are shorter": with $S\to SS\mid\varepsilon$, one piece may be empty. Induct on the length of a shortest derivation instead.
> - Describe the language of **every** nonterminal in a sentence that makes sense on its own.
> - The classic one-mistake error is a missing or extra $\varepsilon$: test $\varepsilon$ and all strings of length 1 and 2.
> - Every left side is a single nonterminal; right sides may mix terminals and nonterminals freely.

**Example.** $L = \{\mathtt{0}^a\mathtt{1}^b\mathtt{0}^c \mid b = a+c\}$ is generated by
$$
S\to AB \qquad A\to\mathtt{0}A\mathtt{1}\mid\varepsilon \qquad B\to\mathtt{1}B\mathtt{0}\mid\varepsilon
$$
$A$ generates $\{\mathtt{0}^n\mathtt{1}^n \mid n\ge0\}$, $B$ generates $\{\mathtt{1}^n\mathtt{0}^n \mid n\ge0\}$, and $S$ concatenates one of each, because $\mathtt{0}^a\mathtt{1}^{a+c}\mathtt{0}^c = \mathtt{0}^a\mathtt{1}^a\bullet\mathtt{1}^c\mathtt{0}^c$. The same language is not regular: $\mathtt{0}^*$ is a [[concepts/fooling-sets|fooling set]], with $z = \mathtt{1}^i$ for $x = \mathtt{0}^i$, because every string in $L$ has as many 1s as 0s.

> [!exam] How grammars are graded
> 2 points for syntax and 8 for correctness, with exam deductions twice the homework ones: 2 for one mistake (typically $\varepsilon$), 4 for a few wrong strings, all 8 if infinitely many strings are wrong; a grammar for $\varnothing$ or $\Sigma^*$ earns nothing. Homework also requires an English description of every nonterminal's language. Ambiguity costs nothing. On Midterm-1-style exams the grammar is paired with a fooling-set proof for the same language.
