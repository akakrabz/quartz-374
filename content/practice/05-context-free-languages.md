## Easy

### 5.1 Closure and ambiguity, true or false

> [!easy] Easy · true or false · context-free closure · ambiguity
> All languages are over $\Sigma = \{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}$. Answer each statement **Yes** (always true) or **No**, with a one-sentence reason; for a No, give a counterexample. You may use the fact ([[1-models-of-computation/05-context-free-languages#56-not-every-language-is-context-free|§5.6]]) that $\{\mathtt{0}^n\mathtt{1}^n\mathtt{0}^n \mid n\ge0\}$ is not context-free.
>
> (a) If $L_1\cup L_2$ is context-free, then $L_1$ and $L_2$ are both context-free.
>
> (b) If $L$ is context-free and $R$ is regular, then $L\setminus R$ is context-free.
>
> (c) If $L$ is context-free and $R$ is regular, then $R\setminus L$ is context-free.
>
> (d) If $L$ is context-free but not regular, then $\overline{L}$ is not regular.
>
> (e) If a grammar $G$ is ambiguous, then $L(G)$ is infinite.
>
> (f) Every regular language is generated by some unambiguous grammar.
>
> *Source: original.*

> [!hint]- Hint
> Closure under union says nothing about the pieces of a union. For (b) and (c), write each difference as an intersection with a complement, and ask which language gets complemented. For (f), think of the grammar you get from a DFA.

> [!solution]- Solution
> **(a) No.** Take $L_1 = \{\mathtt{0}^n\mathtt{1}^n\mathtt{0}^n \mid n\ge0\}$ and $L_2 = \overline{L_1}$: their union is $\Sigma^*$, generated by $S\to\mathtt{0}S\mid\mathtt{1}S\mid\varepsilon$, but $L_1$ is not context-free.
>
> **(b) Yes.** $L\setminus R = L\cap\overline{R}$, the complement of a regular language is regular, and the intersection of a context-free language with a regular language is context-free (construction below).
>
> **(c) No.** Take $R = \Sigma^*$ and $L = \overline{\{\mathtt{0}^n\mathtt{1}^n\mathtt{0}^n \mid n\ge0\}}$, which is context-free (grammar below); then $R\setminus L = \{\mathtt{0}^n\mathtt{1}^n\mathtt{0}^n \mid n\ge0\}$ is not.
>
> **(d) Yes.** If $\overline{L}$ were regular, then $L = \overline{\overline{L}}$ would be regular too, because regular languages are closed under complement (Corollary 3.4).
>
> **(e) No.** $S\to A\mid B$, $A\to\mathtt{0}$, $B\to\mathtt{0}$ is ambiguous — `0` has two parse trees — but its language is $\{\mathtt{0}\}$.
>
> **(f) Yes.** The grammar of a DFA — one nonterminal $[q]$ per state, rules $[q]\to a\,[\delta(q,a)]$, and $[q]\to\varepsilon$ for every accepting $q$ — gives each accepted string exactly one parse tree, which traces the DFA's unique run ([[#52-a-grammar-from-a-dfa|Problem 5.2]] builds one).
>
> **The grammar for (c).** A string is *not* of the form $\mathtt{0}^n\mathtt{1}^n\mathtt{0}^n$ exactly when it has a 1, a later 0 and a still later 1 (so it is not in $\mathtt{0}^*\mathtt{1}^*\mathtt{0}^*$), or it is $\mathtt{0}^a\mathtt{1}^b\mathtt{0}^c$ with $a\ne b$, or with $b\ne c$:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> &S\to N\mid UZ\mid ZV && N\to X\mathtt{1}X\mathtt{0}X\mathtt{1}X && X\to\mathtt{0}X\mid\mathtt{1}X\mid\varepsilon\\
> &U\to\mathtt{0}U\mathtt{1}\mid\mathtt{0}Z\mid Y\mathtt{1} && V\to\mathtt{1}V\mathtt{0}\mid\mathtt{1}Y\mid Z\mathtt{0} && Z\to\mathtt{0}Z\mid\varepsilon \qquad Y\to\mathtt{1}Y\mid\varepsilon
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> Here $U$ generates $\{\mathtt{0}^a\mathtt{1}^b \mid a\ne b\}$, $V$ generates $\{\mathtt{1}^b\mathtt{0}^c \mid b\ne c\}$, $Z$ and $Y$ generate $\mathtt{0}^*$ and $\mathtt{1}^*$, and $X$ generates every string.
>
> **The construction for (b).** Given a grammar for $L$ and a DFA $M = (\Sigma, Q, s, A, \delta)$, use nonterminals $B_{pq}$ for every nonterminal $B$ and states $p, q$, meaning "the strings of $L(B)$ that drive $M$ from $p$ to $q$". For every rule $B\to X_1\cdots X_k$ and every choice of states $p = r_0, r_1, \dots, r_k = q$, add $B_{pq}\to Y_1\cdots Y_k$, where $Y_i = (X_i)_{r_{i-1}r_i}$ if $X_i$ is a nonterminal, and $Y_i = X_i$ if $X_i$ is a terminal with $\delta(r_{i-1},X_i) = r_i$ (if it is a terminal without that transition, skip this choice); a rule $B\to\varepsilon$ gives $B_{pp}\to\varepsilon$. A new start symbol with $S'\to S_{sf}$ for every $f\in A$ generates $L\cap L(M)$. For (b), let $M$ be a DFA for $\overline{R}$.
>
> **Watch out:** (b) and (c) differ only in which language is complemented. Context-free languages survive intersection with a regular language but not complementation, and $R\setminus L = R\cap\overline{L}$ needs $\overline{L}$.
>
> **Answer.** (a) No (b) Yes (c) No (d) Yes (e) No (f) Yes.

### 5.2 A grammar from a DFA

> [!easy] Easy · grammar design · parse trees
> The DFA $M$ with states $0, 1, 2$, start state $0$, accepting states $\{0\}$ and $\delta(q,a) = (2q+a)\bmod 3$ accepts the binary representations of the multiples of 3 (reading $\varepsilon$ as the number 0).
>
> (a) Turn $M$ into a context-free grammar with one nonterminal $R_q$ for each state $q$, and describe the language of each $R_q$ in a self-contained sentence.
>
> (b) Give the derivation of `1001` (nine in binary) and compare it with the run of $M$.
>
> (c) Explain in one or two sentences why your grammar is unambiguous.
>
> *Source: textbook exercise style.*

> [!hint]- Hint
> A rule $R_q\to a\,R_p$ says "read $a$, then continue from state $p$" — the construction of Lemma 5.6 for an automaton with a single module. Where must a derivation be allowed to stop?

> [!solution]- Solution
> **Idea.** $R_q$ generates "the rest of the input" when $M$ is in state $q$: the strings that lead from $q$ to an accepting state. One rule per transition, and one $\varepsilon$-rule per accepting state.
>
> **(a)** The transitions are $\delta(0,\mathtt{0}) = 0$, $\delta(0,\mathtt{1}) = 1$, $\delta(1,\mathtt{0}) = 2$, $\delta(1,\mathtt{1}) = 0$, $\delta(2,\mathtt{0}) = 1$, $\delta(2,\mathtt{1}) = 2$. With start symbol $R_0$:
> $$
> R_0\to\mathtt{0}R_0\mid\mathtt{1}R_1\mid\varepsilon \qquad R_1\to\mathtt{0}R_2\mid\mathtt{1}R_0 \qquad R_2\to\mathtt{0}R_1\mid\mathtt{1}R_2
> $$
> Write $\textsf{val}(x)$ for the number whose binary representation is $x$. A prefix of value $v$ leaves $M$ in state $q = v\bmod 3$, and reading $x$ after it gives the value $v\cdot2^{\lvert x\rvert}+\textsf{val}(x)$, whose remainder mod 3 depends on $v$ only through $q$. So $R_q$ generates the strings $x$ for which $q\cdot2^{\lvert x\rvert}+\textsf{val}(x)$ is a multiple of 3; for $q = 0, 1, 2$ that number is the value of $x$, of $\mathtt{1}x$ and of $\mathtt{10}x$:
> - $R_0$ generates the binary representations of the multiples of 3;
> - $R_1$ generates the strings $x$ such that $\mathtt{1}x$ represents a multiple of 3;
> - $R_2$ generates the strings $x$ such that $\mathtt{10}x$ represents a multiple of 3.
>
> **(b)** $R_0\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{1}R_1\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{10}R_2\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{100}R_1\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{1001}R_0\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{1001}$. The nonterminals $R_0, R_1, R_2, R_1, R_0$ are exactly the states $0, 1, 2, 1, 0$ that $M$ visits on `1001`, and the last step, $R_0\to\varepsilon$, is allowed because the run ends in the accepting state 0.
>
> **(c)** Every sentential form has a single nonterminal, at its end, and each $R_q$ has one rule per next symbol (plus $R_0\to\varepsilon$, which can only finish the derivation), so every step of a derivation of $w$ is forced by the next symbol of $w$. Hence $w$ has at most one derivation, so at most one parse tree — the run of $M$ on $w$.
>
> **Check:** `11` (three): $R_0\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{1}R_1\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{11}R_0\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{11}$. `10` (two): $R_0\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{1}R_1\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{10}R_2$, and $R_2$ has no $\varepsilon$-rule, so the derivation cannot finish. $\varepsilon$ is generated, as the number 0 should be.
>
> **Watch out:** the $\varepsilon$-rules are where acceptance lives. Without $R_0\to\varepsilon$ no derivation ever ends and the grammar generates $\varnothing$; an $\varepsilon$-rule on a non-accepting state adds wrong strings ($R_1\to\varepsilon$ would generate `1`).
>
> **Answer.** $R_0\to\mathtt{0}R_0\mid\mathtt{1}R_1\mid\varepsilon$, $R_1\to\mathtt{0}R_2\mid\mathtt{1}R_0$, $R_2\to\mathtt{0}R_1\mid\mathtt{1}R_2$, start $R_0$; $R_q$ generates the strings that take $M$ from $q$ to state 0, i.e. the $x$ for which $x$, $\mathtt{1}x$, $\mathtt{10}x$ (for $q = 0, 1, 2$) represents a multiple of 3. It is unambiguous because each derivation step is forced by the next symbol.

### 5.3 Find the error, a union

> [!easy] Easy · find the error · grammar design
> A student is asked for a context-free grammar for
> $$
> L = \{\mathtt{0}^n\mathtt{1}^n \mid n\ge0\}\cup\{\mathtt{0}^n\mathtt{1}^{2n} \mid n\ge0\},
> $$
> the strings of 0s followed by either equally many or twice as many 1s, and answers:
>
> *"$S\to\mathtt{0}S\mathtt{1}\mid\mathtt{0}S\mathtt{11}\mid\varepsilon$. Each rule adds one 0 together with one or two 1s, so every 0 is matched with one 1 or with two 1s."*
>
> (a) What is wrong? Give a shortest string that shows it.
>
> (b) Fix the grammar, and describe the language of every nonterminal.
>
> *Source: original.*

> [!hint]- Hint
> Does the student's sentence describe $L$? Try a derivation that uses both rules.

> [!solution]- Solution
> **(a) The slip: the shape is chosen at every step instead of once.** One derivation may mix the two rules, so the grammar generates every $\mathtt{0}^n\mathtt{1}^m$ with $n\le m\le 2n$. The shortest wrong string is `00111`:
> $$
> S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{0}S\mathtt{1}\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{00}S\mathtt{111}\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{00111},
> $$
> with two 0s and three 1s — neither equally many nor twice as many. Every $\mathtt{0}^n\mathtt{1}^{n+1}$ with $n\ge2$ is wrong too, so there are infinitely many wrong strings, which on an exam costs all the correctness points. (Nothing is missing: every string of $L$ is generated.) The student's sentence describes each 0 separately, but $L$ needs *all* the 0s to be matched the same way.
>
> **(b)** Choose the shape once, at the top:
> $$
> S\to A\mid B \qquad A\to\mathtt{0}A\mathtt{1}\mid\varepsilon \qquad B\to\mathtt{0}B\mathtt{11}\mid\varepsilon
> $$
> - $A$ generates $\{\mathtt{0}^n\mathtt{1}^n \mid n\ge0\}$;
> - $B$ generates $\{\mathtt{0}^n\mathtt{1}^{2n} \mid n\ge0\}$;
> - $S$ generates $L(A)\cup L(B) = L$.
>
> **Check:** `0011` comes from $A$ and `011` from $B$; `00111` comes from neither. The empty string has two parse trees (through $A$ and through $B$), so the fixed grammar is ambiguous — which costs nothing.
>
> **Watch out:** the student's grammar is a correct answer to a different question — it generates $\{\mathtt{0}^i\mathtt{1}^j \mid i\le j\le 2i\}$, Example 1 of [[1-models-of-computation/05-context-free-languages#designing-a-grammar|§5.4]]. For a union of two shapes, branch once, with $S\to A\mid B$.
>
> **Answer.** (a) One derivation can mix the two rules; the shortest wrong string is `00111`, and there are infinitely many more. (b) $S\to A\mid B$, $A\to\mathtt{0}A\mathtt{1}\mid\varepsilon$, $B\to\mathtt{0}B\mathtt{11}\mid\varepsilon$.

### 5.4 Recursive automaton to grammar

> [!easy] Easy · recursive automata · grammar design
> A recursive automaton over $\Sigma = \{\mathtt{1},{+},\mathtt{(},\mathtt{)}\}$ has main module $E$ and one more module $T$; an edge labeled with a module name calls that module.
>
> | module | start | accepting | edges |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | $E$ | $s_E$ | $t_E$ | $s_E\xrightarrow{T}x$, $\ x\xrightarrow{+}s_E$, $\ x\xrightarrow{\varepsilon}t_E$ |
> | $T$ | $s_T$ | $t_T$ | $s_T\xrightarrow{\mathtt{1}}t_T$, $\ s_T\xrightarrow{\mathtt{(}}y$, $\ y\xrightarrow{E}z$, $\ z\xrightarrow{\mathtt{)}}t_T$ |
>
> (a) Write the grammar of Lemma 5.6, with one nonterminal $[q]$ for each state $q$.
>
> (b) Describe the language of every nonterminal.
>
> (c) Simplify the grammar to two nonterminals, and decide whether `(1+1)+1`, `1+` and `()` are accepted.
>
> *Source: original.*

> [!hint]- Hint
> Each edge gives one rule: reading $a$ on an edge from $q$ to $p$ gives $[q]\to a\,[p]$, an $\varepsilon$-edge gives $[q]\to[p]$, and a call of module $A$ gives $[q]\to[s_A]\,[p]$ — run all of $A$, then continue at $p$. The accepting state of each module gets the return rule $[t_A]\to\varepsilon$.

> [!solution]- Solution
> **(a)** One rule per edge plus one return rule per module ([[1-models-of-computation/05-context-free-languages#57-recursive-automata|Lemma 5.6]]), with start symbol $[s_E]$:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> &[s_E]\to[s_T]\,[x] && [x]\to{+}\,[s_E]\mid[t_E] && [t_E]\to\varepsilon\\
> &[s_T]\to\mathtt{1}\,[t_T]\mid\mathtt{(}\,[y] && [y]\to[s_E]\,[z] && [z]\to\mathtt{)}\,[t_T] \qquad [t_T]\to\varepsilon
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> **(b)** Call a string an **expression** if it is $t_1{+}t_2{+}\cdots{+}t_k$ for some $k\ge1$, where each **term** $t_i$ is `1` or $\mathtt{(}e\mathtt{)}$ for an expression $e$. Then
> - $[s_E]$ generates the expressions, and $[s_T]$ the terms;
> - $[x]$ generates $\varepsilon$ and the strings that consist of `+` followed by an expression — what may follow a term inside an expression;
> - $[y]$ generates the strings that consist of an expression followed by `)` — what must follow an opening parenthesis;
> - $[z]$ generates only `)`, and $[t_E]$ and $[t_T]$ generate only $\varepsilon$.
>
> **(c)** The nonterminals $[t_E]$, $[t_T]$ and $[z]$ each generate a single string, so substitute it for them; then $[y]$ becomes $[s_E]$ followed by `)`, and $[x]$ becomes $\varepsilon$ or $+$ followed by $[s_E]$. Renaming $[s_E]$, $[s_T]$ to $E$, $T$ gives
> $$
> E\to T\mid T{+}E \qquad T\to\mathtt{1}\mid\mathtt{(}E\mathtt{)}
> $$
> `(1+1)+1` is accepted: $E\rightsquigarrow T{+}E\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{(}E\mathtt{)}{+}E\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{(}T{+}E\mathtt{)}{+}E\rightsquigarrow^*\mathtt{(1{+}1){+}}E\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{(1{+}1){+}}T\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{(1{+}1){+}1}$. `1+` is not: a `+` must be followed by an expression, and expressions are non-empty. `()` is not: between the parentheses there must be an expression.
>
> **Watch out:** a call edge is not a terminal. $s_E\xrightarrow{T}x$ becomes $[s_E]\to[s_T]\,[x]$ — run module $T$ to its end, then continue at $x$ — and not $[s_E]\to T\,[x]$. And without the return rules $[t_E]\to\varepsilon$, $[t_T]\to\varepsilon$ no derivation ever finishes.
>
> **Answer.** (a) The nine rules above (seven nonterminals). (b) $[s_E]$: expressions; $[s_T]$: terms; $[x]$: the empty string or `+` followed by an expression; $[y]$: an expression followed by `)`; $[z]$: only `)`; $[t_E]$, $[t_T]$: only $\varepsilon$. (c) $E\to T\mid T{+}E$, $T\to\mathtt{1}\mid\mathtt{(}E\mathtt{)}$; `(1+1)+1` is accepted, `1+` and `()` are not.

## Medium

### 5.5 Two grammars with a separator

> [!medium] Medium · grammar design
> Let $\Sigma = \{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1},\mathtt{\#}\}$. Give a context-free grammar for each language, and describe the language of every nonterminal in a self-contained sentence. No proof is required.
>
> (a) $L_a = \{u\mathtt{\#}v \mid u,v\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^*,\ \#(\mathtt{1},u) = \#(\mathtt{1},v)\}$: the two sides of the `#` contain equally many 1s.
>
> (b) $L_b = \{x\mathtt{\#}y \mid x,y\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^*,\ y \text{ is a substring of } x^R\}$. For example, `0110#1` and `01#10` are in $L_b$, but `01#01` is not.
>
> *Source: original.*

> [!hint]- Hint
> (a) Only the 1s need partners: pair the first 1 of $u$ with the last 1 of $v$, and let 0s appear anywhere. (b) $y$ is a substring of $x^R$ exactly when $x = u\,y^R\,v$ for some strings $u$ and $v$. Write $p = y^R$; then $x\mathtt{\#}y = u\cdot p\,v\mathtt{\#}p^R$, and in the second part $p$ and $p^R$ are nested around $v\mathtt{\#}$.

> [!solution]- Solution
> **(a) Idea.** Match the 1s of $u$ with the 1s of $v$ from the outside in. The 0s are unconstrained, so they can be added at either end at any time.
> $$
> S\to\mathtt{0}S\mid S\mathtt{0}\mid\mathtt{1}S\mathtt{1}\mid\mathtt{\#}
> $$
> $S$ is the only nonterminal, and it generates exactly $L_a$. Every rule keeps the numbers of 1s on the two sides equal. Conversely, a string $u\mathtt{\#}v\in L_a$ can be taken apart by the rules: if $u$ starts with 0 or $v$ ends with 0, remove that 0; otherwise either $u = v = \varepsilon$ (the string is `#`), or $u = \mathtt{1}u'$ and $v = v'\mathtt{1}$ with $u'\mathtt{\#}v'\in L_a$.
>
> **Check:** $S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{1}S\mathtt{1}\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{1}S\mathtt{01}\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{1\#01}$. `1#0` is not generated, because every 1 is created together with a partner on the other side.
>
> **(b) Idea.** $y$ is a substring of $x^R$ if and only if $y^R$ is a substring of $x$, that is, $x = u\,p\,v$ with $p = y^R$. Then $x\mathtt{\#}y = u\cdot\big(p\,v\mathtt{\#}p^R\big)$: the prefix $u$ is arbitrary, and the rest nests the symbols of $p$ around $v\mathtt{\#}$.
> $$
> S\to ZT \qquad T\to\mathtt{0}T\mathtt{0}\mid\mathtt{1}T\mathtt{1}\mid Z\mathtt{\#} \qquad Z\to\mathtt{0}Z\mid\mathtt{1}Z\mid\varepsilon
> $$
> - $Z$ generates all strings over $\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}$.
> - $T$ generates the strings $x\mathtt{\#}y$ with $x,y\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^*$ such that $y^R$ is a prefix of $x$ — equivalently, $y$ is a suffix of $x^R$.
> - $S$ generates $L_b$: a string of $T$ with an arbitrary string added in front.
>
> **Check:** `01#10` has $u = v = \varepsilon$ and $p = \mathtt{01}$: $S\rightsquigarrow ZT\rightsquigarrow T\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{0}T\mathtt{0}\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{01}T\mathtt{10}\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{01}Z\mathtt{\#10}\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{01\#10}$. For `0110#1` take $u = \mathtt{0}$, $p = \mathtt{1}$, $v = \mathtt{10}$. And `01#01` is not generated: $p = (\mathtt{01})^R = \mathtt{10}$ is not a substring of `01`.
>
> **Watch out:** the $Z$ in $T\to Z\mathtt{\#}$ is easy to forget. Without it, $T$ generates only the strings $p\mathtt{\#}p^R$, and the grammar finds $y$ only at the *start* of $x^R$: it misses `10#1`, whose $y = \mathtt{1}$ is a substring of $x^R = \mathtt{01}$ but not a prefix.
>
> **Answer.** (a) $S\to\mathtt{0}S\mid S\mathtt{0}\mid\mathtt{1}S\mathtt{1}\mid\mathtt{\#}$. (b) $S\to ZT$, $T\to\mathtt{0}T\mathtt{0}\mid\mathtt{1}T\mathtt{1}\mid Z\mathtt{\#}$, $Z\to\mathtt{0}Z\mid\mathtt{1}Z\mid\varepsilon$.

### 5.6 The dangling else

> [!medium] Medium · ambiguity · parse trees
> The grammar $S\to\mathtt{0}S\mathtt{1}S\mid\mathtt{0}S\mid\varepsilon$ generates the set $P$ of strings in which every prefix has at least as many 0s as 1s ([[#510-prefixes-of-balanced-strings-a-proof|Problem 5.10]] proves this). Read `0` as *if* and `1` as *else*.
>
> (a) Find a shortest string with two different parse trees, and give both as leftmost derivations.
>
> (b) Give an unambiguous grammar for $P$, describe the language of every nonterminal, and explain why every string of $P$ has exactly one parse tree.
>
> *Source: classic.*

> [!hint]- Hint
> (a) An *else* after two *if*s can belong to either of them. (b) Let every 1 belong to the nearest unmatched 0 before it: in a rule $S\to\mathtt{0}M\mathtt{1}S$, let $M$ generate only *balanced* strings (equally many 0s and 1s, and every prefix with at least as many 0s as 1s).

> [!solution]- Solution
> **(a)** `001`. Every string of length at most 2 has at most one parse tree, and among the strings of length 3 only `001` has two:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> &S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{0}S\mathtt{1}S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{00}S\mathtt{1}S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{001}S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{001} && \text{(the 1 belongs to the first 0)}\\
> &S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{0}S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{00}S\mathtt{1}S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{001}S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{001} && \text{(the 1 belongs to the second 0)}
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> As trees: $S(\mathtt{0},\,S(\mathtt{0},S(\varepsilon)),\,\mathtt{1},\,S(\varepsilon))$ and $S(\mathtt{0},\,S(\mathtt{0},S(\varepsilon),\mathtt{1},S(\varepsilon)))$. This is the *dangling else*: in `if if … else …` the grammar does not say which *if* the *else* belongs to.
>
> **(b) Idea.** Let every 1 close the nearest unmatched 0, by requiring the part between a 0 and its 1 to be balanced.
> $$
> S\to\mathtt{0}S\mid\mathtt{0}M\mathtt{1}S\mid\varepsilon \qquad M\to\mathtt{0}M\mathtt{1}M\mid\varepsilon
> $$
> - $M$ generates the balanced strings: equally many 0s and 1s, and every prefix with at least as many 0s as 1s.
> - $S$ generates $P$.
>
> **Why there is one parse tree.** Let $w\in P$ be non-empty; it starts with 0. The rule $S\to\mathtt{0}M\mathtt{1}S$ produces strings $\mathtt{0}m\mathtt{1}s$ whose prefix $\mathtt{0}m\mathtt{1}$ is balanced while every shorter non-empty prefix, $\mathtt{0}m'$ with $m'$ a prefix of $m$, has more 0s than 1s; so $\mathtt{0}m\mathtt{1}$ must be the **shortest non-empty balanced prefix** of the string. The rule $S\to\mathtt{0}S$ produces strings with *no* non-empty balanced prefix. Hence:
> - if $w$ has a non-empty balanced prefix, only $S\to\mathtt{0}M\mathtt{1}S$ fits, and the split $w = \mathtt{0}m\mathtt{1}s$ is forced: cut after the shortest such prefix;
> - otherwise only $S\to\mathtt{0}S$ fits, with $w = \mathtt{0}s$.
>
> Either way the first rule and the pieces are determined, and the same argument works for $M$ (a non-empty balanced string must be cut after its shortest non-empty balanced prefix). By induction on the length, every string of $P$ has exactly one parse tree. The language is still $P$: the two cases are exactly those of the proof in Problem 5.10.
>
> **Check:** `001` now has only $S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{0}S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{00}M\mathtt{1}S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{001}S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{001}$ — the else belongs to the nearest if, the rule of C and Java. Another unambiguous grammar puts the unmatched 0s at the top level instead: $S\to M\mid M\mathtt{0}S$ with the same $M$.
>
> **Watch out:** to show ambiguity you need one string with two parse trees — two *leftmost* derivations; two derivations that only expand the nonterminals in another order build the same tree. And the unambiguous grammar must still generate all of $P$: test it on strings such as `0101` and `0010`.
>
> **Answer.** (a) `001`: $S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{0}S\mathtt{1}S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{00}S\mathtt{1}S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{001}S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{001}$ and $S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{0}S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{00}S\mathtt{1}S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{001}S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{001}$. (b) $S\to\mathtt{0}S\mid\mathtt{0}M\mathtt{1}S\mid\varepsilon$, $M\to\mathtt{0}M\mathtt{1}M\mid\varepsilon$, where $M$ generates the balanced strings.

### 5.7 A small CNF conversion

> [!medium] Medium · Chomsky normal form
> Consider the grammar
> $$
> S\to\mathtt{0}S\mathtt{1}\mid AB \qquad A\to\mathtt{0}A\mid\varepsilon \qquad B\to\mathtt{1}B\mid\varepsilon
> $$
> (a) What language does it generate?
>
> (b) Convert it to Chomsky normal form with the algorithm of [[1-models-of-computation/05-context-free-languages#59-the-cnf-conversion-algorithm|§5.9]], showing the grammar after each step.
>
> (c) How many steps does every derivation of `0011` take in your final grammar, and why?
>
> *Source: original.*

> [!hint]- Hint
> Every step except step 4 changes something. When you look for nullable nonterminals, do not stop at the $\varepsilon$-rules: $S\to AB$ makes $S$ nullable, and then the new start symbol too. In step 5, copy the rules of $A$ and $B$ as they are *after* step 3.

> [!solution]- Solution
> **(a)** $A$ generates $\mathtt{0}^*$ and $B$ generates $\mathtt{1}^*$, so $AB$ generates $\mathtt{0}^*\mathtt{1}^*$; the rule $S\to\mathtt{0}S\mathtt{1}$ wraps a string of $\mathtt{0}^*\mathtt{1}^*$ in one more `0`…`1`, which stays in $\mathtt{0}^*\mathtt{1}^*$. So the language is $\mathtt{0}^*\mathtt{1}^*$ — regular, which makes every step easy to test.
>
> **(b)** The grammar after each step:
>
> | step | what happens | grammar |
> |---|---|---|
> | 0 | $S$ is on a right side and nullable: new start symbol $S'$ | $S'\to S$; $\ S\to\mathtt{0}S\mathtt{1}\mid AB$; $\ A\to\mathtt{0}A\mid\varepsilon$; $\ B\to\mathtt{1}B\mid\varepsilon$ |
> | 1 | split $S\to\mathtt{0}S\mathtt{1}$ into $S\to\mathtt{0}C$ and $C\to S\mathtt{1}$ | $S'\to S$; $\ S\to\mathtt{0}C\mid AB$; $\ C\to S\mathtt{1}$; $\ A\to\mathtt{0}A\mid\varepsilon$; $\ B\to\mathtt{1}B\mid\varepsilon$ |
> | 2 | nullable: $A$ and $B$ ($\varepsilon$-rules), then $S$ (by $S\to AB$), then $S'$; not $C$ (its rule contains the terminal 1) | unchanged |
> | 3 | add the variants without a nullable occurrence; drop the $\varepsilon$-rules, but add $S'\to\varepsilon$ | $S'\to S\mid\varepsilon$; $\ S\to\mathtt{0}C\mid AB\mid A\mid B$; $\ C\to S\mathtt{1}\mid\mathtt{1}$; $\ A\to\mathtt{0}A\mid\mathtt{0}$; $\ B\to\mathtt{1}B\mid\mathtt{1}$ |
> | 4 | the unit rules $S'\to S$, $S\to A$, $S\to B$ form no cycle: nothing to merge | unchanged |
> | 5 | copy the rules of $A$ and $B$ into $S$, then the new rules of $S$ into $S'$ | $S'\to\mathtt{0}C\mid AB\mid\mathtt{0}A\mid\mathtt{0}\mid\mathtt{1}B\mid\mathtt{1}\mid\varepsilon$; $\ S\to\mathtt{0}C\mid AB\mid\mathtt{0}A\mid\mathtt{0}\mid\mathtt{1}B\mid\mathtt{1}$; $\ C$, $A$, $B$ as in step 3 |
> | 6 | $T_0\to\mathtt{0}$ and $T_1\to\mathtt{1}$ replace the terminals in rules of length 2 | below |
>
> The final grammar, in Chomsky normal form with start symbol $S'$:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> S'&\to T_0C\mid AB\mid T_0A\mid T_1B\mid\mathtt{0}\mid\mathtt{1}\mid\varepsilon\\
> S&\to T_0C\mid AB\mid T_0A\mid T_1B\mid\mathtt{0}\mid\mathtt{1}\\
> C&\to ST_1\mid\mathtt{1} \qquad A\to T_0A\mid\mathtt{0} \qquad B\to T_1B\mid\mathtt{1} \qquad T_0\to\mathtt{0} \qquad T_1\to\mathtt{1}
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> From step 3 on, $S$ generates $\mathtt{0}^*\mathtt{1}^*$ *without* $\varepsilon$; only $S'$ still generates all of $\mathtt{0}^*\mathtt{1}^*$.
>
> **(c)** Seven. In Chomsky normal form, a parse tree of a string of length $n\ge1$ has exactly $2n-1$ nonterminal nodes — $n$ nodes $X\to a$ above the leaves and $n-1$ nodes $X\to YZ$ — and each derivation step expands one node. For `0011`, $n = 4$; for example
> $$
> S'\rightsquigarrow AB\rightsquigarrow T_0AB\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{0}AB\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{00}B\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{00}T_1B\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{001}B\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{0011}.
> $$
> (`0011` has three parse trees here, exactly as in the original grammar: the conversion does not remove ambiguity.)
>
> **Check:** $S'$ generates $\varepsilon$, `0`, `1` and `0011`, but not `10`. Every rule has the form $X\to YZ$ or $X\to a$, except $S'\to\varepsilon$, and $S'$ appears on no right side. The old start symbol $S$ does (in $C\to ST_1$), which is why it could not stay the start symbol.
>
> **Watch out:**
> - Step 3 adds $S\to A$ and $S\to B$, but never $S\to\varepsilon$: the empty string survives only through $S'\to\varepsilon$.
> - In step 5, copy the rules of $A$ and $B$ as they are after step 3 ($A\to\mathtt{0}A\mid\mathtt{0}$), not the original ones — otherwise $S\to\varepsilon$ comes back.
> - Keep $S'$: the book drops the new start symbol after step 2 only when it is not nullable, and here it is.
>
> **Answer.** (a) $\mathtt{0}^*\mathtt{1}^*$. (b) $S'\to T_0C\mid AB\mid T_0A\mid T_1B\mid\mathtt{0}\mid\mathtt{1}\mid\varepsilon$, $S\to T_0C\mid AB\mid T_0A\mid T_1B\mid\mathtt{0}\mid\mathtt{1}$, $C\to ST_1\mid\mathtt{1}$, $A\to T_0A\mid\mathtt{0}$, $B\to T_1B\mid\mathtt{1}$, $T_0\to\mathtt{0}$, $T_1\to\mathtt{1}$. (c) $2\cdot4-1 = 7$ steps.

## Hard

### 5.8 Enough 1s at the end

> [!hard] Hard · fooling sets · grammar design
> Let $L$ be the set of binary strings whose last $\#(\mathtt{0},w)$ symbols are all 1s:
> $$
> L = \{w\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^* \mid w = x\,\mathtt{1}^{\#(\mathtt{0},w)} \text{ for some string } x\}.
> $$
> For example, `0011`, `101` and `11` are in $L$, but `0101` and `10` are not.
>
> (a) Prove that $L$ is not regular.
>
> (b) Give a context-free grammar for $L$, describe the language of every nonterminal, and explain briefly why the grammar generates exactly $L$.
>
> *Source: original.*

> [!hint]- Hint
> (a) A DFA would have to count the 0s; try $F = \mathtt{0}^*$. (b) Look at the first symbol. Removing a leading 1 keeps a string in $L$. If a string of $L$ starts with 0, what must its last symbol be, and what is left if you remove both?

> [!solution]- Solution
> **(a) Idea.** An algorithm for $L$ must remember how many 0s it has read, to know how many 1s to demand at the end.
>
> **Proof.** Let $F = \mathtt{0}^*$. Let $x$ and $y$ be arbitrary distinct strings in $F$; then $x = \mathtt{0}^i$ and $y = \mathtt{0}^j$ for some integers $i\ne j$. A suffix that distinguishes $x$ from $y$ also distinguishes $y$ from $x$, so we may name the two strings so that $i<j$. Let $z = \mathtt{1}^i$.
>
> Then $xz = \mathtt{0}^i\mathtt{1}^i\in L$, because it has $i$ 0s and its last $i$ symbols are 1s.
>
> And $yz = \mathtt{0}^j\mathtt{1}^i\notin L$, because it has $j$ 0s, but its last $j$ symbols are $\mathtt{0}^{j-i}\mathtt{1}^i$, which contain a 0 because $j>i$.
>
> So $z$ distinguishes $x$ and $y$, and $F$ is a fooling set for $L$. Because $F$ is infinite, $L$ is not regular. $\blacksquare$
>
> **(b) Idea.** Decide by the first symbol: a leading 1 is harmless, and a leading 0 must be paid for with a 1 at the very end.
> $$
> S\to\mathtt{0}S\mathtt{1}\mid\mathtt{1}S\mid\varepsilon
> $$
> $S$ is the only nonterminal, and it generates exactly $L$:
> - **Every rule stays inside $L$.** $\varepsilon\in L$. Let $x\in L$ have $k$ 0s, so $\lvert x\rvert\ge k$ and the last $k$ symbols of $x$ are 1s. Then $\mathtt{1}x$ has the same $k$ 0s and the same last $k$ symbols, so $\mathtt{1}x\in L$; and $\mathtt{0}x\mathtt{1}$ has $k+1$ 0s, and its last $k+1$ symbols are the last $k$ symbols of $x$ followed by `1`, all 1s, so $\mathtt{0}x\mathtt{1}\in L$.
> - **Every string of $L$ is built this way.** Let $w\in L$ be non-empty, with $k$ 0s. If $w = \mathtt{1}x$, then $x$ has the same $k$ 0s and the same last $k$ symbols, so $x\in L$. If $w$ starts with 0, then $k\ge1$, so the last symbol of $w$ is 1 and $w = \mathtt{0}x\mathtt{1}$; now $x$ has $k-1$ 0s, and its last $k-1$ symbols are the symbols just before the final 1 of $w$, all 1s, so $x\in L$. In both cases $x$ is shorter than $w$, so by induction on the length $x$ is generated, and then so is $w$. ([[#510-prefixes-of-balanced-strings-a-proof|Problem 5.10]] shows how such an argument is written out in full.)
>
> **Check:** $S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{0}S\mathtt{1}\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{00}S\mathtt{11}\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{0011}$ and $S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{1}S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{10}S\mathtt{1}\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{101}$. `0101` starts with 0 and ends with 1, but the middle `10` is not in $L$, so `0101` is not generated — as it should be.
>
> **Watch out:** the reflex answer $S\to\mathtt{0}S\mathtt{1}\mid S\mathtt{1}\mid\varepsilon$ generates only strings of the form $\mathtt{0}^m\mathtt{1}^n$ with $n\ge m$. It misses `101`, `1101`, `11101`, … — infinitely many strings, because in $L$ some 1s may come *before* the 0s.
>
> **Grading notes.** Fooling set: 4 points for an explicit infinite fooling set, 6 for the argument with arbitrary $x\ne y$ (2 for the suffix, 2 for each membership claim). Grammar: 2 for syntax and 8 for correctness ([[0-toolkit/06-how-exams-are-graded|how exams are graded]]).
>
> **Answer.** (a) $F = \mathtt{0}^*$: for $\mathtt{0}^i$ and $\mathtt{0}^j$ with $i<j$, the suffix $\mathtt{1}^i$ gives $\mathtt{0}^i\mathtt{1}^i\in L$ and $\mathtt{0}^j\mathtt{1}^i\notin L$. (b) $S\to\mathtt{0}S\mathtt{1}\mid\mathtt{1}S\mid\varepsilon$.

### 5.9 A 1 in the first half

> [!hard] Hard · fooling sets · grammar design
> Let $L$ be the set of binary strings of even length whose first half contains a 1:
> $$
> L = \{xy \mid x,y\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^*,\ \lvert x\rvert = \lvert y\rvert,\ \#(\mathtt{1},x)\ge1\}.
> $$
> For example, `10`, `0100` and `1000` are in $L$, but `01`, `0010` and `101` are not.
>
> (a) Prove that $L$ is not regular.
>
> (b) Give a context-free grammar for $L$, and describe the language of every nonterminal.
>
> *Source: original.*

> [!hint]- Hint
> (a) With $F = \mathtt{0}^*$, put a single 1 into the suffix, so that it lands in the first half of $xz$ but not of $yz$ — and let the parity of $\lvert yz\rvert$ help you. (b) Fix one 1 in the first half and write the string as $u\mathtt{1}v$. How must $\lvert u\rvert$ and $\lvert v\rvert$ be related? Generate $u$ together with the last $\lvert u\rvert$ symbols of $v$.

> [!solution]- Solution
> **(a) Idea.** After reading $\mathtt{0}^i\mathtt{1}$, an algorithm must remember $i$: the 1 lies in the first half exactly when at least $i+1$ more symbols follow (and the total length is even).
>
> **Proof.** Let $F = \mathtt{0}^*$. Let $x$ and $y$ be arbitrary distinct strings in $F$; then $x = \mathtt{0}^i$ and $y = \mathtt{0}^j$ for some integers $i\ne j$. A suffix that distinguishes $x$ from $y$ also distinguishes $y$ from $x$, so we may name the two strings so that $i<j$. Let $z = \mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}^{i+1}$.
>
> Then $xz = \mathtt{0}^i\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}^{i+1}$ has length $2i+2$, and its first half $\mathtt{0}^i\mathtt{1}$ contains a 1, so $xz\in L$.
>
> And $yz = \mathtt{0}^j\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}^{i+1}\notin L$, which has length $i+j+2$. If $i+j$ is odd, that length is odd. If $i+j$ is even, then $j\ge i+2$ (because $j-i$ is even and positive), so the first half has length $\frac{i+j}{2}+1\le j$ and lies inside the block $\mathtt{0}^j$: it contains no 1.
>
> So $z$ distinguishes $x$ and $y$, and $F$ is a fooling set for $L$. Because $F$ is infinite, $L$ is not regular. $\blacksquare$
>
> **(b) Idea.** Pick a 1 in the first half and write the string as $u\mathtt{1}v$. The length $\lvert u\rvert+\lvert v\rvert+1$ must be even and the 1, at position $\lvert u\rvert+1$, must be in the first half; together these say that $\lvert v\rvert-\lvert u\rvert$ is **odd and positive**. So wrap $u$ and the last $\lvert u\rvert$ symbols of $v$ around a 1 followed by a string of odd length.
> $$
> S\to XSX\mid\mathtt{1}O \qquad O\to X\mid XXO \qquad X\to\mathtt{0}\mid\mathtt{1}
> $$
> - $X$ generates the one-symbol strings `0` and `1`.
> - $O$ generates the binary strings of odd length.
> - $S$ generates the strings $u\mathtt{1}v$ in which $\lvert v\rvert-\lvert u\rvert$ is odd and positive, and these are exactly the strings of $L$. If $\lvert v\rvert = \lvert u\rvert+2m+1$, the length is $2(\lvert u\rvert+m+1)$ and the 1 sits at position $\lvert u\rvert+1\le\lvert u\rvert+m+1$, in the first half. Conversely, a 1 at position $k$ of a string of length $2n$ with $k\le n$ gives $\lvert u\rvert = k-1$ and $\lvert v\rvert = 2n-k$, whose difference $2(n-k)+1$ is odd and positive.
>
> **Check:** $S\rightsquigarrow XSX\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{0}SX\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{01}OX\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{01}XX\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{010}X\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{0100}$, and `1000` is $\mathtt{1}O$ with $O\rightsquigarrow^*\mathtt{000}$. `0010` is not generated: its only 1 is at position 3 of 4.
>
> **Watch out:**
> - $S\to XSX\mid\mathtt{1}X$ looks natural, but it only finds a 1 at the *last* position of the first half: it misses `1000`, `1010` and every $\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}^{2k-1}$ with $k\ge2$ — infinitely many strings.
> - $S\to XSX\mid\mathtt{1}Z$, with $Z$ generating all strings, forgets the parity: it generates `1` and `100`.
>
> **Answer.** (a) $F = \mathtt{0}^*$: for $\mathtt{0}^i$ and $\mathtt{0}^j$ with $i<j$, the suffix $\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}^{i+1}$ gives $\mathtt{0}^i\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}^{i+1}\in L$ and $\mathtt{0}^j\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}^{i+1}\notin L$. (b) $S\to XSX\mid\mathtt{1}O$, $O\to X\mid XXO$, $X\to\mathtt{0}\mid\mathtt{1}$.

### 5.10 Prefixes of balanced strings, a proof

> [!hard] Hard · grammar proof
> Let $P$ be the set of strings $w\in\{\mathtt{0},\mathtt{1}\}^*$ in which **every prefix** (including $\varepsilon$ and $w$ itself) has at least as many 0s as 1s. Prove that the grammar
> $$
> S\to\mathtt{0}S\mathtt{1}S\mid\mathtt{0}S\mid\varepsilon
> $$
> generates exactly $P$: prove $L(S)\subseteq P$ and $P\subseteq L(S)$, each by induction, in full.
>
> *Source: textbook exercise style.*

> [!hint]- Hint
> Write $\textsf{bal}(x) := \#(\mathtt{0},x)-\#(\mathtt{1},x)$; then $\textsf{bal}(xy) = \textsf{bal}(x)+\textsf{bal}(y)$, and the prefixes of $xy$ are the prefixes of $x$ and the strings $xq$ for prefixes $q$ of $y$. For $P\subseteq L(S)$, take a non-empty $w\in P$ and look for its *shortest* non-empty prefix with balance 0. If there is one, it has the form $\mathtt{0}x\mathtt{1}$.

> [!solution]- Solution
> **Idea.** Cut $w$ where its first 0 is "closed": after the shortest non-empty prefix with equally many 0s and 1s. If there is such a prefix, $w = \mathtt{0}x\mathtt{1}y$ (rule $S\to\mathtt{0}S\mathtt{1}S$); if not, the first 0 is never closed and $w = \mathtt{0}x$ (rule $S\to\mathtt{0}S$).
>
> **Notation.** $\textsf{bal}(x) := \#(\mathtt{0},x)-\#(\mathtt{1},x)$, so $\textsf{bal}(xy) = \textsf{bal}(x)+\textsf{bal}(y)$, and $P = \{w \mid \textsf{bal}(p)\ge0 \text{ for every prefix } p \text{ of } w\}$. The prefixes of $xy$ are the prefixes of $x$ and the strings $xq$ for prefixes $q$ of $y$.
>
> **Claim 1: $L(S)\subseteq P$.** Let $w$ be an arbitrary string in $L(S)$, fix a derivation $S\rightsquigarrow^* w$, and assume that every string in $L(S)$ shorter than $w$ is in $P$. Every rule other than $S\to\varepsilon$ puts terminals next to its copies of $S$, so the pieces below are strictly shorter than $w$; unlike [[1-models-of-computation/05-context-free-languages#careful-with-those-epsilons|Lemma 5.3]], no shortest-derivation trick is needed. There are three cases, by the first rule of the derivation.
> - **$S\to\varepsilon$.** Then $w = \varepsilon$, whose only prefix has balance 0.
> - **$S\to\mathtt{0}S$.** Then $w = \mathtt{0}x$ with $S\rightsquigarrow^* x$ and $\lvert x\rvert<\lvert w\rvert$, so $x\in P$ by the induction hypothesis. A prefix of $w$ is $\varepsilon$, or $\mathtt{0}q$ for a prefix $q$ of $x$, and $\textsf{bal}(\mathtt{0}q) = 1+\textsf{bal}(q)\ge1$.
> - **$S\to\mathtt{0}S\mathtt{1}S$.** Then $w = \mathtt{0}x\mathtt{1}y$ with $S\rightsquigarrow^* x$ and $S\rightsquigarrow^* y$, both shorter than $w$, so $x, y\in P$ by the induction hypothesis. A prefix of $w$ is $\varepsilon$; or $\mathtt{0}q$ for a prefix $q$ of $x$, with balance $1+\textsf{bal}(q)\ge1$; or $\mathtt{0}x\mathtt{1}q$ for a prefix $q$ of $y$, with balance $1+\textsf{bal}(x)-1+\textsf{bal}(q) = \textsf{bal}(x)+\textsf{bal}(q)\ge0$, because $x$ is a prefix of itself and $x, y\in P$.
>
> In every case every prefix of $w$ has non-negative balance, so $w\in P$.
>
> **Claim 2: $P\subseteq L(S)$.** Let $w$ be an arbitrary string in $P$, and assume that every string in $P$ shorter than $w$ is in $L(S)$. If $w = \varepsilon$, then $S\rightsquigarrow\varepsilon$. Otherwise the first symbol of $w$ is 0, because the one-symbol prefix has balance at least 0. There are two cases.
> - **Some non-empty prefix of $w$ has balance 0.** Let $p$ be the shortest one; $\lvert p\rvert\ge2$, because $\textsf{bal}(\mathtt{0}) = 1$. Deleting the last symbol of $p$ leaves a non-empty prefix of $w$ shorter than $p$, whose balance is $\ge0$ (as $w\in P$) and $\ne0$ (by the choice of $p$), hence $\ge1$; one more symbol brings it to 0, so it was exactly 1 and the last symbol of $p$ is 1. So $p = \mathtt{0}x\mathtt{1}$ and $w = \mathtt{0}x\mathtt{1}y$ for some strings $x$ and $y$. Then $x\in P$: for a prefix $q$ of $x$, $\mathtt{0}q$ is a non-empty prefix of $w$ shorter than $p$, so $\textsf{bal}(\mathtt{0}q)\ge1$ and $\textsf{bal}(q)\ge0$. And $y\in P$: for a prefix $q$ of $y$, $pq$ is a prefix of $w$, so $0\le\textsf{bal}(pq) = \textsf{bal}(p)+\textsf{bal}(q) = \textsf{bal}(q)$. Both $x$ and $y$ are shorter than $w$, so the induction hypothesis gives $S\rightsquigarrow^* x$ and $S\rightsquigarrow^* y$, and therefore $S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{0}S\mathtt{1}S\rightsquigarrow^*\mathtt{0}x\mathtt{1}y = w$.
> - **No non-empty prefix of $w$ has balance 0.** Write $w = \mathtt{0}x$. For a prefix $q$ of $x$, $\mathtt{0}q$ is a non-empty prefix of $w$, so $\textsf{bal}(\mathtt{0}q)\ge1$ and $\textsf{bal}(q)\ge0$; hence $x\in P$. Since $x$ is shorter than $w$, the induction hypothesis gives $S\rightsquigarrow^* x$, and $S\rightsquigarrow\mathtt{0}S\rightsquigarrow^*\mathtt{0}x = w$.
>
> In every case $w\in L(S)$. Together the two claims give $L(S) = P$. $\blacksquare$
>
> **Check:** $\varepsilon$, `0`, `01`, `001`, `0011` and `010` are in $P$ and are generated (`010` $= \mathtt{0}\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\mathtt{1}\cdot\mathtt{0}$ by the first case); `1`, `10` and `011` are not in $P$, and are not generated.
>
> **Watch out:** the cut must be after the **shortest** balanced prefix. After the longest one, `0101` would split as $\mathtt{0}\cdot\mathtt{10}\cdot\mathtt{1}$, and $\mathtt{10}\notin P$.
>
> **Grading notes.** Each inclusion is an induction and is graded like one: an arbitrary string, an explicit strong induction hypothesis, exhaustive cases (one per rule for $L(S)\subseteq P$, the two cases above for $P\subseteq L(S)$), the base case, and every use of the hypothesis on a string that is visibly shorter ([[0-toolkit/06-how-exams-are-graded|how exams are graded]]).
>
> **Answer.** Strong induction on $\lvert w\rvert$ in both directions. $L(S)\subseteq P$: one case per rule, checking every prefix. $P\subseteq L(S)$: cut a non-empty $w\in P$ after its shortest non-empty balanced prefix $\mathtt{0}x\mathtt{1}$, giving $w = \mathtt{0}x\mathtt{1}y$ with $x, y\in P$; if there is none, $w = \mathtt{0}x$ with $x\in P$.
