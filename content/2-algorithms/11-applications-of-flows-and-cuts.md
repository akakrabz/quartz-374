> [!abstract] In one breath
> Maximum flows earn their keep through **flow reductions**: build a flow network whose vertices, edges and capacities are explicit functions of the input, prove that solutions of size $k$ correspond to integral flows of value $k$ in **both directions** — the **integrality theorem** supplies an integral maximum flow — and call a maximum-flow algorithm as a black box, Ford–Fulkerson in $O(E\lvert f^*\rvert)$ time when the flow value is small and Orlin's $O(VE)$ otherwise. Unit capacities turn flows into **edge-disjoint paths**; splitting every vertex into an in-copy and an out-copy adds **vertex capacities** and gives **vertex-disjoint paths**; a source feeding one side of a bipartite graph whose other side drains into a target turns flows into **bipartite matchings**, which Ford–Fulkerson finds in $O(VE)$ time by augmenting along alternating paths. Longer chains of resources give **tuple selection** (exam scheduling), and a dag whose vertices are split into a "flat" and a "sharp" copy turns a **minimum disjoint-path cover** into a maximum matching (minimal faculty hiring). The last two problems use the cut side of the maxflow-mincut theorem: in **baseball elimination** a team can still finish first if and only if some flow saturates every game, and a minimum cut names the teams that eliminate it; in **project selection** the source side of a minimum cut, in a network whose infinite edges enforce the prerequisites, is the most profitable set of projects.

Everything here rests on four facts from [[2-algorithms/10-maximum-flows-and-minimum-cuts|Chapter 10]], used as black boxes (see also its [[2-algorithms/10-maximum-flows-and-minimum-cuts#maximum-flow-as-a-black-box|summary of maximum flow as a black box]]):

> [!key] The black boxes from Chapter 10
> - **Maxflow-mincut theorem** ([[2-algorithms/10-maximum-flows-and-minimum-cuts#103-the-maxflow-mincut-theorem|§10.3]]): the maximum flow value equals the minimum cut capacity; after a maximum flow $f^*$ is found, the vertices reachable from $s$ in the residual graph form the source side of a minimum cut, found in $O(E)$ more time.
> - **Integrality theorem** ([[2-algorithms/10-maximum-flows-and-minimum-cuts#104-ford-and-fulkersons-augmenting-path-algorithm|§10.4]]): if every capacity is an integer (or $\infty$), some maximum flow is integral, and Ford–Fulkerson computes one.
> - **Flow decomposition** ([[2-algorithms/10-maximum-flows-and-minimum-cuts#105-combining-and-decomposing-flows|§10.5]]): every flow $f \ge 0$ with $\lvert f\rvert \ge 0$ is a positive combination of at most $E$ path flows and cycle flows, found in $O(VE)$ time; for an integral flow the weights are integers, so an integral flow of value $k$ is the sum of $k$ unit flows along $s$–$t$ paths plus some cycles — and no cycles at all if the network is a dag.
> - **Running times** ([[2-algorithms/10-maximum-flows-and-minimum-cuts#107-further-progress|§10.7]]): Ford–Fulkerson takes $O(E\lvert f^*\rvert)$ time with integer capacities; Orlin's algorithm takes $O(VE)$ time, always. The book's sentence to remember: *maximum flows can be computed in $O(VE)$ time.*

> [!template] Template — a flow reduction (the four parts)
> 1. **The network:** vertices, **directed** edges with capacities, source and target, all as explicit functions of the input.
> 2. **The claim:** "the input has a solution of size $k$ if and only if the network has an integral $(s,t)$-flow of value $k$", proved both ways — solution ⇒ flow (push one unit along the path of each piece of the solution, check capacities and conservation), and integral flow ⇒ solution (decompose into unit paths and read each path back as a piece of the solution). The integrality theorem then makes the largest solution size equal to $\lvert f^*\rvert$.
> 3. **The algorithm:** a maximum-flow algorithm as a black box — Ford–Fulkerson, $O(E\lvert f^*\rvert)$, when $\lvert f^*\rvert$ is small (unit capacities); otherwise Orlin, $O(VE)$ — plus flow decomposition or a residual search if the solution itself is wanted.
> 4. **The time:** in the input's parameters, through $V$ and $E$ of the network.

## 11.1 Edge-disjoint paths

> [!definition] Definition — edge-disjoint paths
> A set of paths is **edge-disjoint** if no edge lies on two of them; they may share vertices. The problem: given a directed graph $G = (V,E)$ and two vertices $s\ne t$, find as many edge-disjoint paths from $s$ to $t$ as possible.

> [!example] Flow reduction — edge-disjoint paths in a directed graph
> **The network.** $G$ itself, with source $s$, target $t$ and capacity $c(e) = 1$ on every edge.
>
> **The claim.** $G$ has $k$ edge-disjoint paths from $s$ to $t$ if and only if the network has an integral $(s,t)$-flow of value $k$.
>
> *Paths ⇒ flow.* Push one unit along each path: $f(e)$ is the number of paths through $e$, which is 0 or 1 because the paths are edge-disjoint. Each path enters and leaves every vertex other than $s$ and $t$ equally often, so conservation holds, and each path leaves $s$ once and never returns, so $\lvert f\rvert = k$.
>
> *Flow ⇒ paths.* Each edge carries 0 or 1 unit. In the subgraph of saturated edges every vertex other than $s$ and $t$ has as many edges in as out, and $s$ has $k$ more out than in. Walk from $s$ along saturated edges, never reusing one: the walk can get stuck only at $t$. Delete its edges, shortcut the cycles it made to get a path, and repeat; this produces $k$ paths, edge-disjoint because each edge carries one unit and is deleted once. (This is the flow decomposition theorem for 0/1 flows; the leftover saturated edges form cycles, which are discarded.)
>
> **The algorithm.** Ford–Fulkerson. The cut $(\{s\}, V\setminus\{s\})$ is crossed by the at most $V-1$ edges leaving $s$, so $\lvert f^*\rvert\le V-1$ and Ford–Fulkerson runs in $O(E\lvert f^*\rvert) = O(VE)$ time; Orlin's algorithm would be overkill. The walks take $O(E)$ more time, since every step uses up an edge.
>
> **The time.** $O(VE)$.

**Undirected graphs.** Replace every undirected edge $uv$ by two directed edges $u\to v$ and $v\to u$, each of capacity 1, and compute a maximum flow $f^*$ as above. If $f^*$ saturates both $u\to v$ and $v\to u$, remove one unit from both: conservation at $u$ and $v$ and the flow value do not change. (More generally, canceling all flow cycles gives an acyclic maximum flow.) Afterwards every edge is used in at most one direction, and walking along saturated edges gives $\lvert f^*\rvert$ paths that share no undirected edge. Conversely, $k$ edge-disjoint undirected paths, each oriented from $s$ to $t$, use every directed edge at most once and form a flow of value $k$. Same time, $O(VE)$.

> [!key] Corollary — Menger's theorem, edge version (not stated in the book's chapter)
> The maximum number of edge-disjoint paths from $s$ to $t$ equals the minimum number of edges whose deletion leaves no path from $s$ to $t$.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> In the unit-capacity network the capacity of a cut $(S,T)$ is the number of edges from $S$ to $T$, and deleting them leaves no path from $s\in S$ to $t\in T$. Conversely, if deleting a set $F$ of edges leaves no path, let $S$ be the set of vertices still reachable from $s$: every edge from $S$ to $T = V\setminus S$ is in $F$, so $\lVert S,T\rVert\le\lvert F\rvert$. So the minimum cut capacity is the minimum size of such an $F$, and the maxflow-mincut theorem with the claim above finishes the proof. $\square$

> [!trap] Edge-disjoint path traps
> - **Edge-disjoint is not vertex-disjoint:** edge-disjoint paths may share vertices (the figure in §11.2 has 2 edge-disjoint paths but only 1 vertex-disjoint one).
> - **The saturated edges may contain cycles.** Read off paths by walking from $s$; a cycle is not a path from $s$ to $t$.
> - **Undirected graphs:** cancel an edge used in both directions before reading off paths, or the "paths" use one undirected edge twice.
> - **Ford–Fulkerson is polynomial here only because $\lvert f^*\rvert\le V-1$.** With large capacities, $O(E\lvert f^*\rvert)$ is not a polynomial bound; cite Orlin's $O(VE)$ instead.

## 11.2 Vertex capacities and vertex-disjoint paths

> [!definition] Definition — vertex capacities
> Besides the edge capacities, each vertex $v\notin\{s,t\}$ has a capacity $c(v)\ge 0$, and a feasible flow must also satisfy
> $$\sum_{u} f(u\to v)\le c(v),$$
> that is, at most $c(v)$ units pass through $v$.

> [!example] Flow reduction — vertex capacities by vertex splitting (Ford and Fulkerson, 1962)
> **The network.** $\overline{G}$: for every vertex $v\notin\{s,t\}$, two vertices $v_{\text{in}}$ and $v_{\text{out}}$ joined by an edge $v_{\text{in}}\to v_{\text{out}}$ of capacity $c(v)$; for every edge $u\to v$ of $G$, an edge $u_{\text{out}}\to v_{\text{in}}$ of the same capacity, where $s_{\text{out}} = s$ and $t_{\text{in}} = t$ (and likewise $s_{\text{in}} = s$, $t_{\text{out}} = t$ for edges into $s$ or out of $t$). The source is $s$ and the target is $t$.
>
> **The claim.** Feasible flows $f$ in $G$ (respecting the vertex capacities) and feasible flows $\bar f$ in $\overline{G}$ correspond one to one, with the same value, by
> $$\bar f(u_{\text{out}}\to v_{\text{in}}) = f(u\to v),\qquad \bar f(v_{\text{in}}\to v_{\text{out}}) = \sum_u f(u\to v).$$
>
> *$G$ ⇒ $\overline{G}$.* Copied edges keep their flow and capacity; the split edge of $v$ carries the flow through $v$, which is at most $c(v)$. At $v_{\text{in}}$ the flow in is $\sum_u f(u\to v)$, which is the flow out; at $v_{\text{out}}$ the flow in is the same number, and by conservation at $v$ in $G$ it equals $\sum_w f(v\to w)$, the flow out. The edges at $s$ are the same in both graphs, so the values agree.
>
> *$\overline{G}$ ⇒ $G$.* Set $f(u\to v) = \bar f(u_{\text{out}}\to v_{\text{in}})$. The flow into $v$ is the flow into $v_{\text{in}}$, which equals $\bar f(v_{\text{in}}\to v_{\text{out}})\le c(v)$, which equals the flow out of $v_{\text{out}}$, the flow out of $v$: conservation and the vertex capacity both hold.
>
> So maximum flows correspond, integral ones to integral ones.
>
> **The algorithm.** Any maximum-flow algorithm on $\overline{G}$: Orlin's, $O(VE)$.
>
> **The time.** $\overline{G}$ has at most $2V$ vertices and $E + V$ edges and is built in $O(V+E)$ time; the total is $O(VE)$.

A set of $s$–$t$ paths is **vertex-disjoint** (more precisely, *internally* vertex-disjoint) if no two of them share a vertex other than $s$ and $t$.

> [!example] Flow reduction — vertex-disjoint paths
> **The network.** $\overline{G}$ with $c(v) = 1$ for every vertex $v\notin\{s,t\}$ and capacity 1 on every copied edge.
>
> **The claim.** $G$ has $k$ vertex-disjoint paths from $s$ to $t$ if and only if $\overline{G}$ has an integral flow of value $k$, that is (§11.1), $k$ edge-disjoint paths from $s$ to $t$.
>
> *Paths ⇒ flow.* Send a path $s\to v_1\to\cdots\to v_j\to t$ along $s\to v_{1,\text{in}}\to v_{1,\text{out}}\to\cdots\to v_{j,\text{out}}\to t$. Two of the paths share no inner vertex, hence no split edge and no copied edge (only the one-edge path $s\,t$ can use an edge $s\to t$). So every edge of $\overline{G}$ carries at most one unit.
>
> *Flow ⇒ paths.* Take $k$ edge-disjoint paths in $\overline{G}$. A path that enters $v_{\text{in}}$ must leave by $v_{\text{in}}\to v_{\text{out}}$, so contracting each pair turns it into a path of $G$; two of them through the same $v$ would share the edge $v_{\text{in}}\to v_{\text{out}}$.
>
> **The algorithm.** Ford–Fulkerson: at most $V-1$ edges leave $s$, so $\lvert f^*\rvert\le V-1$.
>
> **The time.** $O(VE)$.

> [!trap] Vertex-splitting traps
> - **Edges need capacity 1 too.** With unbounded edge capacities, an edge $s\to t$ would carry unbounded flow. (The book says "assign capacity 1 to every vertex"; the edges keep the unit capacities of §11.1.)
> - **Do not cap $s$ and $t$.** If you split them as well (the book does), take $s_{\text{out}}$ and $t_{\text{in}}$ as source and target, as the book does: then their split edges never cross a cut forward, and their capacities do not matter. With $s_{\text{in}}$ and $t_{\text{out}}$ instead, those split edges need capacity $\infty$.
> - **Direction:** $u\to v$ becomes $u_{\text{out}}\to v_{\text{in}}$. An edge into $v_{\text{out}}$ or out of $v_{\text{in}}$ lets flow bypass $c(v)$.

## 11.3 Bipartite matching

> [!definition] Definition — matching, bipartite graph
> A **matching** in an undirected graph is a set of edges no two of which share a vertex; a **maximum matching** has as many edges as possible. A graph is **bipartite** if its vertices split into two sets $L$ and $R$ such that every edge joins $L$ to $R$. Example: doctors ($L$) and hospitals ($R$), with an edge whenever both find the other acceptable; a maximum matching is a largest set of hires. Unlike [[2-algorithms/04-greedy-algorithms#45-stable-matching|stable matching]] (§4.5), nobody's preferences matter beyond acceptability.

> [!example] Flow reduction — maximum bipartite matching
> **The network.** $G'$: the vertices $L\cup R$ plus a source $s$ and a target $t$; an edge $s\to\ell$ for every $\ell\in L$, an edge $\ell\to r$ for every edge $\ell r$ of $G$ (oriented from $L$ to $R$), an edge $r\to t$ for every $r\in R$; every capacity 1.
>
> **The claim.** $G$ has a matching of size $k$ if and only if $G'$ has an integral $(s,t)$-flow of value $k$.
>
> *Matching ⇒ flow.* For each edge $\ell r$ of the matching $M$ push one unit along $s\to\ell\to r\to t$. These paths share only $s$ and $t$, so no edge carries more than 1; the value is $\lvert M\rvert = k$.
>
> *Flow ⇒ matching.* Every edge carries 0 or 1. Let $M = \{\ell r : f(\ell\to r) = 1\}$. A vertex $\ell\in L$ receives at most one unit, through its only incoming edge $s\to\ell$, so at most one edge out of $\ell$ carries flow; symmetrically each $r\in R$ has only the outgoing edge $r\to t$. So $M$ is a matching. The cut $(\{s\}\cup L,\ R\cup\{t\})$ is crossed forward exactly by the edges $\ell\to r$ and backward by none, so $\lvert f\rvert = \sum f(\ell\to r) = \lvert M\rvert$.
>
> **The algorithm.** Ford–Fulkerson: $\lvert f^*\rvert\le\min(\lvert L\rvert,\lvert R\rvert)\le V/2$. Reading off $M$ takes $O(E)$ time.
>
> **The time.** $O(VE)$ (after discarding isolated vertices, which cannot be matched, $G'$ has $O(E)$ edges); Orlin's algorithm gives the same bound.

It is worth translating Ford–Fulkerson back into $G$. The algorithm maintains a matching $M$, initially empty: the edges of $G$ whose copies carry flow. A vertex is **matched** if it is an endpoint of an edge of $M$, and **unmatched** otherwise.

> [!definition] Definition — alternating path
> An **alternating path** for $M$ (often called an *augmenting* path) is a path from an unmatched vertex of $L$ to an unmatched vertex of $R$ whose edges are alternately not in $M$ and in $M$. It has one more edge outside $M$ than inside, so the symmetric difference $M\oplus P$ is a matching with one more edge.

The residual graph of $G'$ consists of $s\to\ell$ for unmatched $\ell$, $\ell\to r$ for edges not in $M$, $r\to\ell$ for edges in $M$, and $r\to t$ for unmatched $r$ (plus reversed edges into $s$ and out of $t$, which no $s$–$t$ path uses). So augmenting paths of the flow are exactly alternating paths of the matching, and Ford–Fulkerson becomes:

```text
AlternatingPathMatching(G = (L ∪ R, E)):
    M ← ∅
    while G has an alternating path P for M
        M ← M ⊕ P
    return M
```

(Our pseudocode; the book describes the algorithm in words.)

> [!key] Theorem (Berge, 1957)
> A matching in a bipartite graph is maximum if and only if it has no alternating path.

The "only if" is the remark above ($M\oplus P$ is larger). The "if" follows from the maxflow-mincut theorem: no alternating path means no augmenting path in the residual graph, so the flow, and with it $M$, is maximum. Berge proved it independently of flows; the idea is implicit in algorithms of Kuhn (1955), Kőnig (1916) and Jacobi (around 1836). Each alternating path is found by whatever-first search in $O(E)$ time and each iteration adds an edge, so there are at most $V/2$ iterations: **$O(VE)$ time**. Hopcroft and Karp (1973) find many disjoint alternating paths per iteration and run in $O(\sqrt{V}\,E)$ time.

> [!trap] Matching traps
> - **Maximal is not maximum.** In the figure, $\{ax, cy\}$ cannot be extended by any edge, yet the maximum matching has 3 edges. Greedily adding edges is not an algorithm for maximum matching.
> - **Only bipartite graphs.** The reduction needs the bipartition to orient every edge from $L$ to $R$; maximum matching in general graphs needs a different algorithm (Edmonds' blossom algorithm, beyond this book).
> - **The capacities on $s\to\ell$ and $r\to t$ are the matching constraint.** Without them a vertex could be matched twice.
> - **Not stable matching:** maximum matching ignores preferences; stable matching ignores the size question (with complete preference lists every stable matching is perfect).

Concept page: [[concepts/bipartite-matching]].

## 11.4 Tuple selection

> [!definition] Definition — tuple selection (the book's name)
> The input is a list of finite sets $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_d$ (resources) and capacities, each a non-negative integer or $\infty$: a capacity $c(x)$ for every element $x$, and a capacity $c(x,y)$ for every pair $x\in X_i$, $y\in X_{i+1}$ of elements of **adjacent** sets. The task: select as many $d$-tuples $(x_1,\ldots,x_d)\in X_1\times\cdots\times X_d$ as possible such that every element $x$ appears in at most $c(x)$ selected tuples and every adjacent pair $(x,y)$ in at most $c(x,y)$. Bipartite matching is the case $d = 2$, $c(x) = 1$, and $c(x,y)\in\{0,1\}$ according to whether $xy$ is an edge.

> [!example] Flow reduction — tuple selection
> **The network.** A source $s$, a target $t$, and a vertex for every element of every $X_i$, with vertex capacity $c(x)$ (implemented by vertex splitting, §11.2); an edge $s\to w$ of capacity $\infty$ for every $w\in X_1$, an edge $z\to t$ of capacity $\infty$ for every $z\in X_d$, and an edge $x\to y$ of capacity $c(x,y)$ for every $x\in X_i$, $y\in X_{i+1}$, $1\le i<d$ (edges of capacity 0 may be omitted).
>
> **The claim.** There is a selection of $k$ tuples satisfying all the constraints if and only if the network has an integral flow of value $k$.
>
> *Tuples ⇒ flow.* Each tuple $(x_1,\ldots,x_d)$ is a path $s\to x_1\to\cdots\to x_d\to t$; add one unit of flow along each. The flow through $x$ is the number of selected tuples containing $x$, at most $c(x)$; the flow on $x\to y$ is the number containing the pair, at most $c(x,y)$. The value is $k$.
>
> *Flow ⇒ tuples.* Every edge goes from one layer to the next, so the network is a dag and the decomposition of an integral flow of value $k$ has no cycles: it is $k$ unit flows along $s$–$t$ paths. Each path passes through exactly one element of each $X_i$ — a tuple — and, as before, the flow through a vertex or along an edge counts the tuples that use it, so the constraints hold.
>
> **The algorithm.** A maximum flow (Orlin, $O(VE)$, or Ford–Fulkerson when $\lvert f^*\rvert$ is small), then a flow decomposition, $O(VE)$, to list the tuples.
>
> **The time.** $O(VE)$, where the network has $V = O(\sum_i\lvert X_i\rvert)$ vertices and $E = O(\sum_i\lvert X_i\rvert\,\lvert X_{i+1}\rvert + \sum_i\lvert X_i\rvert)$ edges.

Two remarks. First, the capacities count tuples **with multiplicity**: if every capacity along a tuple is at least 2, the flow may select that tuple twice. When the tuples must be distinct, make sure some capacity forces it (in exam scheduling below, each class has capacity 1). Second, the restriction to adjacent pairs is essential: constraining even one pair of non-adjacent sets $X_i$, $X_j$ with $j > i+1$ makes the problem NP-hard, by a reduction from 3-dimensional matching ([[2-algorithms/12-np-hardness|Chapter 12]]).

### Exam scheduling

The book's illustration. There are $n$ classes, $r$ rooms, $t$ time slots and $p$ proctors. Each class needs one room, one time slot and one proctor for its final exam; a room holds at most one exam per time slot; class $i$ fits in room $j$ if and only if $E[i]\le S[j]$, where $E[i]$ is its enrollment and $S[j]$ the room's number of seats; proctor $\ell$ is available in slot $k$ if and only if $A[k,\ell] = \textsf{True}$, oversees at most one exam at a time and at most 5 exams in all. The input size is $N = n + r + tp$. Schedule every exam, or report correctly that it is impossible.

> [!example] Flow reduction — exam scheduling (tuple selection with four resources)
> **The network.** Vertices $s'$, $c_1,\ldots,c_n$ (classes), $r_1,\ldots,r_r$ (rooms), $t_1,\ldots,t_t$ (time slots), $p_1,\ldots,p_p$ (proctors) and $t'$ (the source and target get primes because $t$ is taken). The edges, all directed left to right:
>
> | edge | present when | capacity | rule it enforces |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | $s'\to c_i$ | every class $i$ | 1 | each class has one exam |
> | $c_i\to r_j$ | $E[i]\le S[j]$ | $\infty$ | the room is large enough |
> | $r_j\to t_k$ | every room $j$ and slot $k$ | 1 | one exam per room per slot |
> | $t_k\to p_\ell$ | $A[k,\ell] = \textsf{True}$ | 1 | proctor available, one exam at a time |
> | $p_\ell\to t'$ | every proctor $\ell$ | 5 | at most 5 exams per proctor |
>
> **The claim.** $k$ classes can be scheduled (room, slot and proctor for each, all rules respected) if and only if the network has an integral flow of value $k$. *Schedule ⇒ flow:* the exam of class $i$ in room $j$ at slot $k$ with proctor $\ell$ is the path $s'\,c_i\,r_j\,t_k\,p_\ell\,t'$, and each capacity counts exactly what its rule limits. *Flow ⇒ schedule:* the network is a dag, so an integral flow of value $k$ decomposes into $k$ unit paths; each one names a class, a room that fits it, a slot and a proctor available then, and the capacities say that no class is scheduled twice, no room–slot pair or proctor–slot pair is used twice, and no proctor has more than 5 exams. In particular all $n$ exams can be scheduled if and only if $\lvert f^*\rvert = n$.
>
> **The algorithm.** Ford–Fulkerson ($\lvert f^*\rvert\le n$), then a flow decomposition into paths, each transcribed as (class, room, slot, proctor). If $\lvert f^*\rvert < n$, report that no schedule exists.
>
> **The time.** $V = n + r + t + p + 2 = O(N)$ and $E = O(nr + rt + tp) = O(N^2)$; building the network by brute force takes $O(E)$, the maximum flow and the decomposition $O(VE)$: **$O(N^3)$** in all.

> [!trap] Tuple-selection traps
> - **Only adjacent resources may be constrained together.** A rule like "class $i$ may not use proctor $\ell$" links classes and proctors, which are not adjacent: it cannot be written as a capacity in this network. (Reorder the resources if the constrained pairs allow it.)
> - **Capacities count tuples with multiplicity** (see above).
> - **Index carefully:** $A$ is indexed $[\text{slot}, \text{proctor}]$, so the edge $t_k\to p_\ell$ exists when $A[k,\ell]$ is true (the book writes $A[\ell,k]$ at that point).

## 11.5 Disjoint-path covers

> [!definition] Definition — path cover, disjoint-path cover
> A **path cover** of a directed graph $G$ is a set of directed paths such that every vertex lies on at least one of them; a **disjoint-path cover** is one in which every vertex lies on exactly one (paths of length 0 allowed). The problem: find a disjoint-path cover with as few paths as possible. In general graphs it is NP-hard — a graph has a disjoint-path cover with one path if and only if it has a Hamiltonian path ([[2-algorithms/12-np-hardness#1211-hamiltonian-cycle|§12.11]]) — but in **dags** it reduces to bipartite matching.

> [!example] Reduction — minimum disjoint-path cover of a dag (to bipartite matching)
> **The graph.** The undirected bipartite graph $G' = (V', E')$ with two vertices $v^\flat$ and $v^\sharp$ ("flat" and "sharp") for every vertex $v$ of $G$, and an edge $u^\flat v^\sharp$ for every edge $u\to v$ of $G$. (If $G$ is stored as an adjacency matrix, $G'$ is the bipartite graph with the same matrix.)
>
> **The claim.** The dag $G$ has a disjoint-path cover with $k$ paths if and only if $G'$ has a matching with $V-k$ edges. Hence the minimum number of paths is $V$ minus the size of a maximum matching in $G'$.
>
> *Cover ⇒ matching.* A path with $m$ vertices has $m-1$ edges, so a cover by $k$ paths has $V-k$ edges. In a disjoint-path cover each vertex has at most one outgoing and one incoming cover edge, so $M = \{u^\flat v^\sharp : u\to v \text{ in the cover}\}$ uses each $u^\flat$ and each $v^\sharp$ at most once: a matching with $V-k$ edges.
>
> *Matching ⇒ cover.* Let $M' = \{u\to v : u^\flat v^\sharp\in M\}$. In $(V, M')$ every vertex has out-degree at most 1 (it is matched at most once as $u^\flat$) and in-degree at most 1, so $(V, M')$ is a disjoint union of directed paths and directed cycles — and $G$ has no cycles. So it is a disjoint-path cover; every path has one more vertex than edges, so it has $V-\lvert M\rvert = k$ paths.
>
> **The algorithm.** A maximum matching in $G'$ by Ford–Fulkerson (§11.3).
>
> **The time.** $G'$ has $2V$ vertices and $E$ edges: $O(VE)$.

In other words it is "really" a matching problem: match as many vertices as possible to distinct successors; the paths are then the chains of matched successors, and the number of paths is the number of vertices without a matched successor — the last vertex of each path. The dag matters only in "matching ⇒ cover": in a graph with cycles, $M'$ may contain cycles (a matching in $G'$ is then a cover by disjoint paths **and cycles** — the idea behind the book's Exercise 7 on cycle covers).

### Minimal faculty hiring

The book's application. A university offers $n$ classes in $m$ locations: class $i$ has a start time $C[i].\mathit{start}$, an end time $C[i].\mathit{end}$ and a location $C[i].\mathit{loc}$, and $T[u,v]$ is the time needed to walk from location $u$ to location $v$. Every professor can teach every class; a professor who teaches class $i$ and next class $j$ needs
$$C[j].\mathit{start}\ \ge\ C[i].\mathit{end} + T\big[C[i].\mathit{loc},\,C[j].\mathit{loc}\big].$$
How few professors can teach all the classes?

> [!example] Reduction — minimal faculty hiring (to a disjoint-path cover)
> **The graph.** The directed graph $G$ with one vertex per class and an edge $i\to j$ whenever $C[j].\mathit{start}\ge C[i].\mathit{end} + T[C[i].\mathit{loc}, C[j].\mathit{loc}]$ ("the same professor can teach $i$ and then $j$"). Classes have positive length and walking times are non-negative, so every edge goes to a class that starts later: $G$ is a **dag**.
>
> **The problem on the graph.** A professor's day is a path in $G$, and an assignment of all classes is a disjoint-path cover; so the answer is the size of a **minimum disjoint-path cover** of $G$, that is, $n$ minus the size of a maximum matching in $G'$.
>
> **The algorithm.** The path-cover reduction above, with Ford–Fulkerson for the matching.
>
> **The time.** Building $G$ by brute force takes $O(n^2)$ time; it has $V = n$ and $E = O(n^2)$, so the matching takes $O(VE) = O(n^3)$: **$O(n^3)$** in all.

The book states the walking condition for *every* two classes of the same professor, not only consecutive ones. The two readings agree when walking times obey the triangle inequality $T[u,w]\le T[u,v] + T[v,w]$, as true walking times do: then each path of $G$ also satisfies the condition for non-consecutive classes. If all walking times are equal, a greedy algorithm solves the problem in $O(n\log n)$ time (the book's footnote).

> [!trap] Path-cover traps
> - **Not for general graphs:** the "matching ⇒ cover" step needs acyclicity. With cycles the problem is NP-hard.
> - **Longest path first is not optimal.** In the figure, removing the longest path $a\,c\,d\,f$ leaves $b$ and $e$ isolated: 3 paths instead of 2.
> - **$V - \lvert M\rvert$, not $\lvert M\rvert$:** the matching counts the edges of the cover, not its paths.

## 11.6 Baseball elimination

The American League East on August 30, 1996 (the book's table; "left" counts all remaining games, the last five columns the games left against each division rival):

| team | won–lost | left | NYY | BAL | BOS | TOR | DET |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| New York Yankees | 75–59 | 28 | | 3 | 8 | 7 | 3 |
| Baltimore Orioles | 71–63 | 28 | 3 | | 2 | 7 | 4 |
| Boston Red Sox | 69–66 | 27 | 8 | 2 | | 0 | 0 |
| Toronto Blue Jays | 63–72 | 27 | 7 | 7 | 0 | | 0 |
| Detroit Tigers | 49–86 | 27 | 3 | 4 | 0 | 0 | |

Winning all 27 remaining games, Detroit would finish with 76 wins, more than anyone has now — yet Detroit is "mathematically eliminated", because the rivals still play each other and somebody has to win those games. A flow decides it.

**The formulation.** Team $i$ has won $W[i]$ games, and $G[i,j]$ games between teams $i$ and $j$ remain; $R[n]$ is the number of games team $n$ (the favorite) still plays. Can team $n$ finish with the most wins, possibly tied (no game is tied, every game is played)? We may assume team $n$ wins all its remaining games: turning a loss of team $n$ into a win gives it one more win and its opponent one fewer. Then team $n$ finishes with $W[n] + R[n]$ wins, and it can finish first if and only if the games among the other teams can be decided so that every team $i<n$ wins at most $W[n] + R[n] - W[i]$ of them. If $W[i] > W[n] + R[n]$ for some $i$, team $n$ is already eliminated; assume not.

> [!example] Flow reduction — baseball elimination (Schwartz, 1960s; simplified by Gusfield, Martel and Fernández-Baca)
> **The network.** A source $s$, a target $t$, a **game vertex** $g_{i,j}$ for every pair $1\le i<j<n$, and a **team vertex** $t_i$ for every $i<n$; edges $s\to g_{i,j}$ of capacity $G[i,j]$, edges $g_{i,j}\to t_i$ and $g_{i,j}\to t_j$ of capacity $\infty$, and edges $t_i\to t$ of capacity $W[n]+R[n]-W[i]$. (The book names the team vertices $t_i$ too; they are not the target.)
>
> **The claim (the book's theorem).** Team $n$ can finish first if and only if the maximum flow saturates every edge out of $s$, that is, $\lvert f^*\rvert = \sum_{i<j<n} G[i,j]$.
>
> *Outcome ⇒ flow.* For every game between $i$ and $j$ won by $i$, push one unit along $s\to g_{i,j}\to t_i\to t$. Each edge $s\to g_{i,j}$ carries exactly $G[i,j]$, and $t_i\to t$ carries the number of these games team $i$ wins, at most $W[n]+R[n]-W[i]$ (it loses to team $n$).
>
> *Flow ⇒ outcome.* Take an **integral** maximum flow (integrality theorem) that saturates every edge out of $s$, and let team $i$ win $f(g_{i,j}\to t_i)$ of its games against $j$. Conservation at $g_{i,j}$ gives $f(g_{i,j}\to t_i) + f(g_{i,j}\to t_j) = G[i,j]$: every game gets one winner. Team $i$ wins $\sum_j f(g_{i,j}\to t_i) = f(t_i\to t)\le W[n]+R[n]-W[i]$ more games, so it finishes with at most $W[n]+R[n]$ wins, no more than team $n$.
>
> **The algorithm.** A maximum flow, by Orlin's algorithm.
>
> **The time.** The network has $O(n^2)$ vertices and $O(n^2)$ edges and is built in $O(n^2)$ time; the maximum flow takes $O(VE) = O(n^4)$ time.

**Detroit.** $W[n]+R[n] = 49 + 27 = 76$, so the team edges have capacities $76-75 = 1$ (New York), $76-71 = 5$ (Baltimore), $76-69 = 7$ (Boston) and $76-63 = 13$ (Toronto), in total 26. The edges out of $s$ carry the $3+8+7+2+7 = 27$ games among the four rivals. A flow can never exceed the capacity 26 of the cut formed by the four team edges, so no flow saturates the 27 games: **Detroit is eliminated.** (Detroit's 27 remaining games include 20 against teams outside the division, which Detroit is assumed to win; the rivals' games against outside teams are assumed lost, which can only help Detroit, so only the 27 games among the rivals enter the network.)

The cut is more than a bound: a minimum cut always explains an elimination.

> [!key] The certificate cut (precision added here; the book shows the example only)
> Team $n$ is eliminated if and only if some set $Q$ of other teams satisfies
> $$\sum_{i\in Q} W[i] + \sum_{\substack{i<j\\ i,j\in Q}} G[i,j]\ >\ \lvert Q\rvert\,\big(W[n]+R[n]\big).$$
> The teams of $Q$ already have the first sum of wins and will collect at least the second among themselves, so on average they finish with more than $W[n]+R[n]$, and one of them finishes ahead of team $n$. If team $n$ is eliminated, the teams $i$ whose vertices $t_i$ lie on the source side of a minimum cut form such a set $Q$.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> *If $Q$ exists:* each game between two teams of $Q$ gives a win to a team of $Q$, so at the end of the season the teams of $Q$ have at least the left-hand side in total, which exceeds $\lvert Q\rvert(W[n]+R[n])$; some team of $Q$ ends with more than $W[n]+R[n]$, the most team $n$ can reach.
>
> *If team $n$ is eliminated:* the maximum flow is smaller than the total $\sum G[i,j]$, so some cut $(S,T)$ has capacity less than that total. Let $Q = \{i : t_i\in S\}$. If a game vertex $g_{i,j}$ is in $S$, both $t_i$ and $t_j$ are in $S$ (otherwise an infinite edge crosses the cut), so every game vertex of a pair not inside $Q$ lies in $T$ and its edge $s\to g_{i,j}$ is cut; and every edge $t_i\to t$ with $i\in Q$ is cut. Hence
> $$\sum_{i<j} G[i,j]\ >\ \lVert S,T\rVert\ \ge \sum_{\text{pairs not inside } Q} G[i,j] + \sum_{i\in Q}\big(W[n]+R[n]-W[i]\big),$$
> and subtracting the games of pairs not inside $Q$ from both sides gives the inequality for $Q$. $\square$

For Detroit, $Q$ is all four rivals: $75+71+69+63 = 278$ wins plus the 27 games among them make 305 wins, more than $4\cdot 76 = 304$; no smaller set of rivals works. The comparison "total into $t$ < total out of $s$" is the special case where $Q$ is everybody, and it can miss eliminations (the book's footnote; the following example is ours): with rivals $A$, $B$, $C$ at 8, 8 and 0 wins, five games between $A$ and $B$, one each of $A$ and $B$ against $C$, and team $n$ at 7 wins with 2 games left (against $C$), the team edges have capacities $1 + 1 + 9 = 11\ge 7$ games, yet $Q = \{A,B\}$ gives $8 + 8 + 5 = 21 > 2\cdot 9 = 18$: one of $A$ and $B$ finishes with at least 11 wins, and team $n$ is eliminated.

Kevin Wayne (2001) showed how to find **all** eliminated teams in $O(n^3)$ time, essentially with a single maximum-flow computation.

> [!trap] Baseball traps
> - **Team $n$'s own games are not in the network:** they are all wins for team $n$, counted in $R[n]$.
> - **Check $W[i]\le W[n]+R[n]$ first;** otherwise a capacity is negative and team $n$ is already eliminated.
> - **"Flow ⇒ outcome" needs an integral flow:** half a win is not an outcome. (The book's proof says "a feasible flow"; the integrality theorem provides an integral one of the same value.)
> - **Comparing total capacities is not enough** (example above): compute the maximum flow.

## 11.7 Project selection

We are given $n$ projects and a directed graph $G$ of dependencies: an edge $u\to v$ means project $u$ cannot be done unless project $v$ is done too ($u$ **needs** $v$; the book's graph is a dag, the dependency graph of [[2-algorithms/06-depth-first-search#64-memoization-and-dynamic-programming|§6.4]]). Each project $v$ has a **profit** $p(v)$ (the book writes it with a dollar sign), which may be negative — a cost. A set $X$ of projects is **valid** if it contains everything its members need: $u\in X$ and $u\to v$ imply $v\in X$. Find a valid set with the largest total profit $\textit{profit}(X) = \sum_{v\in X} p(v)$; the empty set, with profit 0, is valid.

The task is to split the projects into the ones we **S**elect and the ones we **T**urn down — a cut. Three questions remain: how to enforce the dependencies, how to maximize with a *minimum* cut, and how to handle negative profits. Infinite edges, the total positive profit, and edges to the target answer them.

> [!example] Cut reduction — project selection
> **The network.** $G'$: the dependency graph plus a source $s$ and a target $t$; an edge $s\to v$ of capacity $p(v)$ for every profitable project ($p(v)>0$), an edge $u\to t$ of capacity $-p(u)$ for every costly project ($p(u)<0$), and capacity $\infty$ on every dependency edge $u\to v$. All capacities are positive.
>
> **The claim.** Let $P = \sum_{p(v)>0} p(v)$ be the total profit available. For every valid set $X$, the cut $(\{s\}\cup X,\ \text{rest}\cup\{t\})$ has finite capacity $P - \textit{profit}(X)$; conversely, for every $(s,t)$-cut $(S,T)$ of finite capacity, $S\setminus\{s\}$ is valid and has profit $P - \lVert S,T\rVert$. So the source side of a **minimum** cut, minus $s$, is a most profitable valid set, with profit $P - \lvert f^*\rvert$.
>
> *Cut ⇒ valid set.* If a dependency edge $u\to v$ had $u\in S$ and $v\in T$, the cut would contain an infinite edge; so a finite cut has none, and $S\setminus\{s\}$ contains everything its members need.
>
> *Valid set ⇒ finite cut.* Conversely, if $X$ is valid, no dependency edge leaves $\{s\}\cup X$, so the cut is finite.
>
> *The capacity.* In a finite cut the only edges from $S$ to $T$ are $s\to v$ with $v\in T$ (profit turned down) and $u\to t$ with $u\in S$ (cost paid). With $\textit{yield}(Y) = \sum_{v\in Y,\,p(v)>0} p(v)$ and $\textit{cost}(Y) = \sum_{u\in Y,\,p(u)<0} (-p(u))$,
> $$\lVert S,T\rVert = \textit{yield}(T) + \textit{cost}(S) = P - \textit{yield}(S) + \textit{cost}(S) = P - \textit{profit}(S\setminus\{s\}).$$
> Smaller cuts are larger profits.
>
> **The algorithm.** A maximum flow $f^*$ by Orlin's algorithm; then the set $S$ of vertices reachable from $s$ in the residual graph is the source side of a minimum cut ([[2-algorithms/10-maximum-flows-and-minimum-cuts#103-the-maxflow-mincut-theorem|§10.3]]); return $S\setminus\{s\}$.
>
> **The time.** $G'$ has $n+2$ vertices and at most $E + n$ edges and is built in $O(n + E)$ time ($E$ = number of dependencies); the maximum flow takes $O(VE)$ time.

**The book's example.** Eight projects with profits $-2, -3, -5, -8$ (costly) and $4, 6, 2, 3$ (profitable); $-3$ needs $-2$, 4 needs $-2$ and $-3$, 6 needs $-3$ and 2, 2 needs $-5$, and 3 needs $-5$ and $-8$. Then $P = 4+6+2+3 = 15$, the minimum cut has capacity 13 — the costs $2+3+5$ of the selected costly projects plus the profit 3 turned down — and the best selection, $\{4, 6, 2, -2, -3, -5\}$, earns $15 - 13 = 2$. It is the only valid set with profit 2; every other valid set earns at most 0.

The reduction never uses acyclicity: with a cycle of dependencies, every finite cut takes the whole cycle or none of it, which is exactly what validity demands.

> [!trap] Project-selection traps
> - **Direction of the infinite edges:** from the project that needs to the project needed. Reversed, they would let you take a project without its prerequisites but force you to take every project that needs a selected one — the wrong constraint.
> - **No negative capacities:** a cost becomes a positive capacity on an edge **into** $t$.
> - **It is a minimum cut** (the book once says "maximum cut problem"; maximum cut is a different, NP-hard problem).
> - **The answer is the source side of the cut**, found by a residual search after the maximum flow — not the set of saturated edges, and not $\lvert f^*\rvert$ itself, which is $P$ minus the best profit.

## Summary

- A flow reduction has four parts: the network as explicit functions of the input, the claim "solution of size $k$ ⇔ integral flow of value $k$" proved in both directions, a maximum-flow black box, and the time in the input's parameters. Integrality turns the maximum flow into the largest solution; flow decomposition (no cycles in a dag) reads the solution off.
- Unit capacities: flows are edge-disjoint paths, $O(VE)$ by Ford–Fulkerson since $\lvert f^*\rvert\le V-1$; undirected edges become two directed ones, and an edge used both ways is canceled. Menger: the maximum number of edge-disjoint paths equals the minimum number of edges whose deletion separates $t$ from $s$.
- Vertex capacities by vertex splitting: $v\mapsto v_{\text{in}}\to v_{\text{out}}$ with capacity $c(v)$, $u\to v\mapsto u_{\text{out}}\to v_{\text{in}}$; with all capacities 1 it computes vertex-disjoint paths.
- Bipartite matching: $s\to L\to R\to t$, all capacities 1, $O(VE)$. Ford–Fulkerson there is the alternating-path algorithm $M\leftarrow M\oplus P$; Berge: $M$ is maximum iff it has no alternating path. Hopcroft–Karp: $O(\sqrt V E)$.
- Tuple selection: a layered network with element and adjacent-pair capacities (exam scheduling: classes → rooms → slots → proctors, $O(N^3)$); non-adjacent constraints make it NP-hard.
- The fewest disjoint paths covering a dag: $V$ minus a maximum matching in the flat/sharp bipartite graph; minimal faculty hiring is a path cover of the compatibility dag, $O(n^3)$. In general graphs the problem is NP-hard.
- Baseball elimination: team $n$ can finish first iff the flow saturates all games among the others; otherwise a minimum cut gives a set $Q$ of teams whose wins plus mutual games exceed $\lvert Q\rvert(W[n]+R[n])$. $O(n^4)$ with Orlin.
- Project selection: a valid set is the source side of a finite cut, the capacity is $P - \textit{profit}$, so a minimum cut (from the residual graph after a maximum flow) is a best selection, $O(VE)$.

> [!exam] On the exams
> Flows are **not taught in CS/ECE 374 this semester** and appear on no exam: the course calendar has no flow lectures, homework or labs, and Erickson's own page for the book says CS 374 covers everything in it "except for flows". Read this chapter for the habit it trains, which the exams do test: model the problem as a graph whose vertices and edges are explicit functions of the input, invoke a standard algorithm as a black box, and prove that the model is faithful **in both directions**. That is the [[0-toolkit/06-how-exams-are-graded#13-graph-reductions|graph-reduction rubric]] of Midterm 2 and the final, and the "only if" / "if" structure of every NP-hardness proof ([[2-algorithms/12-np-hardness#128-the-general-pattern|§12.8]]). Erickson, *Algorithms*, Chapter 11 (pp. 353–368; the exercises, pp. 368–378, are on the [[2-algorithms/11-applications-of-flows-and-cuts-exercises|exercises page]]), summarized in our own words; the black boxes are Chapter 10's. The reductions, their sizes and running times, the exam-scheduling network, the path-cover reduction, minimal faculty hiring, the baseball formulation and theorem with the 1996 American League East standings, and the project-selection network with the book's eight-project example (Figures 11.7–11.9) follow the book; every reduction is rewritten in the four-part format with both directions of the claim proved, the vertex-splitting correspondence is spelled out (the book calls it "routine definition-chasing"), and $s$ and $t$ are left unsplit (the book splits them and uses $s_{\text{out}}$ and $t_{\text{in}}$). The bowtie graph, the matching example, the dag of the path-cover figure, the alternating-path pseudocode, Menger's theorem, the certificate-cut theorem and its proof, the second baseball example and the exam-scheduling table are additions. Corrections and precisions: the proof of the path-cover claim labels its two directions the wrong way round ("⇐" proves cover ⇒ matching) and projects "$M'$" where it means $M$, and the bipartite graph is called both $G'$ and $H$ (p. 361); the matching proof says "$U$" and "$W$" for $L$ and $R$ (p. 356); the exam-scheduling edge rule writes $A[\ell,k]$ for $A[k,\ell]$ (p. 360); the baseball proof's "conversely" needs an integral flow, which it does not say (p. 365), and its formula $R[i] = \sum_j G[i,j]$ does not match the example, where Detroit's $R[n] = 27$ counts games outside the division (pp. 364–365), and the pairs $1\le i<j<n$ give $\binom{n-1}{2}$ game vertices, not the $\binom{n}{2}$ it counts (p. 365); project selection says "maximum cut problem" for minimum cut and "includes all its dependents" for its prerequisites (pp. 366–367); tuple selection counts tuples with multiplicity; the bound $\lvert f^*\rvert\le V-1$ for edge-disjoint paths assumes no parallel edges; and the faculty-hiring condition for all pairs of a professor's classes reduces to consecutive pairs only under the triangle inequality. Every reduction on this page is implemented and compared with brute force — exhaustive search for edge-disjoint and vertex-disjoint paths, all matchings, all multisets of tuples, all exam schedules, all path covers and class partitions, all outcomes of the remaining games, all valid project sets — on thousands of random instances, the vertex-splitting correspondence is checked against a linear program, and every number, table and figure label is recomputed, by `tools/checks/a11.py`.
