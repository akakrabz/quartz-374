> [!abstract] In one breath
> A **flow network** is a directed graph with a source $s$, a target $t$ and a non-negative **capacity** on every edge. An **$(s,t)$-flow** puts a number on every edge so that flow is **conserved** at every vertex except $s$ and $t$; it is **feasible** if $0\le f\le c$ on every edge, and its **value** $|f|$ is the net flow out of $s$. An **$(s,t)$-cut** splits the vertices into $S\ni s$ and $T\ni t$, and its **capacity** $\lVert S,T\rVert$ adds up the capacities of the edges from $S$ to $T$. Every feasible flow is at most every cut (Lemma 10.1), and the **maxflow-mincut theorem** says the largest flow equals the smallest cut. Its proof is an algorithm: in the **residual graph** $G_f$ — unused capacity forward, cancellable flow backward — any $s$–$t$ path is an **augmenting path** that increases the flow, and when there is none, the vertices reachable from $s$ form a cut of the same value. **Ford–Fulkerson** augments along any such path; with integer capacities it ends with an integral maximum flow after at most $|f^*|$ augmentations, $O(E|f^*|)$ time — exponential in the worst case, and possibly endless with irrational capacities. **Edmonds and Karp** augment along the fattest path, $O(E^2\log E\log|f^*|)$, or the shortest one, $O(VE^2)$ for any capacities; and the bound to cite is Orlin's $O(VE)$. (Flows are not part of CS 374 this semester; see the end of the page.)

## 10.1 Flows

The chapter opens with the first recorded application: in the mid-1950s Harris and Ross modeled the rail network from the Soviet Union into Eastern Europe as a graph with 44 vertices and 105 weighted edges and determined, by trial and error, both how much could be shipped and the cheapest way to cut the network ("the bottleneck"). Both problems take a directed graph $G = (V,E)$ with two special vertices: the **source** $s$ and the **target** $t$.

> [!definition] Definition — flow, conservation, value, feasibility
> An **$(s,t)$-flow** (or just *flow*) is a function $f\colon E\to\mathbb{R}$ that satisfies the **conservation constraint** at every vertex $v$ other than $s$ and $t$:
> $$
> \sum_u f(u\to v) = \sum_w f(v\to w),
> $$
> that is, the flow into $v$ equals the flow out of $v$. By convention $f(u\to v) = 0$ when $u\to v$ is not an edge. The **value** of $f$ is the net flow out of the source:
> $$
> |f| = \sum_w f(s\to w) - \sum_u f(u\to s).
> $$
> Given a **capacity** function $c\colon E\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$ (again $c(u\to v) = 0$ for non-edges), $f$ is **feasible** if $0\le f(e)\le c(e)$ for every edge $e$. A flow **saturates** $e$ if $f(e) = c(e)$ and **avoids** $e$ if $f(e) = 0$. The **maximum flow problem**: given $G$, $c$, $s$ and $t$, find a feasible $(s,t)$-flow of largest value.

**The value is also the net flow into $t$.** Write $\partial f(v) = \sum_w f(v\to w) - \sum_u f(u\to v)$ for the net flow **out of** $v$. Conservation says $\partial f(v) = 0$ for every $v\ne s,t$. Summed over all vertices, the flow on each edge $x\to y$ appears twice, with $+$ in $\partial f(x)$ and with $-$ in $\partial f(y)$, so $\sum_v\partial f(v) = 0$, hence $\partial f(s) + \partial f(t) = 0$ and
$$
|f| = \partial f(s) = -\partial f(t).
$$
(The book's displayed formula for $\partial f(v)$ on p. 329 subtracts the outflow from the inflow, the opposite of its words "net flow out"; with that sign, $|f|$ would be $-\partial f(s)$. The page uses the words' version, which is also the one the proof of Lemma 10.1 uses.)

> [!trap] Flow traps
> - **Conservation holds at every vertex except $s$ and $t$**, and nothing more is required of a flow; feasibility ($0\le f\le c$) is a separate condition.
> - **The value is a net flow**: subtract the flow on edges *into* $s$. A feasible flow can even have negative value (one unit on an edge $t\to s$); the maximum is still $\ge 0$, because the zero flow is feasible.
> - **A flow is a function on edges**, not a collection of paths — §10.5 shows how to convert one into the other.

## 10.2 Cuts

> [!definition] Definition — cut, capacity, minimum cut
> An **$(s,t)$-cut** (or just *cut*) is a partition of the vertices into two sets $S$ and $T$ — $S\cup T = V$, $S\cap T = \varnothing$ — with $s\in S$ and $t\in T$. Its **capacity** is the total capacity of the edges that start in $S$ and end in $T$:
> $$
> \lVert S,T\rVert = \sum_{v\in S}\sum_{w\in T} c(v\to w).
> $$
> The definition is asymmetric: edges from $T$ to $S$ do not count. The **minimum cut problem**: find an $(s,t)$-cut of smallest capacity. (A network with $V$ vertices has $2^{V-2}$ cuts.)

Intuitively, a minimum cut is the cheapest way to separate $t$ from $s$: delete the edges from $S$ to $T$ and no flow can get through. The two problems are linked by a simple inequality.

> [!key] Lemma 10.1
> Let $f$ be **any** feasible $(s,t)$-flow and $(S,T)$ **any** $(s,t)$-cut. Then $|f|\le\lVert S,T\rVert$. Moreover, $|f| = \lVert S,T\rVert$ if and only if $f$ **saturates every edge from $S$ to $T$** and **avoids every edge from $T$ to $S$**.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Add up $\partial f(v)$ over $v\in S$. Every term except $\partial f(s) = |f|$ is zero by conservation ($t\notin S$). In the sum, the flow on an edge with both endpoints in $S$ appears once with each sign and cancels, so only the edges that cross the cut remain:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> |f| = \sum_{v\in S}\partial f(v) &= \sum_{v\in S}\sum_{w\in T} f(v\to w) - \sum_{v\in S}\sum_{u\in T} f(u\to v)\\
> &\le \sum_{v\in S}\sum_{w\in T} f(v\to w) \;\le\; \sum_{v\in S}\sum_{w\in T} c(v\to w) = \lVert S,T\rVert.
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> The first inequality drops the flow from $T$ to $S$, which is $\ge 0$; it is an equality if and only if $f$ avoids every edge from $T$ to $S$. The second uses $f\le c$; it is an equality if and only if $f$ saturates every edge from $S$ to $T$. $\square$

The equality in the middle needs only conservation, not feasibility: **for every flow and every cut, the flow out of $S$ minus the flow into $S$ is $|f|$.** And the lemma immediately gives a certificate of optimality:

> [!key] Corollary — certificates
> If $|f| = \lVert S,T\rVert$ for some feasible flow $f$ and some cut $(S,T)$, then $f$ is a maximum flow and $(S,T)$ is a minimum cut: no flow can exceed $\lVert S,T\rVert$, and no cut can be smaller than $|f|$.

> [!example] Example — four cuts of the network in the figure above
> | $S$ | edges from $S$ to $T$ | $\lVert S,T\rVert$ | flow of $f$ out of $S$ − into $S$ |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | $\{s\}$ | $s\to a$, $s\to c$ | $20 + 10 = 30$ | $10 - 0$ |
> | $\{s, a, c\}$ | $a\to b$, $c\to d$ | $5 + 10 = 15$ | $10 - 0$ ($b\to c$ carries 0) |
> | $\{s, a, b\}$ | $s\to c$, $a\to c$, $b\to c$, $b\to t$ | $10 + 10 + 15 + 15 = 50$ | $15 - 5$ ($d\to b$ carries 5) |
> | $V\setminus\{t\}$ | $b\to t$, $d\to t$ | $15 + 20 = 35$ | $10 - 0$ |
>
> The last column is always $|f| = 10$, and every capacity is at least 10, as Lemma 10.1 says. Of the network's $2^4 = 16$ cuts, $(\{s,a,c\}, \{b,d,t\})$ is the only one of capacity 15, the minimum (the book's Figure 10.3): no flow in this network can exceed 15. Its edge $b\to c$, from $T$ to $S$, does not count in the capacity.

> [!trap] Cut traps
> - **Only edges from $S$ to $T$ count in the capacity.** Edges from $T$ to $S$ count — negatively — in the net flow across the cut, not in its capacity.
> - **A cut is a partition of the vertices**; "the edges of the cut" are the ones leaving $S$. Choose $S$, and the capacity follows.
> - **A minimum cut minimizes total capacity**, not the number of edges.

## 10.3 The maxflow-mincut theorem

Surprisingly, Lemma 10.1 is always tight: some flow and some cut have the same value. This was first proved by Lester Ford (of Ford's shortest-path algorithm, [[2-algorithms/08-shortest-paths#83-the-only-sssp-algorithm|§8.3]]) and Delbert Fulkerson in 1954, and independently by Elias, Feinstein and Shannon in 1956.

> [!key] The maxflow-mincut theorem
> In every flow network with source $s$ and target $t$, the value of the maximum $(s,t)$-flow equals the capacity of the minimum $(s,t)$-cut.

**Reduced networks.** The proof is easier if $G$ is **reduced**: there is at most one edge between any two vertices, so $c(u\to v) = 0$ or $c(v\to u) = 0$. Any network can be made reduced without changing its maximum flow value or minimum cut capacity: **subdivide** an edge $u\to v$ by a new vertex $x$, replacing it with the path $u\to x\to v$, and set $c(u\to x) = c(x\to v) = c(u\to v)$ (the book's Figure 10.4).

> [!definition] Definition — residual capacity, residual graph
> Let $f$ be a feasible flow in a reduced network. The **residual capacity** of an ordered pair of vertices is
> $$
> c_f(u\to v) = \begin{cases} c(u\to v) - f(u\to v) & \text{if } u\to v\in E\\ f(v\to u) & \text{if } v\to u\in E\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
> $$
> It is how much more flow could go from $u$ to $v$: the unused capacity of a **forward** edge, or the flow on the reverse edge, which can be cancelled (a **backward** edge). Because $f$ is feasible, $c_f\ge 0$. The **residual graph** $G_f = (V, E_f)$ has an edge $u\to v$ for every pair with $c_f(u\to v) > 0$; it may contain edges that are not in $G$, and an edge with $0 < f(u\to v) < c(u\to v)$ appears in both directions, so $G_f$ is usually not reduced.

> [!definition] Definition — augmenting path, augmentation
> An **augmenting path** is a directed path $P$ from $s$ to $t$ in $G_f$. Its **bottleneck** $F = \min_{u\to v\in P} c_f(u\to v)$ is positive. **Augmenting** $f$ along $P$ gives the flow $f'$ defined on every edge $u\to v$ of $G$ by
> $$
> f'(u\to v) = \begin{cases} f(u\to v) + F & \text{if } u\to v\in P\\ f(u\to v) - F & \text{if } v\to u\in P\\ f(u\to v) & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
> $$
> Then $f'$ is a feasible flow and $|f'| = |f| + F$.

The proof of the theorem shows more: three statements about a feasible flow $f$ are equivalent.

> [!key] Maximum flows, augmenting paths and cuts
> For a feasible flow $f$, the following are equivalent:
> 1. $f$ is a maximum flow;
> 2. the residual graph $G_f$ has no path from $s$ to $t$;
> 3. $|f| = \lVert S,T\rVert$ for some cut $(S,T)$ — and then one such cut is $S$ = the vertices reachable from $s$ in $G_f$.

> [!proof]- Proof of the theorem (click to expand)
> **An augmenting path increases the flow (1 ⇒ 2).** Let $f'$ be the augmented flow. *Feasibility*, edge by edge: if $u\to v\in P$, then $f'(u\to v) = f(u\to v) + F > f(u\to v)\ge 0$ and $f'(u\to v)\le f(u\to v) + c_f(u\to v) = c(u\to v)$; if $v\to u\in P$, then $f'(u\to v) = f(u\to v) - F < f(u\to v)\le c(u\to v)$ and $f'(u\to v)\ge f(u\to v) - c_f(v\to u) = 0$; otherwise nothing changes. *Conservation*: $P$ enters and leaves every interior vertex once; entering along a forward edge adds $F$ to its inflow and along a backward edge removes $F$ from its outflow, and leaving adds $F$ to its outflow or removes $F$ from its inflow — either way the net flow out of the vertex is unchanged. *Value*: $P$ is a path, so only its first edge touches $s$, and it raises $\partial f(s)$ by $F$. So $|f'| = |f| + F > |f|$, and $f$ was not maximum.
>
> **No augmenting path gives a cut of equal value (2 ⇒ 3).** Let $S$ be the set of vertices reachable from $s$ in $G_f$ and $T = V\setminus S$; then $t\in T$, so $(S,T)$ is a cut. For $u\in S$ and $v\in T$, $c_f(u\to v) = 0$ — otherwise $v$ would be reachable. If $u\to v\in E$, that means $c(u\to v) - f(u\to v) = 0$: $f$ saturates every edge from $S$ to $T$. If $v\to u\in E$, it means $f(v\to u) = 0$: $f$ avoids every edge from $T$ to $S$. By Lemma 10.1, $|f| = \lVert S,T\rVert$.
>
> **3 ⇒ 1** is the Corollary above. **The theorem:** let $f$ be a maximum flow. (One exists: the feasible flows form a closed and bounded subset of $\mathbb{R}^E$ and $|f|$ is continuous; for integer capacities, §10.4 constructs one.) By 1 ⇒ 2 ⇒ 3, some cut has capacity $|f|$, and by Lemma 10.1 no cut has less. $\square$

**Finding a minimum cut.** Given a maximum flow $f$, build $G_f$ ($O(E)$ time) and run [[2-algorithms/05-basic-graph-algorithms#55-whatever-first-search|whatever-first search]] from $s$ ($O(V+E)$): the marked vertices are $S$, and $(S, V\setminus S)$ is a minimum cut. It is the minimum cut with the **smallest** source side: every minimum cut $(S', T')$ is saturated and avoided by $f$ (Lemma 10.1), so no residual edge leaves $S'$, and the search from $s$ stays inside $S'$.

> [!example] Example — one augmentation finishes the job
> For the flow of value 10 in the §10.1 figure, the residual graph is the one drawn above; every vertex, $t$ included, is reachable from $s$. The augmenting path $s\to c\to a\to b\to d\to t$ has residual capacities $10, 10, 5, 5, 15$, so $F = 5$. Its edges $s\to c$, $a\to b$ and $d\to t$ are forward: their flow rises by 5. Its edges $c\to a$ and $b\to d$ are backward: they lower $f(a\to c)$ from 10 to 5 and $f(d\to b)$ from 5 to 0. In words, 5 units that went $s\to a\to c$ now enter $c$ directly from $s$, freeing $a$ to send 5 units to $b$; and $d$ stops sending flow back up to $b$ and sends all 10 units straight to $t$. The new flow has value 15. In its residual graph, $s$ reaches only $a$ and $c$, because $a\to b$ and $c\to d$ are saturated: the cut $S = \{s, a, c\}$ of capacity $5 + 10 = 15$ certifies that the flow is maximum.

> [!trap] Residual-graph traps
> - **Backward edges are essential.** Pushing flow along paths of $G$ and deleting saturated edges — no backward edges — can get stuck below the maximum. In the network $s\to a$, $s\to b$, $a\to b$, $a\to t$, $b\to t$, all of capacity 1, pushing 1 unit along $s\to a\to b\to t$ leaves no path with spare capacity, yet the maximum flow is 2 ($s\to a\to t$ and $s\to b\to t$). The residual graph has the path $s\to b\to a\to t$, whose backward edge $b\to a$ cancels the flow on $a\to b$. (This is the point of the book's Exercise 17.)
> - **The residual capacity of a backward edge $v\to u$ is the flow $f(u\to v)$**, not the capacity.
> - **The reachable set is a minimum cut only when $f$ is maximum.** For the flow of value 10 above it is all of $V$.
> - **Antiparallel edges.** If both $u\to v$ and $v\to u$ are edges, $c_f(u\to v)$ has two contributions; the definition above assumes a reduced network, which subdivision provides.

## 10.4 Ford and Fulkerson's augmenting-path algorithm

The proof suggests the algorithm: start with the zero flow, and augment along **any** augmenting path until there is none. (The book states it in one sentence; the pseudocode below writes it out for a reduced network.)

```text
FordFulkerson(G, c, s, t):
    for every edge u→v
        f(u→v) ← 0
    while G_f has a path from s to t
        P ← any path from s to t in G_f            ⟨⟨whatever-first search in G_f⟩⟩
        F ← min{c_f(u→v) : u→v ∈ P}                ⟨⟨the bottleneck⟩⟩
        for every edge u→v of P
            if u→v ∈ E
                f(u→v) ← f(u→v) + F                ⟨⟨forward edge: push⟩⟩
            else
                f(v→u) ← f(v→u) − F                ⟨⟨backward edge: cancel⟩⟩
    return f
```

When it stops, $G_f$ has no augmenting path, so $f$ is a maximum flow (§10.3). Whether it stops depends on the capacities.

> [!key] Integrality theorem
> If all capacities are integers, then there is a maximum flow in which the flow on every edge is an integer — and Ford–Fulkerson finds one.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> By induction on the iterations, all flow values and all residual capacities are integers: before the first iteration the flow is 0 and the residual capacities are the capacities; afterwards the bottleneck $F$ is a minimum of integers, and augmenting changes each flow value, and hence each residual capacity, by $0$ or $\pm F$. So every iteration raises $|f|$ by a positive integer. Since $|f|$ never exceeds the capacity of any cut, the algorithm halts, and it halts with no augmenting path, that is, with a maximum flow. $\square$

**Running time.** With integer capacities, each iteration raises the value by at least 1, so there are at most $|f^*|$ iterations, where $f^*$ is the maximum flow. Each one builds $G_f$ and finds a path by whatever-first search in $O(E)$ time:
$$
\text{Ford–Fulkerson with integer capacities: } O(E\,|f^*|) \text{ time.}
$$
This is fast when the maximum flow is small — with capacities 1 and no parallel edges, $|f^*|\le V-1$ (at most one unit on each edge out of $s$), so $O(VE)$ — which is exactly the situation of many applications in [[2-algorithms/11-applications-of-flows-and-cuts|Chapter 11]].

**The bound is tight.** In Edmonds and Karp's example below, with a large integer $X$, the maximum flow is $2X$, but Ford–Fulkerson may alternate between the augmenting paths $s\to u\to v\to t$ and $s\to v\to u\to t$, each carrying one unit: $2X$ iterations, $\Theta(X) = \Omega(|f^*|)$. The network is described by $O(\log X)$ bits, so this running time is **exponential** in the size of the input.

**Rational and irrational capacities** (♥ in the book). Multiplying all capacities by the same positive constant multiplies every flow and cut by it, so with rational capacities (scaled by a common denominator) Ford–Fulkerson still halts, in exponential time. With **irrational** capacities it may run forever — and the infinite sequence of flows need not even converge to the maximum. The smallest example is Uri Zwick's (1993) network of 6 vertices and 9 edges; Ford and Fulkerson gave one with 10 vertices and 48 edges in 1962.

> [!example] Example — Zwick's network does not terminate
> Let $\varphi = (\sqrt5 - 1)/2\approx 0.618$, so that $1 - \varphi = \varphi^2$. Track the residual capacities $c - f$ of the three horizontal edges $b\to a$, $b\to c$, $d\to c$ (capacities $1, 1, \varphi$); the six edges of capacity $X$ never carry more than 3 units, so they are never the bottleneck. After the central path $s\to b\to c\to t$ (1 unit) the three numbers are $1, 0, \varphi$, which is $\varphi^{k-1}, 0, \varphi^k$ with $k = 1$. From $\varphi^{k-1}, 0, \varphi^k$, four augmentations:
>
> | path | pushes | horizontal residual capacities afterwards |
> |---|---|---|
> | B: $s\,d\,c\,b\,a\,t$ | $\varphi^k$ | $\varphi^{k+1},\ \varphi^k,\ 0$ |
> | C: $s\,b\,c\,d\,t$ | $\varphi^k$ | $\varphi^{k+1},\ 0,\ \varphi^k$ |
> | B: $s\,d\,c\,b\,a\,t$ | $\varphi^{k+1}$ | $0,\ \varphi^{k+1},\ \varphi^{k+2}$ |
> | A: $s\,a\,b\,c\,t$ | $\varphi^{k+1}$ | $\varphi^{k+1},\ 0,\ \varphi^{k+2}$ |
>
> — the same pattern with $k$ replaced by $k+2$, so it repeats forever. After $4n+1$ augmentations the horizontal residual capacities are $\varphi^{2n}, 0, \varphi^{2n+1}$, and the flow value tends to
> $$
> 1 + 2\sum_{i\ge 1}\varphi^i = 1 + \frac{2\varphi}{1-\varphi} = 2 + \sqrt5 \approx 4.24 < 7,
> $$
> while the maximum flow is $2X + 1$. (The book, p. 336, writes the residual capacities as $\varphi^{2n-2}, 0, \varphi^{2n-1}$ and the limit as $1 + 2/(1-\varphi) = 4 + \sqrt5$; both are off — the exponents by 2, the limit by 2 — but its conclusion, a limit below 7, stands.)

The practical lesson: with floating-point capacities, a careless implementation of Ford–Fulkerson can loop forever on round-off errors without coming close to the answer.

> [!trap] Ford–Fulkerson traps
> - **$O(E|f^*|)$ is not polynomial**: $|f^*|$ can be exponential in the number of bits of the input. Quote it only when $|f^*|$ is known to be small (at most $V$, say); otherwise cite $O(VE)$ (§10.7).
> - **The integrality theorem says *some* maximum flow is integral**, not every one. With all capacities 1, the network $s\to a$, $s\to b$, $a\to c$, $b\to c$, $c\to t$ has the fractional maximum flow $\tfrac12$ on the first four edges and 1 on $c\to t$.
> - **Termination needs integer (or rational) capacities**; with irrational or floating-point ones there is no guarantee.
> - **"Any path" includes backward edges.** An implementation that searches only the edges of $G$ is GreedyFlow, which can fail (the diamond above).

## 10.5 Combining and decomposing flows

Flows are defined edge by edge, with constraints at the vertices, but they have a second description, as sums of paths and cycles. Here flows are arbitrary functions satisfying conservation — not necessarily feasible or non-negative.

**Linear combinations.** If $f$ and $g$ are $(s,t)$-flows and $\alpha,\beta$ are real numbers, then $h = \alpha f + \beta g$, that is $h(u\to v) = \alpha f(u\to v) + \beta g(u\to v)$, is an $(s,t)$-flow with $|h| = \alpha|f| + \beta|g|$: conservation and value are linear in $f$.

> [!definition] Definition — path flow, cycle flow, flow decomposition
> For a directed path $P$ from $s$ to $t$, the **path flow** $P$ is 1 on the edges of $P$ and 0 on all other edges; it is an $(s,t)$-flow of value 1. For a directed cycle $C$, the **cycle flow** $C$ is 1 on the edges of $C$ and 0 elsewhere; it is an $(s,t)$-flow of value 0. (The book uses one letter for the path and its flow, and also puts $-1$ on the reversals of the path's edges. For a path of $G$ in a reduced network those reversals are never edges, and with antiparallel edges the $-1$ breaks conservation, so that clause is omitted here. The clause does fit a path that may use edges of a reduced network backward, such as an augmenting path: then $P$ is a flow of value 1, and augmenting gives $f + F\cdot P$.) A linear combination of path flows and cycle flows is a **flow decomposition**.

> [!key] Flow decomposition theorem
> Every non-negative $(s,t)$-flow $f$ with $|f|\ge 0$ is a positive linear combination of directed $(s,t)$-paths and directed cycles, such that an edge lies on at least one of them if and only if $f(u\to v) > 0$. Their number is at most $\#f$, the number of edges with positive flow — so at most $E$. Moreover:
> - a **circulation** (a flow with $|f| = 0$, so conserved at every vertex) is a combination of at most $\max\{0, \#f - 1\}$ cycles, and no paths;
> - an **acyclic** flow (no directed cycle of edges with positive flow) is a combination of paths only.
>
> (The book states the theorem for every non-negative flow. A non-negative flow can have negative value — one unit on an edge $t\to s$ — and is then no such combination, since paths have value 1 and cycles 0; the book's proof covers exactly the cases $|f| = 0$ and $|f| > 0$.)

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> **Circulations**, by induction on $\#f$. If $\#f = 0$, $f$ is the empty combination. Otherwise pick an edge $u\to v$ with $f(u\to v) > 0$ and walk $v_0 = u$, $v_1 = v$, $v_2, \ldots$ along edges with positive flow; conservation guarantees that every vertex entered with positive flow can be left along such an edge, so the walk continues until it repeats a vertex, and the part between the two visits is a simple directed cycle $C$ on which $f > 0$. Let $F = \min_{e\in C} f(e)$ and $f' = f - F\cdot C$. Then $f'$ is a non-negative circulation, and at least one edge of $C$ has $f'(e) = 0$, so $\#f'\le\#f - 1$. If $f' = 0$, then $f = F\cdot C$ is one cycle, and $\#f\ge 2$ (a cycle has at least two edges). Otherwise $f'$ has at most $\#f' - 1\le\#f - 2$ cycles by induction, and $f = f' + F\cdot C$ has at most $\#f - 1$.
>
> **Flows with $|f| > 0$.** Add a new edge $t\to s$ carrying $|f|$ units. Now flow is conserved at $s$ and $t$ too, so this is a circulation with $\#f + 1$ positive edges, a combination of at most $\#f$ cycles. Remove the new edge: each cycle through it becomes a path from $s$ to $t$, and the others remain cycles. $\square$

Consequences:

- **Cancelling cycles.** While some directed cycle has positive flow on all its edges, subtract its bottleneck around it: the value does not change, the flow stays feasible (flows only decrease), and an edge is emptied each time, so this stops — with an acyclic flow. So every network has an **acyclic maximum flow**. (Merely dropping the cycles of a decomposition is not enough: a sum of paths can contain a cycle, as the second decomposition in the traps below shows.)
- **An $O(VE)$ algorithm.** The proof is an algorithm: add the edge $t\to s$ with $|f|$ units; repeatedly start at any vertex with positive outflow and follow edges with positive flow until a vertex repeats — at most $V$ steps, and conservation, which now holds everywhere, means the walk never gets stuck — then subtract the cycle's bottleneck, which empties at least one edge. That is at most $E$ rounds of $O(V)$ time: $O(VE)$. (The book, p. 339, walks from $s$ and stops as soon as it reaches $t$. That can get stuck: on the circulation $s\to a\to t\to b\to s$ it removes the path $s\,a\,t$ and leaves the flow $t\to b\to s$, which cannot continue past $s$. Stopping at $t$ is safe only for the amount the added edge $t\to s$ still carries. The book's version does work whenever no edge into $s$ carries flow — for instance on every flow Ford–Fulkerson computes, because an augmenting path never enters $s$.)
- **A lower bound for path-by-path algorithms.** A decomposition has at most $E$ paths and cycles of at most $V$ edges each, so its total size is $O(VE)$; and there are flows for which **every** decomposition has total size $\Omega(VE)$. Any maximum-flow algorithm that builds its flow one path or cycle at a time — every implementation of Ford–Fulkerson — therefore needs $\Omega(VE)$ time in the worst case.

> [!trap] Decomposition traps
> - **Decompositions are not unique.** The flow in the figure is also the sum of five paths and no cycle: $s\,a\,b\,c\,d\,t$ (3), $s\,c\,d\,b\,t$ (3), $s\,a\,b\,t$ (1), $s\,a\,c\,d\,t$ (5) and $s\,c\,d\,t$ (3) — even though the flow is not acyclic ($c\to d\to b\to c$ carries flow on every edge).
> - **Only the paths carry value**; the weights of the paths add up to $|f|$, the cycles contribute nothing.
> - **The theorem needs $f\ge 0$ and $|f|\ge 0$** — a maximum flow satisfies both.

## 10.6 Edmonds and Karp's algorithms

Ford–Fulkerson does not say which augmenting path to take, and its bad worst case comes from bad choices. In the early 1970s Jack Edmonds and Richard Karp published two natural rules, both of which make it efficient.

### Fattest augmenting paths

> *Choose the augmenting path with the largest bottleneck value.*

The **maximum-bottleneck** path from $s$ to $t$ in $G_f$ is found by best-first search, like Jarník's ([[2-algorithms/07-minimum-spanning-trees#74-jarníks-prims-algorithm|§7.4]]) or Dijkstra's ([[2-algorithms/08-shortest-paths#86-best-first-dijkstras-algorithm|§8.6]]) algorithm: grow a tree from $s$, always adding the vertex reachable through the best path found so far, where a path is as good as its bottleneck. Concretely, run Dijkstra's algorithm with a max-priority queue and the key $b(v) = \max_{u\to v}\min\{b(u), c_f(u\to v)\}$ in place of $\min_{u\to v}\{\mathit{dist}(u) + w(u\to v)\}$, starting from $b(s) = \infty$: $O(E\log V)$ time. (Emulating Kruskal's algorithm — insert edges by decreasing capacity until $s$ reaches $t$ — also works, less efficiently for directed graphs.)

**Number of iterations.** Let $f$ be the current flow. The maximum flow of the residual network $G_f$ (with capacities $c_f$) has value $|f^*| - |f|$, and by the decomposition theorem it is a sum of at most $E$ paths and cycles. (At most $E$ because a maximum flow of $G_f$ never needs both $u\to v$ and $v\to u$: cancelling the 2-cycle leaves a maximum flow.) The paths carry the whole value, so one of them carries at least $(|f^*| - |f|)/E$, and the fattest path is at least that fat. Each augmentation therefore multiplies the remaining value $|f^*| - |f|$ by at most $1 - 1/E$, and after $E\ln|f^*|$ iterations the remaining value is at most
$$
|f^*|\left(1 - \frac1E\right)^{E\ln|f^*|} < |f^*|\, e^{-\ln|f^*|} = 1.
$$
With integer capacities the remaining value is an integer, hence 0: the flow is maximum. So there are $O(E\log|f^*|)$ iterations of $O(E\log V)$ time:
$$
\text{fattest augmenting paths, integer capacities: } O(E^2\log E\,\log|f^*|) \text{ time}
$$
($\log E = O(\log V)$) — polynomial in the input size, unlike plain Ford–Fulkerson. With arbitrary real capacities this rule too can run forever (Queyranne's example, the book's Exercise 18), but the analysis shows that its flows converge to a maximum flow.

### Shortest augmenting paths

> *Choose the augmenting path with the smallest number of edges.*

This rule was suggested as a heuristic by Ford and Fulkerson themselves, and a variant was found independently by Yefim Dinitz in 1970. The shortest augmenting path is found by [[2-algorithms/08-shortest-paths#84-unweighted-graphs-breadth-first-search|breadth-first search]] in $G_f$, $O(E)$ time — and, surprisingly, the number of iterations is polynomial **regardless of the capacities**. Let $f_i$ be the flow after $i$ augmentations and $G_i$ its residual graph ($f_0 = 0$, $G_0 = G$), and let $\mathit{level}_i(v)$ be the unweighted distance from $s$ to $v$ in $G_i$ — the level of $v$ in a breadth-first search tree — with $\mathit{level}_i(v) = \infty$ if $s$ cannot reach $v$. The $(i+1)$st augmenting path is a shortest $s$–$t$ path in $G_i$.

> [!key] Lemma 10.2
> $\mathit{level}_i(v)\ge\mathit{level}_{i-1}(v)$ for every vertex $v$ and every $i > 0$: levels never decrease.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Fix $i$; induction on $\mathit{level}_i(v)$ (not on $i$). If $v = s$, both levels are 0; if $s$ cannot reach $v$ in $G_i$, $\mathit{level}_i(v) = \infty$. Otherwise let $s\cdots u\to v$ be a shortest path to $v$ in $G_i$, so $\mathit{level}_i(v) = \mathit{level}_i(u) + 1$, and by induction $\mathit{level}_i(u)\ge\mathit{level}_{i-1}(u)$. It remains to show $\mathit{level}_{i-1}(u)\ge\mathit{level}_{i-1}(v) - 1$. If $u\to v$ is an edge of $G_{i-1}$, this is the defining property of breadth-first levels. If not, the $i$th augmentation created it, so $v\to u$ was on the $i$th augmenting path, a shortest path in $G_{i-1}$, and $\mathit{level}_{i-1}(v) = \mathit{level}_{i-1}(u) - 1$. Either way $\mathit{level}_i(v) = \mathit{level}_i(u) + 1\ge\mathit{level}_{i-1}(u) + 1\ge\mathit{level}_{i-1}(v)$. $\square$

Each augmentation removes its bottleneck edges from the residual graph (they become saturated, or their flow is fully cancelled), and may add reversals of path edges.

> [!key] Lemma 10.3
> During the shortest-augmenting-path algorithm, each edge $u\to v$ disappears from the residual graph at most $V/2$ times.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Suppose $u\to v$ is in $G_i$, disappears in the next augmentation, and is back in $G_{j+1}$ for the first time after that ($i < j$). It disappeared because it was on the shortest path augmented in $G_i$, so $\mathit{level}_i(v) = \mathit{level}_i(u) + 1$; it came back because $v\to u$ was on the shortest path augmented in $G_j$, so $\mathit{level}_j(u) = \mathit{level}_j(v) + 1$. By Lemma 10.2,
> $$
> \mathit{level}_j(u) = \mathit{level}_j(v) + 1\ge\mathit{level}_i(v) + 1 = \mathit{level}_i(u) + 2.
> $$
> So between consecutive disappearances of $u\to v$ the level of $u$ grows by at least 2. When $u\to v$ is on an augmenting path, $\mathit{level}(u)\le V-2$, so it disappears at most $V/2$ times. $\square$

**Running time.** At least one edge disappears in every iteration. The residual graph has at most $2E$ possible edges ($u\to v$ and $v\to u$ for each edge of $G$), each disappearing at most $V/2$ times, so there are at most $VE$ iterations (the book counts $E$ residual edges and writes $EV/2$; the asymptotic bound is the same). Each takes $O(E)$ time:
$$
\text{Edmonds–Karp (shortest augmenting paths): } O(VE^2) \text{ time, for any capacities.}
$$
Dinitz's algorithm also augments along shortest paths, with extra bookkeeping ("blocking flows"), in $O(V^2E)$ time.

> [!example] Example — the book's network and Edmonds and Karp's bad example
> On the network of §10.1, starting from the zero flow, breadth-first search finds $s\to a\to b\to t$ (3 edges, bottleneck 5) and then $s\to c\to d\to t$ (bottleneck 10): the maximum flow 15 after two augmentations, without ever using a backward edge. The fattest-path rule also needs two: a path of bottleneck 10 (such as $s\to c\to d\to t$), then one of bottleneck 5. On the bad example of §10.4 both rules choose $s\to u\to t$ and $s\to v\to t$ and finish after 2 augmentations instead of $2X$.

| rule for the augmenting path | finding it | iterations | total time |
|---|---|---|---|
| any path (Ford–Fulkerson), integer capacities | whatever-first search, $O(E)$ | $\le\lvert f^*\rvert$ | $O(E\lvert f^*\rvert)$ |
| fattest path, integer capacities | best-first search, $O(E\log V)$ | $O(E\log\lvert f^*\rvert)$ | $O(E^2\log E\log\lvert f^*\rvert)$ |
| shortest path (Edmonds–Karp), any capacities | breadth-first search, $O(E)$ | $\le VE$ | $O(VE^2)$ |

> [!trap] Edmonds–Karp traps
> - **"Edmonds–Karp" usually means the shortest-path rule**: $O(VE^2)$ for any capacities, irrational ones included. The fattest-path bound depends on $|f^*|$ and needs integer capacities.
> - **Lemma 10.2 is about breadth-first levels in the changing residual graph**, and its proof uses that every augmenting path is a shortest path; it is not a property of arbitrary augmenting paths.
> - **Iterations, not time:** $O(VE)$ augmentations of $O(E)$ each give $O(VE^2)$.

## 10.7 Further progress

Decades of research have produced much faster algorithms; the book's Figure 10.10 lists several, all purely combinatorial and with running times independent of the capacities. Most come in a simple version and a faster one that maintains a spanning tree of the network with **dynamic trees**, so that each iteration takes logarithmic time.

| technique | direct | with dynamic trees | sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| blocking flow | $O(V^2E)$ | $O(VE\log V)$ | Dinitz; Karzanov; Even and Itai; Sleator and Tarjan |
| network simplex | $O(V^2E)$ | $O(VE\log V)$ | Dantzig; Goldfarb and Hao; Goldberg, Grigoriadis and Tarjan |
| push-relabel (generic) | $O(V^2E)$ | — | Goldberg and Tarjan |
| push-relabel (FIFO) | $O(V^3)$ | $O(VE\log(V^2/E))$ | Goldberg and Tarjan |
| push-relabel (highest label) | $O(V^2\sqrt E)$ | — | Cheriyan and Maheshwari; Tunçel |
| pseudoflow | $O(V^2E)$ | $O(VE\log V)$ | Hochbaum |
| incremental BFS | $O(V^2E)$ | $O(VE\log(V^2/E))$ | Goldberg, Held, Kaplan, Tarjan and Werneck |
| compact networks | — | $O(VE)$ | Orlin |

**The bound to cite.** James Orlin's algorithm (2012) runs in $O(VE)$ time, exactly the worst-case size of a flow decomposition. It is far beyond the book — it uses several complicated older algorithms as black boxes — and it does not build a decomposition explicitly (with $O(V)$ edges an extension runs in $O(V^2/\log V)$ time). For analyzing algorithms that *use* maximum flows, the book's advice is to write on your cheat sheet: **maximum flows can be computed in $O(VE)$ time.**

**Unit capacities.** If every capacity is 1, Karzanov (1973) showed that Dinitz's blocking-flow algorithm runs in $O(\min\{V^{2/3}, E^{1/2}\}\,E)$ time — any flow in a unit-capacity network decomposes into paths of that total size — and Aleksander Mądry (2013) lowered this to $O(E^{10/7}\operatorname{polylog} E)$. Beyond the book: in 2022 Chen, Kyng, Liu, Peng, Probst Gutenberg and Sachdeva announced an algorithm for integer capacities that runs in almost-linear time, $E^{1+o(1)}$, by continuous optimization; the book's advice for citations is unaffected.

## Maximum flow as a black box

Chapter 11 uses this chapter only through a few facts:

- **Maximum flows** can be computed in $O(VE)$ time (§10.7); when the maximum flow value is known to be small, Ford–Fulkerson's $O(E|f^*|)$ is as good or better (for capacities 1 and no parallel edges, $|f^*|\le V-1$: $O(VE)$).
- **Integrality**: with integer capacities, Ford–Fulkerson returns a maximum flow with an integer on every edge (§10.4). With capacities 1 such a flow is just a set of edges, and decomposing it (§10.5 — every bottleneck is 1) gives $|f^*|$ edge-disjoint paths from $s$ to $t$, plus possibly some cycles.
- **Minimum cuts** come for free: from a maximum flow, whatever-first search in $G_f$ finds a minimum cut in $O(V+E)$ more time (§10.3).

A problem solved by a **flow reduction** is written up like a [[concepts/graph-reductions|graph reduction]], plus a proof in both directions:

> [!template] Template — solving a problem by reduction to maximum flow
> 1. **The network.** Its vertices, its **directed** edges and their capacities, the source $s$ and the target $t$ — all as explicit functions of the input.
> 2. **The claim, proved both ways.** "The input has a solution of size $k$ if and only if the network has an $(s,t)$-flow of value $k$." ⇒: turn a solution into a flow (check conservation and capacities). ⇐: take an **integral** maximum flow (integrality theorem) and turn it into a solution, usually by decomposing it into paths.
> 3. **The algorithm.** Compute a maximum flow as a black box: Ford–Fulkerson in $O(E|f^*|)$ if the flow value is small, otherwise $O(VE)$.
> 4. **The time**, in the parameters of the **input**: how many vertices and edges the network has, as functions of the input size.

Concept pages: [[concepts/maximum-flow]], [[concepts/flow-reductions]].

## Summary

- A flow network: directed graph, source $s$, target $t$, capacities $c\ge 0$. An $(s,t)$-flow is conserved at every vertex but $s$ and $t$; it is feasible if $0\le f\le c$; its value $|f|$ is the net flow out of $s$, equal to the net flow into $t$.
- An $(s,t)$-cut $(S,T)$ has capacity $\lVert S,T\rVert$ = total capacity of the edges from $S$ to $T$ (edges from $T$ to $S$ do not count). The net flow across any cut is $|f|$.
- Lemma 10.1: $|f|\le\lVert S,T\rVert$ for every feasible flow and every cut, with equality iff the edges $S\to T$ are saturated and the edges $T\to S$ avoided — then both are optimal.
- Residual graph $G_f$: forward edges with $c - f > 0$, backward edges with $f > 0$. Maxflow-mincut: $f$ is maximum ⇔ $G_f$ has no $s$–$t$ path ⇔ $|f| = \lVert S,T\rVert$ for $S$ = the vertices $s$ reaches in $G_f$, which is a minimum cut. Max flow = min cut.
- Ford–Fulkerson augments along any path in $G_f$: with integer capacities an integral maximum flow (integrality theorem) in $O(E|f^*|)$ time, which can be exponential (Edmonds–Karp's $2X$ example); with irrational capacities it may never halt (Zwick).
- Every non-negative flow with $|f|\ge 0$ is a positive combination of at most $E$ paths and cycles, computable in $O(VE)$ time; there is always an acyclic maximum flow; path-by-path algorithms need $\Omega(VE)$ time.
- Edmonds–Karp: fattest paths, $O(E^2\log E\log|f^*|)$ for integer capacities; shortest paths by breadth-first search, levels never decrease (Lemma 10.2), each edge disappears at most $V/2$ times (Lemma 10.3), $O(VE^2)$ for any capacities.
- Faster algorithms exist (Dinitz $O(V^2E)$, push-relabel, …); cite Orlin's $O(VE)$.

> [!exam] On the exams
> **Flows are not taught in CS/ECE 374 this semester and appear on no exam.** The Fall 2026 course calendar has no lecture on them — after shortest paths and Floyd–Warshall it moves on to NP-hardness — the official Midterm 2 announcement lists flows among the topics not on the exam, and Erickson's own book page says CS 374 covers the book "except for flows". So there is no rubric for this chapter; it is on the site for completeness, for the book's sake, and because [[2-algorithms/11-applications-of-flows-and-cuts|Chapter 11]] builds on it. Three ideas transfer to material that *is* examined. **Reductions:** a flow reduction is a [[0-toolkit/06-how-exams-are-graded#13-graph-reductions|graph reduction]] — an explicit graph built from the input, a named problem on it, a black-box algorithm, the time in the input's parameters — and its proof in two directions ("solution ⇒ flow" and "integral flow ⇒ solution") has the same shape as the two directions of an NP-hardness reduction in [[2-algorithms/12-np-hardness|Chapter 12]]. **Cuts as certificates:** Lemma 10.1 lets one explicit object bound every possible solution — a single cut of capacity 15 proves that no flow in the network exceeds 15 — just as one fooling set of size $k$ proves that every DFA for a language needs $k$ states ([[concepts/fooling-sets]]). **Residual graphs are graph reductions:** "is this flow maximum?" is reachability in $G_f$, answered by whatever-first search, and Edmonds–Karp is breadth-first search while the fattest path is Dijkstra-style best-first search.
