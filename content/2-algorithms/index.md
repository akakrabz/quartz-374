A review of Jeff Erickson's [*Algorithms*](https://jeffe.cs.illinois.edu/teaching/algorithms/) for someone who has **already read it**: the algorithms with their recurrences and running times, the proof ideas, the patterns to reuse, the exam write-up format, and the traps. The first three chapters build one idea in three steps. **Recursion** solves a problem by trusting the recursive call on smaller instances (Ch 1), and recursion trees turn its running time into a sum. **Backtracking** is recursion over a sequence of decisions — try every choice for the next one (Ch 2) — and it is exponential because the same subproblems come back again and again. **Dynamic programming** keeps the backtracking recurrence and stores each subproblem's answer in an array (Ch 3), which turns exponential into polynomial time. **Greedy algorithms** make each decision without looking back (Ch 4) — fast, usually wrong, and correct only with an exchange-argument proof. Then the course turns to **graphs**: the vocabulary, the representations and one search that does everything — whatever-first search (Ch 5) — and its most useful instance, **depth-first search**, with its starting and finishing times, topological sort, dynamic programming on dags and strong components (Ch 6). Most graph exam problems are **reductions**: build a graph from the input and call a standard search on it. **Shortest paths** (Ch 8–9) add weights: one generic relaxation algorithm, specialized by the order in which edges are relaxed — breadth-first, topological, best-first (Dijkstra), all at once (Bellman–Ford) — and, for all pairs, Floyd–Warshall's dynamic program. **Maximum flows** (Ch 10) push as much as possible from $s$ to $t$; the maxflow-mincut theorem certifies the answer, and many matching and scheduling problems reduce to it (Ch 11). Finally, **NP-hardness** (Ch 12) is how to argue that no fast algorithm is likely: reduce a known hard problem *to* yours.

**Midterm 2** (Monday, November 9, 7–9 pm) covers Homeworks 5–9 — recursion, divide and conquer, backtracking, dynamic programming, graph search, depth-first search, topological sort and shortest paths, plus the interface of Bellman–Ford and Floyd–Warshall (Chapters 1–3, 5, 6, 8 and part of 9); review with **[[0-toolkit/08-review-algorithms|the Midterm 2 review]]**. Greedy algorithms and MSTs were not lectured; flows (Ch 10–11) are not part of CS 374 this semester; NP-hardness (Ch 12) is lectured after Midterm 2 and tested on the final (Friday, December 11). Every chapter page follows the book's sections, links the chapter PDF, writes each algorithm in the format its rubric grades, and ends with a summary and an "On the exams" box. Each chapter has a page of **solved book exercises** — the easier ones, roughly the first ten; hard (♥) problems are skipped.
