*Solved exercises for [[2-algorithms/11-applications-of-flows-and-cuts|Chapter 11]] · book pp. 368–372 · statements paraphrased; see the book for the originals*

These are Exercises 11.1–11.10, with only part (a) of 11.5. Skipped: 11.5(c), marked ♥ (particularly challenging); 11.5(b), which needs a careful argument that a huge grid can be shrunk to size polynomial in the number of terminals — far more thought than an easy exercise deserves; and 11.11–11.22, beyond the first batch (11.16(c), 11.19(b) and 11.20–11.22 are ♥, and 11.19(b) is also ♣). Flows are not part of CS 374 this semester and appear on no exam; the modeling habits are the same as for graph reductions, though. Every modeling solution is written in the flow-reduction format ([[concepts/flow-reductions]]): **the network** — vertices, directed edges, capacities, source and target as explicit functions of the input; **the claim** — the input has a solution of size $k$ if and only if the network has a flow of value $k$, proved in both directions (solution → flow; integral flow → solution); **the algorithm**, a maximum-flow algorithm as a black box; and **the time**, in the input's parameters. Write your own answer before opening a solution.

> [!tip] Black boxes from Chapters 10 and 11, used below without re-explanation
> - **Flows and cuts** ([[2-algorithms/10-maximum-flows-and-minimum-cuts#101-flows|§10.1]], [[2-algorithms/10-maximum-flows-and-minimum-cuts#102-cuts|§10.2]], [[concepts/maximum-flow]]): a flow obeys $0\le f\le c$ and conservation at every vertex but $s$ and $t$; $\lvert f\rvert$ is the net flow out of $s$. For every cut $(S, T)$ with $s\in S$, $t\in T$: $\lvert f\rvert = f(S\to T) - f(T\to S)\le\lVert S, T\rVert$, the total capacity of the edges from $S$ to $T$.
> - **Maxflow–mincut** ([[2-algorithms/10-maximum-flows-and-minimum-cuts#103-the-maxflow-mincut-theorem|§10.3]]): the maximum flow value equals the minimum cut capacity. A flow $f$ is maximum if and only if the residual graph $G_f$ has no path from $s$ to $t$; then the vertices reachable from $s$ in $G_f$ form a minimum cut.
> - **Integrality, Ford–Fulkerson** ([[2-algorithms/10-maximum-flows-and-minimum-cuts#104-ford-and-fulkersons-augmenting-path-algorithm|§10.4]], [[concepts/ford-fulkerson]]): with integer capacities, Ford–Fulkerson returns an integral maximum flow $f^*$ in $O(E\lvert f^*\rvert)$ time — the right choice when the flow value is small. In general, Orlin's algorithm takes $O(VE)$ time ([[2-algorithms/10-maximum-flows-and-minimum-cuts#107-further-progress|§10.7]]).
> - **Flow decomposition** ([[2-algorithms/10-maximum-flows-and-minimum-cuts#105-combining-and-decomposing-flows|§10.5]]): an integral flow of value $k$ is the sum of $k$ unit flows along simple paths from $s$ to $t$ plus unit flows around cycles (which can be dropped).
> - **Chapter 11 gadgets**: vertex capacities by splitting $v$ into $v_{\mathrm{in}}\to v_{\mathrm{out}}$ ([[2-algorithms/11-applications-of-flows-and-cuts#112-vertex-capacities-and-vertex-disjoint-paths|§11.2]]); maximum bipartite matching as a unit-capacity flow $s\to L\to R\to t$, in $O(VE)$ time by Ford–Fulkerson ([[2-algorithms/11-applications-of-flows-and-cuts#113-bipartite-matching|§11.3]], [[concepts/bipartite-matching]]); the fewest vertex-disjoint paths covering a dag, $V$ minus a maximum matching ([[2-algorithms/11-applications-of-flows-and-cuts#115-disjoint-path-covers|§11.5]]).

## Exercise 11.1 — Balanced graphs

> [!question] Exercise 11.1 (book p. 368)
> $G = (V, E)$ is a directed graph in which every vertex has equal in-degree and out-degree. $G$ contains $k$ edge-disjoint paths from a vertex $u$ to another vertex $v$. Must $G$ also contain $k$ edge-disjoint paths from $v$ to $u$? Give a proof or a counterexample.

> [!hint]- Hint
> Count the edges that cross a cut in each direction.

> [!solution]- Solution
> **Yes.** Write $e(S)$ for the number of edges from a vertex set $S$ to $V\setminus S$.
>
> *Paths versus cuts* ([[2-algorithms/11-applications-of-flows-and-cuts#111-edge-disjoint-paths|§11.1]]). With unit capacities, the largest number of edge-disjoint paths from $x$ to $y$ is the maximum $(x, y)$-flow value (integrality and flow decomposition), which by the maxflow–mincut theorem is the minimum of $e(S)$ over the vertex sets $S$ with $x\in S$ and $y\notin S$.
>
> *Balance.* For every vertex set $S$,
> $$
> 0 = \sum_{x\in S}\bigl(\deg_{\mathrm{out}}(x) - \deg_{\mathrm{in}}(x)\bigr) = e(S) - e(V\setminus S),
> $$
> because an edge with both ends in $S$ adds 1 to an out-degree and 1 to an in-degree in the sum, an edge leaving $S$ only to an out-degree, and an edge entering $S$ only to an in-degree. As many edges leave $S$ as enter it.
>
> *Conclusion.* Let $T$ be any vertex set with $v\in T$ and $u\notin T$, and $S = V\setminus T$. Each of the $k$ paths from $u\in S$ to $v\notin S$ has an edge leaving $S$, and these edges are distinct, so $e(S)\ge k$. By balance, $e(T) = e(V\setminus T) = e(S)\ge k$. Every set separating $v$ from $u$ thus has at least $k$ outgoing edges, so there are at least $k$ edge-disjoint paths from $v$ to $u$. (Parallel edges change nothing.)
>
> **Answer.** Yes. In a balanced graph every vertex set has as many outgoing as incoming edges, so every cut from $v$ to $u$ has as many edges as the opposite cut from $u$ to $v$, which is at least $k$.

> [!trap]
> - Balance is essential: with the single edge $u\to v$, there is one path from $u$ to $v$ and none back.
> - "Reverse the $k$ paths" is not a proof: the reversed edges need not exist.

## Exercise 11.2 — A path through a given vertex

> [!question] Exercise 11.2 (book p. 368)
> Given an undirected graph $G = (V, E)$ and three vertices $u$, $v$, $w$, describe and analyze an algorithm that decides whether there is a path from $u$ to $w$ that passes through $v$. *(Book's hint: if $G$ were directed, this problem would be NP-hard.)*

> [!hint]- Hint
> Cut the path at $v$. You need two paths out of $v$, one to $u$ and one to $w$, sharing no vertex other than $v$.

> [!solution]- Solution
> Paths are simple, and $u$, $v$, $w$ are distinct (if $v$ is $u$ or $w$, the question is whether $u$ reaches $w$). A path from $u$ to $w$ through $v$, cut at $v$, is two paths starting at $v$ — one to $u$ (reversed), one to $w$ — that share only $v$; conversely, such two paths, the first reversed, join into one path. So we look for two such paths.
>
> **Network $H$** (vertex capacities as in [[2-algorithms/11-applications-of-flows-and-cuts#112-vertex-capacities-and-vertex-disjoint-paths|§11.2]]):
> - vertices $x_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $x_{\mathrm{out}}$ for every $x\ne v$; the source $v_{\mathrm{out}}$; a target $t$;
> - edges, all of capacity 1: $x_{\mathrm{in}}\to x_{\mathrm{out}}$ for every $x\ne v$; $x_{\mathrm{out}}\to y_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $y_{\mathrm{out}}\to x_{\mathrm{in}}$ for every edge $xy\in E$ (except those that would enter $v$); and $u_{\mathrm{out}}\to t$, $w_{\mathrm{out}}\to t$.
>
> $H$ has $2V$ vertices and at most $V + 2E + 1$ edges.
>
> **Claim.** $G$ has a path from $u$ to $w$ through $v$ if and only if $H$ has a $(v_{\mathrm{out}}, t)$-flow of value 2.
>
> *Path ⇒ flow.* Push one unit from $v_{\mathrm{out}}$ along the half to $u$ — through $x_{\mathrm{in}}\to x_{\mathrm{out}}$ for each vertex $x$ on it — and then into $t$; do the same along the half to $w$. The halves share no vertex but $v$, so no edge carries two units. The value is 2.
>
> *Flow ⇒ path.* Take an integral flow of value 2 and decompose it into two unit paths from $v_{\mathrm{out}}$ to $t$. Both edges into $t$ carry one unit. The only edge into $u_{\mathrm{out}}$ is $u_{\mathrm{in}}\to u_{\mathrm{out}}$, of capacity 1, so the unit through $u$ goes straight on to $t$: one path ends at $u$, and the other never visits $u$. The same holds for $w$. Every split edge carries at most one unit, so the two paths share no vertex except $v$. Read in $G$, they are the two halves.
>
> **Algorithm.** Build $H$ and run Ford–Fulkerson; answer yes if and only if the maximum flow value is 2 (and then read the path off the flow).
>
> **Time.** The two edges into $t$ cap the value at 2, so Ford–Fulkerson stops after at most two augmentations, each found by whatever-first search in $O(V + E)$ time.
>
> **Answer.** Yes if and only if the unit-vertex-capacity network from $v$ to a target fed by $u$ and $w$ carries a flow of value 2: $O(V+E)$ time.

> [!trap]
> - **Two separate searches fail.** In the graph with edges $ux$, $xv$, $uy$, $yv$, $xw$, the first-found path $u, x, v$ leaves no way from $v$ to $w$, yet $u, y, v, x, w$ is a solution. The flow chooses both halves at once.
> - Without vertex capacities, the halves could meet at a vertex, and joined they would not be a path.

## Exercise 11.3 — Multi-terminal flows

> [!question] Exercise 11.3 (book pp. 368–369)
> A directed graph $G = (V, E)$ with edge capacities has sources $s_1, \dots, s_\sigma$ and targets $t_1, \dots, t_\tau$, no vertex being both. A **multi-terminal flow** $f: E\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$ respects the capacities and conserves flow at every vertex that is neither a source nor a target; its value is the total excess of the sources,
> $$
> \lvert f\rvert = \sum_{i=1}^{\sigma}\Bigl(\sum_{w} f(s_i\to w) - \sum_{x} f(x\to s_i)\Bigr).
> $$
> We want a multi-terminal flow of maximum value; which source sends to which target does not matter.
>
> (a) Prove that the following algorithm computes a maximum multi-terminal flow; MaxFlow($G$, $s$, $t$) returns a maximum $(s, t)$-flow.
>
> ```text
> MaxMultiFlow(G, s[1 .. σ], t[1 .. τ]):
>     f ← 0                                    ⟨⟨initialize the flow⟩⟩
>     for i ← 1 to σ
>         for j ← 1 to τ
>             f′ ← MaxFlow(G_f, s[i], t[j])
>             f ← f + f′                       ⟨⟨update the flow⟩⟩
>     return f
> ```
>
> (b) Describe a more efficient algorithm.

### (a) Correctness of MaxMultiFlow

> [!hint]- Hint
> Two lemmas. A multi-terminal flow is maximum once no target is reachable from any source in $G_f$. And if no residual edge leaves a vertex set $R$, adding a maximum flow from $a$ to $b$ creates none — unless the flow comes into $R$ from outside ($a\notin R$, $b\in R$).

> [!solution]- Solution
> **Conventions** ([[2-algorithms/10-maximum-flows-and-minimum-cuts#103-the-maxflow-mincut-theorem|§10.3]]). If $f'$ is a flow in the residual graph $G_f$, then $f + f'$ — on an edge $x\to y$ of $G$, $f(x\to y) + f'(x\to y) - f'(y\to x)$ — is a feasible flow in $G$. It conserves flow wherever $f$ and $f'$ both do; each vertex's excess (outflow minus inflow) is the sum of its excesses in $f$ and $f'$; and its residual capacities are
> $$
> c_{f+f'}(x\to y) = c_f(x\to y) - f'(x\to y) + f'(y\to x),
> $$
> so $G_{f+f'}$ is the residual graph of $f'$ in $G_f$. The total excess of a vertex set $S$ is the flow on the edges leaving $S$ minus the flow on the edges entering $S$.
>
> **Lemma A (stopping rule).** If $G_f$ has no path from a source to a target, $f$ is a maximum multi-terminal flow.
>
> *Proof.* Let $S$ be the set of vertices reachable in $G_f$ from some source. $S$ contains every source and no target, and its other vertices have excess 0. So for every multi-terminal flow $g$, $\lvert g\rvert$ is the total excess of $S$, which is $g(\text{out of } S) - g(\text{into } S)\le\lVert S, V\setminus S\rVert$. No residual edge leaves $S$, so $f$ saturates every edge leaving $S$ and is 0 on every edge entering $S$: $\lvert f\rvert = \lVert S, V\setminus S\rVert\ge\lvert g\rvert$. ∎
>
> **Lemma B (closed sets stay closed).** Suppose no edge of $G_f$ leaves the vertex set $R$, and $f'$ is a maximum $(a, b)$-flow in $G_f$, where it is *not* the case that $a\notin R$ and $b\in R$. Then no edge of $G_{f+f'}$ leaves $R$.
>
> *Proof.* $f'$ is 0 on the edges leaving $R$, whose residual capacity is 0. The net flow of $f'$ out of $R$ is 0 if $a$ and $b$ lie on the same side, and $\lvert f'\rvert$ if $a\in R$ and $b\notin R$ — but then $\lvert f'\rvert$ is at most the residual capacity of the cut around $R$, which is 0. So the net flow out of $R$ is 0, $f'$ is 0 on the edges entering $R$ as well, and the formula gives $c_{f+f'} = 0$ on every edge leaving $R$. ∎
>
> **The algorithm is correct.** Each iteration adds a flow in $G_f$, so $f$ stays a feasible multi-terminal flow. Fix a source $s_i$.
> - Right after iteration $(i, j)$, the new $G_f$ is the residual graph of the maximum flow $f'$, so it has no path from $s_i$ to $t_j$. Let $R_{ij}$ be the set of vertices reachable from $s_i$ at that moment: no residual edge leaves it, and $t_j\notin R_{ij}$. Iterations $(i, j+1), \dots, (i, \tau)$ start at $s_i\in R_{ij}$, so by Lemma B $R_{ij}$ stays closed, and everything reachable from $s_i$ stays inside it.
> - Hence the set $R_i$ reachable from $s_i$ after iteration $(i, \tau)$ lies inside every $R_{ij}$ and contains no target. Every later iteration ends at a target, which is outside $R_i$, so by Lemma B $R_i$ stays closed until the end.
>
> At the end, no target is reachable from any source in $G_f$, and Lemma A says $f$ is maximum. ∎ (The proof does not care which maximum flow each MaxFlow call returns.)
>
> **Answer.** After source $s_i$'s inner loop, the set it reaches in $G_f$ is closed and contains no target; later augmentations end outside that set, so they never reopen it. At the end no source reaches a target, and the cut around everything the sources reach proves that $f$ is maximum.

### (b) One maximum flow

> [!hint]- Hint
> One super-source, one super-target.

> [!solution]- Solution
> **Network $G'$.** $G$ plus a new source $s^*$ with an edge $s^*\to s_i$ of capacity $\infty$ for every $i$, and a new target $t^*$ with an edge $t_j\to t^*$ of capacity $\infty$ for every $j$. (Any capacity larger than the sum of all capacities in $G$ works as well as $\infty$.)
>
> **Claim.** If $f^*$ is a maximum $(s^*, t^*)$-flow in $G'$, its restriction $f$ to the edges of $G$ is a maximum multi-terminal flow, and $\lvert f\rvert = \lvert f^*\rvert$.
>
> *Proof.* $f$ respects the capacities and conserves flow at every vertex that is not a terminal. Conservation of $f^*$ at $s_i$ makes the excess of $s_i$ in $f$ equal to $f^*(s^*\to s_i)$, so $\lvert f\rvert = \lvert f^*\rvert$. If $G_f$ had a path from some $s_i$ to some $t_j$, then $s^*\to s_i\to\cdots\to t_j\to t^*$ would be an augmenting path for $f^*$ (the infinite edges always have residual capacity), contradicting the maximality of $f^*$. By Lemma A of part (a), $f$ is maximum. ∎
>
> **Time.** One maximum flow on $V + 2$ vertices and $E + \sigma + \tau$ edges: $O(VE)$ with Orlin's algorithm — instead of the $\sigma\tau$ maximum flows of part (a).
>
> **Answer.** Join a super-source to every source and every target to a super-target with infinite capacities and compute one maximum flow: $O(VE)$ time.

## Exercise 11.4 — Closing the fewest stations

> [!question] Exercise 11.4 (book p. 369)
> The rail network of Sodor is an undirected graph $G = (V, E)$: a vertex per station, an edge per rail connection. A disease has broken out at station $s$, and the controller wants to close as few stations as possible so that no train can travel from $s$ to station $t$; a closed station cannot be passed through, and $s$ and $t$ themselves cannot be closed. Describe and analyze an algorithm that computes the minimum number of stations to close. On the book's example (redrawn below) the answer is 2.

> [!hint]- Hint
> This is a minimum *vertex* cut. Split each station into an entrance and an exit joined by an edge of capacity 1, and make every rail edge too expensive to cut.

> [!solution]- Solution
> If $s$ and $t$ are adjacent, no closing helps: report that. Assume they are not.
>
> **Network $H$.** Vertices $x_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $x_{\mathrm{out}}$ for every station $x$. Edges: $x_{\mathrm{in}}\to x_{\mathrm{out}}$ with capacity 1 for every $x\notin\{s, t\}$ and capacity $\infty$ for $x\in\{s, t\}$ (they cannot be closed); for every rail connection $xy$, the edges $x_{\mathrm{out}}\to y_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $y_{\mathrm{out}}\to x_{\mathrm{in}}$ with capacity $\infty$. Source $s_{\mathrm{out}}$, target $t_{\mathrm{in}}$. $2V$ vertices, $V + 2E$ edges.
>
> **Claim.** The fewest stations whose closing blocks all travel from $s$ to $t$ equals the minimum capacity of an $(s_{\mathrm{out}}, t_{\mathrm{in}})$-cut in $H$ — by the maxflow–mincut theorem, the maximum flow value.
>
> *Closing $k$ stations ⇒ a cut of capacity $k$.* Let $C$ be a blocking set of $k$ stations and $R$ the stations reachable from $s$ in $G - C$, so $t\notin R$. Put $S = \{x_{\mathrm{in}}, x_{\mathrm{out}} : x\in R\}\cup\{x_{\mathrm{in}} : x\in C\}$; it contains $s_{\mathrm{out}}$ and not $t_{\mathrm{in}}$. An edge $x_{\mathrm{out}}\to y_{\mathrm{in}}$ with $x\in R$ has $y\in R\cup C$, since otherwise $y$ would be reachable in $G - C$. So the only edges leaving $S$ are the $k$ edges $x_{\mathrm{in}}\to x_{\mathrm{out}}$ with $x\in C$.
>
> *A cut of capacity $k$ ⇒ closing $k$ stations.* A cut $(S, T)$ of finite capacity $k$ contains no infinite edge, so its edges are $k$ split edges $x_{\mathrm{in}}\to x_{\mathrm{out}}$; let $C$ be those $k$ stations. A route $s = y_0, y_1, \dots, y_\ell = t$ in $G$ that avoids $C$ would give the path $s_{\mathrm{out}}\to y_{1,\mathrm{in}}\to y_{1,\mathrm{out}}\to\cdots\to t_{\mathrm{in}}$ in $H$, none of whose edges goes from $S$ to $T$; it starts in $S$, so it would end in $S$ — but $t_{\mathrm{in}}\in T$. So $C$ blocks all travel.
>
> **Algorithm.** Compute a maximum flow $f^*$ by Ford–Fulkerson and return $\lvert f^*\rvert$. To name the stations, let $S$ be the vertices reachable from $s_{\mathrm{out}}$ in the final residual graph (a minimum cut) and close every $x$ with $x_{\mathrm{in}}\in S$ and $x_{\mathrm{out}}\notin S$.
>
> **Time.** Closing all $V - 2$ other stations works, so $\lvert f^*\rvert\le V - 2$ and Ford–Fulkerson takes $O(E\lvert f^*\rvert) = O(VE)$ time.
>
> On the book's network (figure), the flow has value 2 — two routes that share no station besides $s$ and $t$ — and the cut found closes the two circled stations. (In general, an integral maximum flow decomposes into $\lvert f^*\rvert$ routes sharing only $s$ and $t$: the minimum number of stations to close equals the maximum number of such routes, Menger's theorem.)
>
> **Answer.** The value of a maximum flow in the network with unit-capacity split edges for the stations and infinite-capacity rail edges: $O(VE)$ time.

## Exercise 11.5 — The escape problem

> [!question] Exercise 11.5(a) (book pp. 369–370)
> The $n\times n$ grid is the undirected graph on vertices $(i, j)$, $1\le i, j\le n$, in which each vertex is adjacent to $(i\pm 1, j)$ and $(i, j\pm 1)$ when these exist; vertices with $i\in\{1, n\}$ or $j\in\{1, n\}$ are **boundary** vertices. Given $m$ distinct **terminals** $(x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_m, y_m)$, the **escape problem** asks whether there are $m$ vertex-disjoint paths connecting the terminals to $m$ distinct boundary vertices. (The book's positive example is redrawn below.)
>
> (a) Describe and analyze an algorithm whose running time is a small polynomial in $n$.
>
> (Part (b), polynomial in $m$ when the input is just $n$ and the terminal list, and part (c), marked ♥, are skipped.)

> [!hint]- Hint
> Give every grid vertex capacity 1, feed each terminal one unit from a super-source, and let every boundary vertex drain one unit into a super-target.

> [!solution]- Solution
> **Network $H$.**
> - Vertices: $p_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $p_{\mathrm{out}}$ for every grid vertex $p$; a source $s$; a target $t$.
> - Edges, all of capacity 1: $p_{\mathrm{in}}\to p_{\mathrm{out}}$ for every $p$; $p_{\mathrm{out}}\to q_{\mathrm{in}}$ for every ordered pair of grid neighbors $p, q$; $s\to (x_k, y_k)_{\mathrm{in}}$ for $k = 1, \dots, m$; $p_{\mathrm{out}}\to t$ for every boundary vertex $p$.
>
> $2n^2 + 2$ vertices and $O(n^2)$ edges.
>
> **Claim.** The terminals can escape if and only if $H$ has an $(s, t)$-flow of value $m$.
>
> *Escape paths ⇒ flow.* For each escape path $P_k$, push one unit from $s$ to its terminal, along $P_k$ (through the split edge of every vertex on it) and from its boundary end into $t$. The paths are vertex-disjoint, so every split edge, grid edge and edge into $t$ carries at most one unit, and the $m$ edges out of $s$ carry one each. The value is $m$.
>
> *Flow ⇒ escape paths.* Take an integral flow of value $m$ and decompose it into $m$ unit paths from $s$ to $t$. All $m$ edges out of $s$ are saturated, so each terminal starts exactly one path. Every grid vertex has a split edge of capacity 1, so the paths are vertex-disjoint; in particular no path passes through another terminal, and their last grid vertices — boundary vertices, because only those have an edge into $t$ — are distinct. Merging $p_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $p_{\mathrm{out}}$ gives the $m$ escape paths. (A terminal on the boundary escapes by the path of length 0.)
>
> **Algorithm.** Compute a maximum flow by Ford–Fulkerson; answer yes if and only if its value is $m$.
>
> **Time.** The $4n - 4$ edges into $t$ cap the value at $4n - 4$ (for $n\ge 2$; so the answer is no whenever $m > 4n - 4$), and each augmenting path takes $O(n^2)$ time to find: $O(n^2\cdot n) = O(n^3)$.
>
> On the book's instance — 8 terminals in a $7\times 7$ grid — the maximum flow is 8, so the answer is yes.
>
> **Answer.** Unit capacities on the grid vertices, a source feeding each terminal one unit, each boundary vertex draining one unit to the target; yes if and only if the maximum flow is $m$: $O(n^3)$ time.

## Exercise 11.6 — Mini-golf

> [!question] Exercise 11.6 (book pp. 370–371)
> A mini-golf field is a closed polygon bounded by $m$ horizontal and vertical segments. It has $n$ starting points and $n$ holes, all at distinct locations, whose one-to-one correspondence has been lost; we know only that in the true correspondence each starting point sees its hole — the segment between them does not touch the boundary of the field. Describe and analyze an algorithm that computes a one-to-one correspondence between starting points and holes meeting this straight-line requirement, or reports that none exists. The input is the coordinates of the $m$ corners, the $n$ starting points and the $n$ holes; whether two segments intersect can be decided in $O(1)$ time.

> [!hint]- Hint
> Which holes can each starting point see? Then it is bipartite matching.

> [!solution]- Solution
> **Bipartite graph $B$.** Left vertices: the starting points $p_1, \dots, p_n$; right vertices: the holes $h_1, \dots, h_n$; an edge $p_ih_j$ whenever the segment $p_ih_j$ intersects none of the $m$ sides of the field. (Both endpoints lie in the field, so such a segment stays inside it.) Testing $n^2$ segments against $m$ sides takes $O(mn^2)$ time.
>
> **Network** ([[2-algorithms/11-applications-of-flows-and-cuts#113-bipartite-matching|§11.3]]). Edges $s\to p_i$, $p_i\to h_j$ for every edge $p_ih_j$ of $B$, and $h_j\to t$, all of capacity 1.
>
> **Claim.** A legal correspondence exists if and only if the network has a flow of value $n$; the edges $p_i\to h_j$ that carry flow are then the correspondence.
>
> *Correspondence ⇒ flow.* A legal correspondence pairs each $p_i$ with a visible $h_j$, every hole used once. Push one unit along $s\to p_i\to h_j\to t$ for each pair; these paths share only $s$ and $t$, so the flow is feasible, with value $n$.
>
> *Flow ⇒ correspondence.* In an integral flow of value $n$ all $n$ edges out of $s$ are saturated, each $p_i$ sends its unit to one hole, and each $h_j$ receives at most one unit (its edge to $t$ has capacity 1). So the edges carrying flow pair every starting point with a different visible hole.
>
> **Time.** $O(mn^2)$ to build $B$; Ford–Fulkerson with $\lvert f^*\rvert\le n$ on $O(n^2)$ edges takes $O(n^3)$. Total $O(mn^2 + n^3)$.
>
> **Answer.** Maximum bipartite matching between starting points and holes, with an edge for each segment that misses all $m$ walls; a perfect matching is the correspondence: $O(mn^2 + n^3)$ time.

## Exercise 11.7 — Cycle covers

> [!question] Exercise 11.7 (book p. 371)
> A **cycle cover** of a directed graph $G = (V, E)$ is a set of vertex-disjoint cycles that together contain every vertex. Describe and analyze an algorithm that finds a cycle cover of $G$ or correctly reports that none exists. *(Book's hint: use bipartite matching.)*

> [!hint]- Hint
> In a cycle cover every vertex has exactly one successor and exactly one predecessor.

> [!solution]- Solution
> **Bipartite graph $B$.** Two copies of every vertex: an out-copy $v^{-}$ on the left and an in-copy $v^{+}$ on the right (the book's $v^\flat$, $v^\sharp$ of §11.5), and an edge $u^{-}v^{+}$ for every edge $u\to v$ of $G$ (a loop $v\to v$, if $G$ has one, gives $v^{-}v^{+}$).
>
> **Network** ([[2-algorithms/11-applications-of-flows-and-cuts#113-bipartite-matching|§11.3]]). Edges $s\to v^{-}$ and $v^{+}\to t$ for every vertex $v$, and $u^{-}\to v^{+}$ for every edge $u\to v$, all of capacity 1; $2V + 2$ vertices, $E + 2V$ edges. Integral flows of value $k$ are exactly matchings of size $k$ in $B$.
>
> **Claim.** $G$ has a cycle cover if and only if $B$ has a perfect matching, that is, the network has a flow of value $V$.
>
> *Cover ⇒ matching.* In a cycle cover every vertex has exactly one outgoing and one incoming cover edge, so $M = \{u^{-}v^{+} : u\to v \text{ a cover edge}\}$ meets every $v^{-}$ once and every $v^{+}$ once: a perfect matching.
>
> *Matching ⇒ cover.* A perfect matching gives every vertex $u$ a successor $\sigma(u)$ with $u^{-}\sigma(u)^{+}\in M$, hence $u\to\sigma(u)\in E$. Every $v^{+}$ is matched exactly once, so $\sigma$ is a permutation of $V$. Following $\sigma$ from any vertex returns to it, so the vertices split into disjoint cycles $u\to\sigma(u)\to\sigma(\sigma(u))\to\cdots$ of $G$ — a cycle cover. (A cycle of length 1 is a loop; one of length 2 uses the two opposite edges $u\to v$ and $v\to u$.)
>
> **Algorithm.** If $E < V$, report that there is no cover (every vertex needs an outgoing edge). Otherwise compute a maximum flow by Ford–Fulkerson; if its value is less than $V$, report that there is no cover; else follow the successors to list the cycles in $O(V)$ time.
>
> **Time.** $\lvert f^*\rvert\le V$ on $O(E)$ edges: $O(VE)$.
>
> **Answer.** A cycle cover is a perfect matching between out-copies and in-copies of the vertices; find one by maximum flow and follow the successors: $O(VE)$ time.

## Exercise 11.8 — Domino tilings

> [!question] Exercise 11.8 (book p. 371)
> Some squares of an $n\times n$ checkerboard are deleted; the input is a boolean array $\mathit{Deleted}[1 .. n, 1 .. n]$. Describe and analyze an algorithm that decides whether the remaining squares can be tiled by dominoes: each domino covers exactly two adjacent undeleted squares, and each undeleted square is covered by exactly one domino. The output is a single boolean; the placement is not needed. For the book's board (redrawn below) the answer is True.

> [!hint]- Hint
> Color the board like a checkerboard. Every domino covers one square of each color.

> [!solution]- Solution
> Call square $(i, j)$ **black** if $i + j$ is even and **white** otherwise. Two squares that share a side have different colors, so a domino covers one black and one white square.
>
> **Network.** A source $s$, a target $t$, and a vertex per undeleted square; edges $s\to b$ for every black square $b$, $b\to w$ for every white square $w$ adjacent to $b$, and $w\to t$ for every white square $w$, all of capacity 1. This is the matching network of the bipartite graph of adjacent black–white pairs ([[2-algorithms/11-applications-of-flows-and-cuts#113-bipartite-matching|§11.3]]): at most $n^2 + 2$ vertices and $O(n^2)$ edges, since every square has at most 4 neighbors.
>
> **Claim.** The board can be tiled if and only if the numbers of black and of white squares are equal, say to $k$, and the network has a flow of value $k$.
>
> *Tiling ⇒ flow.* Each domino covers one black and one white square, so there are $k$ dominoes, $k$ black and $k$ white squares. Push one unit along $s\to b\to w\to t$ for each domino $\{b, w\}$; every square is in one domino, so every edge carries at most one unit, and the value is $k$.
>
> *Flow ⇒ tiling.* An integral flow of value $k$ saturates all $k$ edges out of $s$ and, since there are $k$ white squares, all $k$ edges into $t$. So each black square sends its unit to one adjacent white square, and each white square receives exactly one unit: the pairs carrying flow are $k$ dominoes covering every square exactly once.
>
> **Algorithm.** Count the squares of each color; if the counts differ, return False. Otherwise compute a maximum flow and return True if and only if its value is the number of black squares.
>
> **Time.** Ford–Fulkerson: $\lvert f^*\rvert\le n^2/2$ augmentations of $O(n^2)$ time each, $O(n^4)$ in all. (Hopcroft and Karp's matching algorithm, mentioned in §11.3, would take $O(E\sqrt{V}) = O(n^3)$.)
>
> On the book's board, 56 squares remain, 28 of each color, and the maximum flow is 28: True.
>
> **Answer.** Equal color counts and a perfect matching between adjacent black and white squares, found by maximum flow: $O(n^4)$ time with Ford–Fulkerson.

## Exercise 11.9 — One token per row and column

> [!question] Exercise 11.9 (book pp. 371–372)
> Each square of an $n\times n$ grid is black or white; the input is a boolean array $\mathit{IsWhite}[1 .. n, 1 .. n]$. Describe and analyze an algorithm that decides whether tokens can be placed so that every token is on a white square and every row and every column contains exactly one token. For the grid of the book's Figure 11.13 the answer is True.

> [!hint]- Hint
> A placement pairs every row with a column.

> [!solution]- Solution
> **Network.** A source $s$, a vertex $r_i$ per row, a vertex $c_j$ per column, a target $t$; edges $s\to r_i$ and $c_j\to t$ for all $i, j$, and $r_i\to c_j$ whenever $\mathit{IsWhite}[i, j]$, all of capacity 1. (The matching network of the bipartite graph of rows and columns with an edge per white square.) $2n + 2$ vertices, at most $n^2 + 2n$ edges.
>
> **Claim.** A placement exists if and only if the network has a flow of value $n$.
>
> *Placement ⇒ flow.* For the token in row $i$ and column $j$, push one unit along $s\to r_i\to c_j\to t$; the tokens use distinct rows and distinct columns, so every edge carries at most one unit, and the value is $n$.
>
> *Flow ⇒ placement.* An integral flow of value $n$ saturates every $s\to r_i$, so each row $i$ sends its unit to one column $j$ with $\mathit{IsWhite}[i, j]$; each column receives at most one unit, so the $n$ columns are distinct. Put a token on each square $(i, j)$ whose edge carries flow: one per row, one per column, all white.
>
> **Time.** $\lvert f^*\rvert\le n$ augmentations of $O(n^2)$ time: $O(n^3)$.
>
> On Figure 11.13 the maximum flow is 9, so the answer is True. The book's tokens sit in columns 4, 6, 1, 5, 2, 3, 8, 9, 7 of rows 1–9; the only other placement swaps the tokens of rows 3 and 4 (columns 5 and 1).
>
> **Answer.** True if and only if the rows-to-columns matching network, with an edge per white square, has a flow of value $n$: $O(n^3)$ time.

## Exercise 11.10 — Nesting boxes

> [!question] Exercise 11.10 (book p. 372)
> Each of $n$ boxes is given by its height, width and depth; every side length is strictly between 10 cm and 20 cm. One box fits inside another if it can be rotated so that its height, width and depth are respectively smaller than those of the other. Boxes can be nested recursively; a box is **visible** if it is not inside another box. Describe and analyze an algorithm that nests the boxes so that as few boxes as possible are visible.

> [!hint]- Hint
> The side lengths guarantee that a box can hold at most one box directly, so a nesting is a set of chains. Which section of Chapter 11 covers a dag with as few paths as possible?

> [!solution]- Solution
> **Fitting test.** Sort each box's sides: $a_1\le a_2\le a_3$ for box $A$ and $b_1\le b_2\le b_3$ for box $B$. Then $A$ fits in $B$ if and only if $a_k < b_k$ for $k = 1, 2, 3$. If these hold, the sorted orientation works. Conversely, if some rotation works, each of $B$'s $k$ smallest sides exceeds the side of $A$ placed along it; these are $k$ different sides of $A$, all smaller than $b_k$, so the $k$-th smallest side of $A$ is smaller than $b_k$.
>
> **Nestings are chains.** Two boxes side by side inside a third would need more than $10 + 10 = 20$ cm in some direction, but every side is shorter than 20 cm. So every box directly holds at most one box, a nesting is a set of chains $B_1\subset B_2\subset\cdots\subset B_r$, Russian-doll style, and the visible boxes are the outermost box of each chain.
>
> **Dag.** $D$ has a vertex per box and an edge $i\to j$ whenever box $i$ fits in box $j$; it is acyclic, because the sorted sides strictly increase along every edge. A chain is a path in $D$, and every path in $D$ is a chain. So the fewest visible boxes is the fewest vertex-disjoint paths covering $D$ ([[2-algorithms/11-applications-of-flows-and-cuts#115-disjoint-path-covers|§11.5]]).
>
> **Network.** Out-copies $i^{-}$ and in-copies $j^{+}$ of the boxes, a source $s$ and a target $t$; edges $s\to i^{-}$, $j^{+}\to t$ and $i^{-}\to j^{+}$ whenever box $i$ fits in box $j$, all of capacity 1.
>
> **Claim.** The boxes can be nested with $k$ visible boxes if and only if the network has a flow of value $n - k$.
>
> *Nesting ⇒ flow.* A nesting with $k$ chains places $n - k$ boxes directly inside another one. Push one unit along $s\to i^{-}\to j^{+}\to t$ for each box $i$ placed directly in box $j$: each box is directly inside at most one box and directly holds at most one, so every edge carries at most one unit, and the value is $n - k$.
>
> *Flow ⇒ nesting.* An integral flow of value $n - k$ is a matching of $n - k$ pairs $i^{-}j^{+}$. Place box $i$ directly inside box $j$ for each pair: every box goes inside at most one box and receives at most one, and the placements follow edges of the acyclic $D$, so they form chains. The outermost boxes are those whose out-copy is unmatched: $n - (n - k) = k$ visible boxes.
>
> **Algorithm.** Sort the sides of every box; build $D$ by testing all pairs; compute a maximum flow $f^*$; place box $i$ directly inside box $j$ for every edge $i^{-}\to j^{+}$ that carries flow. The number of visible boxes is $n - \lvert f^*\rvert$.
>
> **Time.** $O(n^2)$ to build $D$; Ford–Fulkerson with $\lvert f^*\rvert\le n$ on $O(n^2)$ edges takes $O(n^3)$. Total $O(n^3)$.
>
> **Example.** Boxes $A = 12\times 18\times 11$, $B = 13\times 12\times 17$, $C = 19\times 14\times 19$, $D = 15\times 16\times 13$, $E = 11\times 11\times 11$ have sorted sides $(11, 12, 18)$, $(12, 13, 17)$, $(14, 19, 19)$, $(13, 15, 16)$, $(11, 11, 11)$. The fitting pairs are $A, B, D, E$ in $C$ and $E$ in $B$ and in $D$. The maximum flow is 2, so 3 boxes stay visible — for example $E$ inside $B$ inside $C$, with $A$ and $D$ alone. No nesting does better: no two of $A$, $B$, $D$ fit one inside the other, so they lie in three different chains. (In general, the fewest visible boxes equals the largest number of boxes no two of which nest — Dilworth's theorem; the algorithm does not need it.)
>
> **Answer.** Sort each box's sides, build the dag of "fits inside", and cover it with as few vertex-disjoint paths as possible: $n$ minus a maximum matching between out-copies and in-copies, $O(n^3)$ time.

> [!trap]
> - Comparing the sides in the given orientation misses rotations; compare sorted sides.
> - The 10–20 cm condition is what makes this a matching problem. Without it a large box could hold several small boxes side by side, and the problem becomes a packing problem.
