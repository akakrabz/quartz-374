> [!abstract] In one breath
> Given a directed graph with edge weights $w$ and a source vertex $s$, the **single-source shortest path** problem asks for a shortest path from $s$ to every vertex, and the answer is stored as a **shortest-path tree**, which exists because subpaths of shortest paths are shortest paths. Negative edges are allowed, but a **negative cycle** that some walk from $s$ to $t$ can touch makes "the shortest walk to $t$" meaningless. Every algorithm in the chapter is an instance of **Ford's generic algorithm**: store a tentative distance $\mathit{dist}(v)$ and a predecessor $\mathit{pred}(v)$ at every vertex, and **relax** any **tense** edge — an edge $u\to v$ with $\mathit{dist}(u) + w(u\to v) < \mathit{dist}(v)$ — until no edge is tense. The instances differ only in which tense edges they relax, and each is the right tool for one class of graphs: **breadth-first search** for unweighted graphs, $O(V+E)$; relaxing in **topological order** for dags with any weights, $O(V+E)$; **Dijkstra's algorithm**, a priority queue, for non-negative weights, $O(E\log V)$; and **Bellman–Ford**, which relaxes every edge $V-1$ times — Bellman's dynamic program over the number of edges — for arbitrary weights, $O(VE)$, detecting negative cycles on the way.

## 8.1 Shortest path trees

> [!definition] Definition — length, distance, SSSP, shortest-path tree
> Let $G = (V,E)$ be a directed graph with **edge weights** $w(u\to v)$, real numbers of any sign. The **length** of a walk $P$ is the sum of its edge weights, $w(P) = \sum_{u\to v\in P} w(u\to v)$, counted with repetition. A **shortest path** from $s$ to $t$ is a walk from $s$ to $t$ of minimum length (§8.2 says when one exists), and its length is the **distance** $\delta(s,t)$, or $\delta(t)$ when the source is fixed; $\delta(t) = \infty$ if $s$ cannot reach $t$.
>
> The **single-source shortest path (SSSP)** problem: given $G$, $w$ and a **source** $s$, find a shortest path from $s$ to **every** vertex. A **shortest-path tree** rooted at $s$ is a tree of directed edges, pointing away from $s$, that contains every vertex $s$ can reach, such that for every such vertex $v$ the tree path from $s$ to $v$ is a shortest path.
>
> (The book writes $\mathit{dist}(v)$ both for the true distance and for the value an algorithm stores; this page writes $\delta(v)$ for the former.)

Almost every algorithm that finds one shortest path, from $s$ to $t$, actually solves (much of) SSSP, and stores the answer as a shortest-path tree: one predecessor pointer per vertex, $O(V)$ space for $V$ paths. That a tree suffices rests on one fact: **every subpath of a shortest path is a shortest path** (if the piece from $x$ to $y$ could be replaced by something shorter, so could the whole path). So if shortest paths are unique, two of them that meet at $x$ share their whole prefix up to $x$, and their union is a tree. If they are not unique, two shortest paths that diverge, meet again at a vertex $d$ and diverge again can be uncrossed: replace the first one's part up to $d$ by the second one's (the book's Figure 8.1), which has the same length.

> [!key] Shortest-path trees exist
> If no negative cycle is reachable from $s$, then $G$ has a shortest-path tree rooted at $s$.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Every vertex $v$ that $s$ can reach has a shortest walk (§8.2), and one with no repeated vertex: removing a cycle, of weight $\ge 0$, does not make it longer. Among the shortest paths to $v\ne s$ choose one with the fewest edges, $h(v)$ of them, and let $\mathit{pred}(v) = u$ be the vertex before $v$ on it. Its prefix is a shortest path to $u$ with $h(v) - 1$ edges. If some shortest path $Q$ to $u$ had fewer edges, then $Q$ followed by $u\to v$ would be a walk of length $\delta(v)$ with fewer than $h(v)$ edges, and it — or, if it repeats a vertex, what is left after deleting its cycles — would be a shortest path to $v$ with fewer than $h(v)$ edges, which is impossible. So $h(\mathit{pred}(v)) = h(v) - 1$, the pointers cannot form a cycle, and they form a tree rooted at $s$. Since $\delta(v) = \delta(\mathit{pred}(v)) + w(\mathit{pred}(v)\to v)$ for every $v$, induction on $h(v)$ shows that the tree path to $v$ has length $\delta(v)$. $\square$

Shortest-path trees and [[concepts/minimum-spanning-trees|minimum spanning trees]] ([[2-algorithms/07-minimum-spanning-trees|Chapter 7]]) are both optimal spanning trees, but very different creatures:

| | shortest-path tree | minimum spanning tree |
|---|---|---|
| natural setting | directed graphs | undirected graphs |
| shape | rooted at $s$, edges directed away from $s$ | unrooted, undirected |
| optimizes | the path from $s$ to **each** vertex separately | the **total** weight of the tree |
| uniqueness | a different tree for every source | unique if the edge weights are distinct |

The book adds that every shortest-path tree may use a different set of edges from the minimum spanning tree. They cannot be disjoint, though, when the weights are distinct and positive: the vertex nearest to $s$ is reached by the lightest edge at $s$, and that edge is in the minimum spanning tree.

> [!trap] Shortest-path tree traps
> - **A shortest-path tree is not a minimum spanning tree**, nor the reverse: in the figure, the path from $s$ to $d$ in the minimum spanning tree has length 14, the distance is 8. Prim's algorithm and Dijkstra's algorithm look alike (both best-first, [[2-algorithms/05-basic-graph-algorithms#56-important-variants|§5.6]]) but use different priorities: $w(u\to v)$ versus $\mathit{dist}(u) + w(u\to v)$.
> - **The tree depends on the source**; a shortest-path tree from $s$ says nothing about distances between other pairs of vertices (that is [[2-algorithms/09-all-pairs-shortest-paths|Chapter 9]]).
> - **Vertices that $s$ cannot reach** are not in the tree; their distance is $\infty$.

## 8.2 Negative edges

When weights are distances or times they are non-negative, but when they are **costs**, a negative weight is a transition that earns a profit — and then **negative cycles** are possible: directed cycles whose total weight is negative.

> [!key] When does a shortest walk exist?
> A shortest walk from $s$ to $t$ exists if and only if $s$ can reach $t$ and **no walk from $s$ to $t$ touches a negative cycle**. If some walk from $s$ to $t$ passes through a vertex of a negative cycle $C$, going once more around $C$ gives a shorter walk, so there is no shortest one; write $\delta(t) = -\infty$.

So for a fixed source there are exactly three cases:

| situation | $\delta(t)$ |
|---|---|
| $s$ cannot reach $t$ | $+\infty$ |
| some walk from $s$ to $t$ touches a negative cycle | $-\infty$: no shortest walk |
| otherwise | finite, and attained by a path (no repeated vertex) with at most $V-1$ edges |

In the last case a shortest walk can be taken to be a path, because removing a cycle (of weight $\ge 0$) never makes a walk longer. That is why everyone, the book included, says "shortest path" while meaning "shortest walk". The distinction matters only with negative cycles: then a shortest **simple** path from $s$ to $t$ still exists (there are finitely many), but computing it is NP-hard, by a reduction from the Hamiltonian path problem. The algorithms of this chapter compute shortest walks, and report negative cycles instead of answers.

**Undirected graphs.** The chapter considers only directed graphs. Every algorithm also works for an undirected graph, by replacing each edge $uv$ with the two edges $u\to v$ and $v\to u$ — **if and only if no edge is negative**. A negative undirected edge $uv$ would become the negative cycle $u\to v\to u$, of weight $2w(uv)$, while an undirected path may use $uv$ only once. Worse, with negative undirected edges subpaths of shortest paths need not be shortest, and the shortest paths from $s$ need not form a tree even when they are unique (right of the figure: $s\,v\,u$ is the shortest path to $u$, but its subpath $s\,v$, of length 1, is not a shortest path to $v$). One shortest path in an undirected graph with negative edges can be computed in $O(VE + V^2\log V)$ time by a reduction to maximum-weight matching, which is beyond the book.

> [!trap] Negative-edge traps
> - **A negative edge is not a negative cycle.** Dags can have negative edges and perfectly good shortest paths (§8.5); so can any graph whose cycles are all non-negative (§8.7).
> - **Only reachable negative cycles matter.** A negative cycle that $s$ cannot reach changes no distance from $s$; one that $s$ can reach but that cannot reach $t$ leaves $\delta(t)$ finite — although Ford's algorithm and Bellman–Ford, which work on all vertices at once, still see it.
> - **Do not "fix" negative weights by adding a constant** $c$ to every edge: a path with $k$ edges grows by $kc$, so paths with fewer edges are favored. With $s\to t$ of weight 1 and $s\to a\to t$ of weights $-2$ and $2$, the shortest path is $s\,a\,t$, of length 0; add 2 to every weight and $s\to t$ (now 3) beats $s\,a\,t$ (now 4).
> - **An undirected negative edge is not two directed edges.**

## 8.3 The only SSSP algorithm

Like graph traversal and minimum spanning trees, almost every SSSP algorithm is an instance of one generic algorithm, proposed by Lester Ford in 1956 (and independently by Dantzig in 1957 and Minty in 1958). Every vertex $v$ stores two values describing a **tentative** shortest path from $s$ to $v$:

- $\mathit{dist}(v)$, the length of the tentative path, or $\infty$ if there is none yet;
- $\mathit{pred}(v)$, the predecessor of $v$ on the tentative path, or Null.

The predecessor pointers play the role of the parent pointers of whatever-first search ([[2-algorithms/05-basic-graph-algorithms#55-whatever-first-search|§5.5]]) and define a tentative shortest-path tree.

```text
InitSSSP(s):
    dist(s) ← 0
    pred(s) ← Null
    for all vertices v ≠ s
        dist(v) ← ∞
        pred(v) ← Null
```

> [!definition] Definition — tense edge, relaxation
> An edge $u\to v$ is **tense** if $\mathit{dist}(u) + w(u\to v) < \mathit{dist}(v)$: the tentative path to $u$ followed by $u\to v$ is shorter than the tentative path to $v$. **Relaxing** a tense edge replaces the second by the first:
> ```text
> Relax(u→v):
>     dist(v) ← dist(u) + w(u→v)
>     pred(v) ← u
> ```
> With the convention $\infty + w = \infty$, an edge out of a vertex with $\mathit{dist}(u) = \infty$ is never tense.

**Ford's generic algorithm** is one line: *repeatedly relax tense edges, until there are no more tense edges.*

```text
FordSSSP(s):
    InitSSSP(s)
    while there is at least one tense edge
        Relax any tense edge
```

If FordSSSP halts, the predecessor pointers form a shortest-path tree and $\mathit{dist}(v) = \delta(v)$ for every $v$ — in particular $\mathit{dist}(v) = \infty$ exactly for the vertices $s$ cannot reach — and it halts if and only if no negative cycle is reachable from $s$. That follows from four claims.

> [!key] Ford's algorithm: the four claims
> 1. At every moment, for every vertex $v$, $\mathit{dist}(v)$ is $\infty$ or the length of some walk from $s$ to $v$. In particular $\mathit{dist}(v) \ge \delta(v)$, and $\mathit{dist}(v)$ never increases.
> 2. If $G$ has no negative cycle, $\mathit{dist}(v)$ is $\infty$ or the length of a **path** from $s$ to $v$; more precisely, if the walk that produced $\mathit{dist}(v)$ contains a cycle, that cycle is negative. Hence, without negative cycles, FordSSSP halts: every relaxation lowers some $\mathit{dist}(v)$, and there are finitely many paths.
> 3. If no edge is tense, $\mathit{dist}(v)$ is the length of the predecessor path $s\cdots\mathit{pred}(\mathit{pred}(v))\,\mathit{pred}(v)\,v$, for every $v$ with $\mathit{dist}(v) < \infty$.
> 4. If no edge is tense, every predecessor path is a shortest path. Hence, if a negative cycle is reachable from $s$, some edge is always tense, and FordSSSP never halts.

> [!proof]- Proofs (click to expand)
> **Claim 1**, by induction on the number of relaxations: Relax$(u\to v)$ appends $u\to v$ to the walk behind $\mathit{dist}(u)$, and it lowers $\mathit{dist}(v)$, so values never increase.
>
> **Claim 2.** Suppose Relax$(u\to v)$ creates a walk that visits $v$ twice: the walk to $u$ passed through $v$ at a time when $\mathit{dist}(v)$ was some value $d_0$, and continued from $v$ to $u$ along a cycle-part of length $c$. The relaxation sets $\mathit{dist}(v)$ to $d_0 + c + w(u\to v)$, which is smaller than the current $\mathit{dist}(v) \le d_0$; so the cycle $v\cdots u\to v$ has weight $c + w(u\to v) < 0$.
>
> **Claim 3.** When Relax sets $\mathit{pred}(v)\leftarrow u$, it makes $\mathit{dist}(v) = \mathit{dist}(u) + w(u\to v)$; later $\mathit{dist}(u)$ can only decrease, so $\mathit{dist}(v) \ge \mathit{dist}(\mathit{pred}(v)) + w(\mathit{pred}(v)\to v)$ at all times, with equality when that edge is not tense. A cycle of predecessor pointers would have negative weight: when the last of its pointers is set, $\mathit{pred}(v)\leftarrow u$, the value $\mathit{dist}(v)$ has just dropped, so the inequality for the cycle edge leaving $v$ is strict, and summing the inequalities around the cycle gives $0 > w(C)$. So without negative cycles the pointers lead back to $s$, and summing the equalities along the predecessor path gives $\mathit{dist}(v)$.
>
> **Claim 4.** With no tense edge, $\mathit{dist}(v_j) \le \mathit{dist}(v_{j-1}) + w(v_{j-1}\to v_j)$ along any walk $s = v_0\to v_1\to\cdots\to v_\ell$, so $\mathit{dist}(v_\ell)$ is at most the length of **every** walk from $s$ to $v_\ell$; by Claims 1 and 3 it is the length of the predecessor path, which is therefore shortest. If a negative cycle were reachable from $s$, the walks to its vertices would be arbitrarily short, which no finite $\mathit{dist}$ value can match — so the algorithm cannot stop. $\square$

Ford's algorithm does not say how to find tense edges or which one to relax first. As with whatever-first search there are several instances, but here **both efficiency and correctness depend on the structure of the graph**, and each of the four common instances is the best choice for one class of graphs:

| instance | which tense edges, in which order | for | time |
|---|---|---|---|
| breadth-first search | out-edges of the vertices in a FIFO queue | unweighted graphs | $O(V+E)$ |
| DagSSSP | the edges into (or out of) each vertex, in topological order | dags, any weights | $O(V+E)$ |
| Dijkstra | out-edges of the vertex with smallest $\mathit{dist}$, by a priority queue | non-negative weights | $O(E\log V)$ |
| Bellman–Ford | all edges, $V-1$ times over | any weights | $O(VE)$ |

> [!trap] Ford's algorithm traps
> - **Ford's algorithm is a framework, not an algorithm to cite:** an arbitrary order of relaxations can take exponentially many steps (§8.6 shows a graph where even Dijkstra's order does). On an exam, cite one of the four instances.
> - **Tense means strictly shorter.** An edge with $\mathit{dist}(u) + w(u\to v) = \mathit{dist}(v)$ is not tense; relaxing it would change nothing but the pointer.
> - **During the algorithm $\mathit{dist}(v)$ is only an upper bound** on $\delta(v)$; proofs must not assume it is correct before the end.

Concept page: [[concepts/shortest-paths]].

## 8.4 Unweighted graphs: breadth-first search

In the simplest case every edge has weight 1, and the length of a path is its number of edges. Then shortest paths are found by **breadth-first search**: whatever-first search with a queue ([[2-algorithms/05-basic-graph-algorithms#56-important-variants|§5.6]]), here written as an instance of Ford's algorithm. (Edward Moore described it in 1957 as the first published method for the shortest path through a maze; Konrad Zuse had already described it in 1945, as a program to count components.) The queue holds vertices whose outgoing edges may be tense:

```text
BFS(s):
    InitSSSP(s)
    Push(s)
    while the queue is not empty
        u ← Pull()
        for all edges u→v
            if dist(v) > dist(u) + 1          ⟨⟨if u→v is tense⟩⟩
                dist(v) ← dist(u) + 1         ⟨⟨relax u→v⟩⟩
                pred(v) ← u
                Push(v)
```

For the analysis, split the run into **phases** with an imaginary token ✠: push it after $s$; whenever it is pulled, push it again; a phase ends when the token is pulled. The algorithm ends when the queue contains only the token. The token changes nothing — the same vertices are pushed and pulled in the same order, with the same results:

```text
BFSWithToken(s):
    InitSSSP(s)
    Push(s)
    Push(✠)                                   ⟨⟨start the first phase⟩⟩
    while the queue contains at least one vertex
        u ← Pull()
        if u = ✠
            Push(✠)                           ⟨⟨start the next phase⟩⟩
        else
            for all edges u→v
                if dist(v) > dist(u) + 1      ⟨⟨if u→v is tense⟩⟩
                    dist(v) ← dist(u) + 1     ⟨⟨relax u→v⟩⟩
                    pred(v) ← u
                    Push(v)
```

So the first phase consists of scanning $s$ alone, and "the end of phase 0" is the moment before the first Pull.

> [!key] Lemma 8.1
> For every integer $i\ge 0$ and every vertex $v$, at the end of the $i$th phase, either $\mathit{dist}(v) = \infty$ or $\mathit{dist}(v)\le i$, and $v$ is in the queue if and only if $\mathit{dist}(v) = i$.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Induction on $i$. At the end of phase 0 the queue is $s$, ✠ and InitSSSP has just set $\mathit{dist}(s) = 0$ and every other $\mathit{dist}(v) = \infty$. For $i > 0$: by the induction hypothesis, phase $i$ starts with the queue holding exactly the vertices $u$ with $\mathit{dist}(u) = i - 1$, followed by the token, and it pulls all of them before the token. Scanning such a $u$ relaxes each tense edge $u\to v$ by setting $\mathit{dist}(v) = i$ and pushes $v$ behind the token; these are the only assignments in phase $i$, so the queue always reads: some labels $i-1$, the token, some labels $i$. When the token comes out, the queue holds exactly the vertices whose label was set to $i$ during the phase — and every vertex with label $i$ got it during phase $i$, since labels $\le i - 1$ never become $i$. $\square$

Labels are therefore assigned in non-decreasing order, and a label never increases, so **each $\mathit{dist}(v)$ is assigned at most once**, in phase $\mathit{dist}(v)$. Consequently each $\mathit{pred}(v)$ changes at most once, each vertex is pushed at most once (phase $\mathit{dist}(v)$) and pulled at most once (phase $\mathit{dist}(v) + 1$), and each edge $u\to v$ is tested at most once (phase $\mathit{dist}(u) + 1$): **breadth-first search runs in $O(V+E)$ time.** The queue is a "wavefront" moving outward from $s$ and passing over every vertex and edge once. It also follows that the test "$\mathit{dist}(v) > \mathit{dist}(u) + 1$" can be replaced by "$\mathit{dist}(v) = \infty$": the distances then act as the marks of whatever-first search, a vertex being marked when its label is finite.

> [!key] Theorem 8.2
> When BFS ends, $\mathit{dist}(v) = \delta(v)$, the number of edges of a shortest path from $s$ to $v$, for every vertex $v$.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Take any path $s = v_0\to v_1\to\cdots\to v_\ell = v$. By induction on $j$, $\mathit{dist}(v_j) \le j$ at the end: $\mathit{dist}(v_0) = 0$, and if $\mathit{dist}(v_{j-1}) \le j-1 < \infty$, then $v_{j-1}$ was pushed and later pulled with that label (labels are assigned once); right after scanning it, $\mathit{dist}(v_j) \le \mathit{dist}(v_{j-1}) + 1 \le j$, and labels never increase. So $\mathit{dist}(v)$ is at most the length of every path, $\mathit{dist}(v) \le \delta(v)$; and it is the length of the predecessor path (Claims 1 and 3 of §8.3), so $\mathit{dist}(v) \ge \delta(v)$. $\square$

> [!trap] Breadth-first search traps
> - **Fewest edges, not least weight.** On a weighted graph BFS answers the wrong question: with $s\to t$ of weight 10 and $s\to a\to t$ of weights 1 and 1, it reports the one-edge path. Use it only when every edge has the same weight.
> - **On an unweighted graph, BFS is the right algorithm**: Dijkstra's algorithm also works but is slower, $O(E\log V)$ instead of $O(V+E)$, and the graph-reduction rubric deducts a point for it.
> - **Depth-first search does not find shortest paths**, weighted or not.

## 8.5 Directed acyclic graphs: depth-first search

Shortest paths are just as easy in a **dag**, even with weights, and even with negative weights — a dag has no cycles, negative or otherwise. It is a standard dynamic program ([[2-algorithms/06-depth-first-search#64-memoization-and-dynamic-programming|§6.4]], [[concepts/dynamic-programming-on-dags]]):

> [!definition] Function — $\delta(v)$
> For every vertex $v$ of the dag $G$, $\delta(v)$ is the length of a shortest path from $s$ to $v$, and $\infty$ if $s$ cannot reach $v$. We need $\delta(v)$ for every $v$.

$$
\delta(v) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } v = s\\ \min\{\delta(u) + w(u\to v) \mid u\to v\in E\} & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad\text{with } \min\varnothing = \infty
$$

- **Why it is correct:** the last edge of a shortest path to $v\ne s$ is some $u\to v$, and the rest is a shortest path to $u$. The identity holds in every graph without negative cycles, but it is a **recurrence** only in a dag: around a cycle $u\to v\to x\to u$, computing $\delta(x)$ needs $\delta(v)$, which needs $\delta(u)$, which needs $\delta(x)$.
- **Memoization:** a field $\mathit{dist}(v)$ in every vertex.
- **Dependencies:** $\delta(v)$ reads $\delta(u)$ for every edge $u\to v$, so the dependency graph is the **reversal** of $G$.
- **Evaluation order:** postorder of a depth-first search of $\textsf{rev}(G)$ — equivalently, a **topological order of $G$** ([[2-algorithms/06-depth-first-search#63-topological-sort|§6.3]]): every vertex after all of its in-neighbors.
- **Time:** $O(1 + \text{in-degree of } v)$ per vertex, $O(V+E)$ in all, plus $O(V+E)$ for the topological sort.

```text
DagSSSP(s):
    for all vertices v in topological order
        if v = s
            dist(v) ← 0
        else
            dist(v) ← ∞
            for all edges u→v
                if dist(v) > dist(u) + w(u→v)       ⟨⟨if u→v is tense⟩⟩
                    dist(v) ← dist(u) + w(u→v)      ⟨⟨relax u→v⟩⟩
```

This dynamic program is another instance of Ford's algorithm. Moving the initialization out of the loop and adding predecessors makes that visible (the two versions compute the same values):

```text
DagSSSP(s):
    InitSSSP(s)
    for all vertices v in topological order
        for all edges u→v
            if u→v is tense
                Relax(u→v)
```

It **pulls** the wavefront forward: each vertex relaxes its *incoming* edges. Relaxing *outgoing* edges instead, as the other algorithms do, **pushes** it and works just as well, also in $O(V+E)$ time:

```text
PushDagSSSP(s):
    InitSSSP(s)
    for all vertices u in topological order
        for all outgoing edges u→v
            if u→v is tense
                Relax(u→v)
```

Its correctness follows from Ford's claims, or directly by induction in topological order: when $u$ is scanned, every edge into $u$ comes from an earlier vertex and has already been relaxed (if tense), so $\mathit{dist}(u) = \delta(u)$ from then on.

> [!example] Example — the book's dag
> In the topological order $s, a, b, c, d, e, f$ of the figure: $\delta(s) = 0$, $\delta(a) = 0 + 3 = 3$, $\delta(b) = \min\{0 + 8,\ 3 + 6\} = 8$, $\delta(c) = \min\{0 + 7,\ 8 - 2\} = 6$, $\delta(d) = \min\{3 + 12,\ 8 + 5\} = 13$, $\delta(e) = \min\{8 + 0,\ 6 + 10,\ 13 + 1\} = 8$ and $\delta(f) = \min\{13 - 3,\ 8 + 1\} = 9$. PushDagSSSP computes the same values in a different order of relaxations: scanning $a$ sets $\mathit{dist}(d) = 15$, and only scanning $b$ lowers it to 13.

How this relates to [[2-algorithms/06-depth-first-search#64-memoization-and-dynamic-programming|§6.4]]: there, $\textsf{LLP}(v)$ is the longest path from $v$ **to** a target $t$, which depends on the out-neighbors of $v$, so it is evaluated in postorder of $G$ itself; here $\delta(v)$ is the shortest path **from** $s$, which depends on the in-neighbors, so it is evaluated in postorder of $\textsf{rev}(G)$, that is, in topological order of $G$. Either direction works for either problem. **Longest paths in a dag** are shortest paths after negating every weight — still a dag, still $O(V+E)$. (In a general graph negating weights turns every positive cycle into a negative one, and longest simple paths are NP-hard.)

> [!trap] Dag traps
> - **The order must be topological.** Preorder of a depth-first search is not (see the [[2-algorithms/06-depth-first-search#63-topological-sort|§6.3]] trap); compute a topological order first, or recurse with memoization.
> - **Check that the graph you built is a dag** before citing DagSSSP — typically, every edge goes from a smaller to a larger index, time step or layer — and say so in the write-up.
> - **On a dag, DagSSSP beats Dijkstra** even with non-negative weights ($O(V+E)$ versus $O(E\log V)$), and Dijkstra may fail with negative weights (§8.6).

## 8.6 Best-first: Dijkstra's algorithm

Replacing the FIFO queue of breadth-first search by a **priority queue**, with the key of each vertex $v$ its tentative distance $\mathit{dist}(v)$, gives the algorithm universally called **Dijkstra's algorithm** — although it was first "published" by Leyzorek and colleagues in a 1957 report, and found independently by Dijkstra (1956, published 1959), Minty, Whiting and Hillier, and, nearly, Dantzig.

```text
Dijkstra(s):
    InitSSSP(s)
    Insert(s, 0)
    while the priority queue is not empty
        u ← ExtractMin()
        for all edges u→v
            if u→v is tense
                Relax(u→v)
                if v is in the priority queue
                    DecreaseKey(v, dist(v))
                else
                    Insert(v, dist(v))
```

At all times, **if an edge $u\to v$ is tense, then $u$ is in the priority queue or is the vertex being scanned**: an edge becomes tense only when $\mathit{dist}(u)$ drops, and then $u$ is inserted (or its key decreased); scanning $u$ leaves none of its edges tense. So when the queue is empty no edge is tense: Dijkstra is an instance of Ford's algorithm, and it **computes shortest paths whenever no negative cycle is reachable from $s$** — negative edges allowed: by Claim 2 each $\mathit{dist}(v)$ can drop only finitely often, so it halts, while with a reachable negative cycle it never does. (The book states the invariant as "if and only if", but only this direction holds: a vertex in the queue may have no tense edges at all.)

### No negative edges

With no negative edges the algorithm behaves like breadth-first search: a wavefront expands from $s$ and passes over the vertices in increasing order of distance. Let $u_i$ be the vertex returned by the $i$th ExtractMin and $d_i$ the value of $\mathit{dist}(u_i)$ just after that extraction; so $u_1 = s$ and $d_1 = 0$. A priori a vertex could be extracted several times.

> [!key] Lemma 8.3
> If $G$ has no negative-weight edges, then $d_i \le d_j$ for all $i < j$.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> It suffices to show $d_{i+1}\ge d_i$. If the edge $u_i\to u_{i+1}$ was relaxed during the $i$th iteration, then afterwards $\mathit{dist}(u_{i+1}) = \mathit{dist}(u_i) + w(u_i\to u_{i+1}) \ge \mathit{dist}(u_i)$. Otherwise $u_{i+1}$ was already in the queue when $u_i$ was extracted, with key $\mathit{dist}(u_{i+1}) \ge \mathit{dist}(u_i)$ because $u_i$ was the minimum, and the $i$th iteration did not change it. $\square$

> [!key] Lemma 8.4
> If $G$ has no negative-weight edges, every vertex is extracted from the priority queue at most once.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Suppose $v$ is extracted in iteration $i$, re-inserted in iteration $j$ and extracted again in iteration $k$, with $i < j < k$. A re-insertion follows a strict decrease of $\mathit{dist}(v)$, and $\mathit{dist}(v)$ never increases, so $d_k < d_i$ — contradicting Lemma 8.3. $\square$

So with non-negative weights every vertex is scanned at most once and every edge relaxed at most once; $\mathit{dist}(v)$ may still change several times — the first change Inserts $v$, later ones DecreaseKey it — but never after $v$ is extracted.

> [!key] Theorem 8.5
> If $G$ has no negative-weight edges, then when Dijkstra ends, $\mathit{dist}(v) = \delta(v)$ for every vertex $v$.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Take any path $s = v_0\to\cdots\to v_\ell = v$ and let $L_j$ be the length of its prefix up to $v_j$. By induction, $\mathit{dist}(v_j)\le L_j$ at the end: $\mathit{dist}(v_0) = 0 = L_0$; if $\mathit{dist}(v_{j-1})\le L_{j-1} < \infty$, then $v_{j-1}$ was inserted and extracted, its value at its (only) extraction is final, and right after it was scanned, $\mathit{dist}(v_j) \le \mathit{dist}(v_{j-1}) + w(v_{j-1}\to v_j) \le L_{j-1} + w(v_{j-1}\to v_j) = L_j$, which stays true because $\mathit{dist}(v_j)$ never increases. So $\mathit{dist}(v)$ is at most the length of every path, and it is the length of the predecessor path. $\square$

**Running time.** With non-negative weights Dijkstra performs at most $V$ Insert and $V$ ExtractMin operations and at most $E$ DecreaseKey operations (one per relaxation), plus $O(V+E)$ other work. The cost depends on the priority queue:

| priority queue | Insert, ExtractMin, DecreaseKey | Dijkstra's algorithm |
|---|---|---|
| binary heap (the standard choice; Johnson 1977) | $O(\log V)$ each | $O(E\log V)$ |
| unsorted array: scan the wavefront for the minimum (Dijkstra's original) | $O(1)$, $O(V)$, $O(1)$ | $O(V^2)$ — faster than the heap when $E = \Omega(V^2)$ |
| Fibonacci heap | $O(1)$, $O(\log V)$, $O(1)$, amortized | $O(E + V\log V)$ |

($O(E\log V)$ assumes, as usual, that $s$ can reach every vertex, so $E\ge V-1$; in general it is $O(V + E\log V)$.) If the graph is known to have no negative edges, the algorithm can be simplified slightly by inserting every vertex at the start; this is the version in most textbooks, on Wikipedia and in Dijkstra's paper, and it is the "best-first search" of [[2-algorithms/05-basic-graph-algorithms#56-important-variants|§5.6]] (which keeps pairs in the queue instead of decreasing keys, $O(E\log E) = O(E\log V)$ time):

```text
NonnegativeDijkstra(s):
    InitSSSP(s)
    for all vertices v
        Insert(v, dist(v))
    while the priority queue is not empty
        u ← ExtractMin()
        for all edges u→v
            if u→v is tense
                Relax(u→v)
                DecreaseKey(v, dist(v))
```

### Negative edges

NonnegativeDijkstra is **wrong** on graphs with negative edges, even without negative cycles: once a vertex has been extracted it is never scanned again, so a later decrease of its distance never reaches its successors.

The book's Dijkstra, which re-inserts such a vertex, stays correct — but the wavefront intuition is gone: a vertex can be extracted many times, an edge relaxed many times, and distances need not be found in increasing order. On the graph of the §8.7 figure (the book's Figure 8.15) it extracts $d$ six times, with $\mathit{dist}(d) = 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, 0$, and relaxes each of the three edges $e\to d$, $f\to e$ and $f\to d$ twice. Worse, the running time can be **exponential**: on the graphs of Shier and Witzgall (the book's Figure 8.14), a chain of $k$ triangles in which the $j$th triangle from the right has edges of weight $0$ and $-2^j$ through its apex and $-2^{j-1}$ along its base, Dijkstra performs $3(2^k - 1) = \Theta(2^{V/2})$ relaxations on $V = 2k+1$ vertices, and more complex graphs force $\Theta(2^V)$. In practice it is usually fast even with negative edges. The book therefore reserves the name "Dijkstra's algorithm" for the re-inserting version — a correct algorithm that is sometimes slow beats a fast one that is sometimes wrong — and notes that NonnegativeDijkstra is no faster on graphs without negative edges.

> [!trap] Dijkstra traps
> - **Dijkstra's $O(E\log V)$ bound and its wavefront proof need non-negative weights.** With negative edges, cite Bellman–Ford ($O(VE)$, §8.7), or DagSSSP if the graph is a dag — never "Dijkstra" without saying which version and what it costs.
> - **Adding a constant to make the weights non-negative changes the answer** (§8.2 trap). Chapter 9's reweighting does it correctly, with a different constant at every vertex.
> - **Prim is not Dijkstra:** the key of $v$ is $\mathit{dist}(u) + w(u\to v)$, not $w(u\to v)$.
> - **One target:** with non-negative weights, the search may stop as soon as $t$ is extracted — its distance is final then.
> - **Unweighted graph, or a dag:** breadth-first search or DagSSSP is faster; using Dijkstra costs a point under the graph-reduction rubric.

## 8.7 Relax all the edges: Bellman–Ford

The simplest instance of Ford's algorithm was sketched by Shimbel in 1954, described by Moore in 1957, and rediscovered by Woodbury and Dantzig (1957), Bellman (1958) and Minty (1958); it is almost universally called **Bellman–Ford**, because Bellman used Ford's formulation. In one line: *relax all the tense edges, then recurse.*

```text
BellmanFord(s):
    InitSSSP(s)
    while there is at least one tense edge
        for every edge u→v
            if u→v is tense
                Relax(u→v)
```

The key to its correctness and its running time is the length of the shortest walk with a bounded number of edges.

> [!definition] Definition — $\mathit{dist}_{\le i}(v)$
> For every vertex $v$ and integer $i\ge 0$, $\mathit{dist}_{\le i}(v)$ is the length of the shortest walk from $s$ to $v$ with **at most $i$ edges** ($\infty$ if there is none). In particular $\mathit{dist}_{\le 0}(s) = 0$ and $\mathit{dist}_{\le 0}(v) = \infty$ for $v\ne s$.

> [!key] Lemma 8.6
> For every vertex $v$ and every integer $i\ge 0$, after $i$ iterations of the main loop of BellmanFord, $\mathit{dist}(v)\le\mathit{dist}_{\le i}(v)$.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> Induction on $i$; $i = 0$ is InitSSSP. For $i > 0$ let $W$ be a shortest walk from $s$ to $v$ with at most $i$ edges. If $W$ has no edges, then $v = s$ and $\mathit{dist}(s)\le 0$ always. Otherwise let $u\to v$ be its last edge; after $i-1$ iterations $\mathit{dist}(u)\le\mathit{dist}_{\le i-1}(u)$, and when iteration $i$ considers $u\to v$, either it is not tense, so $\mathit{dist}(v)\le\mathit{dist}(u) + w(u\to v)$ already, or it is relaxed, with the same result. Either way $\mathit{dist}(v)\le\mathit{dist}_{\le i-1}(u) + w(u\to v) = \mathit{dist}_{\le i}(v)$, and $\mathit{dist}(v)$ never increases afterwards. $\square$

Without negative cycles, the shortest walk to any vertex is a path, with at most $V-1$ edges, so $\mathit{dist}_{\le V-1}(v) = \delta(v)$: by Lemma 8.6 (and $\mathit{dist}(v)\ge\delta(v)$, Claim 1) BellmanFord **halts with the correct distances after at most $V-1$ iterations**. Said the other way round, if some edge is still tense after $V-1$ iterations, the graph has a negative cycle. Hence the concrete version:

```text
BellmanFord(s):
    InitSSSP(s)
    repeat V − 1 times
        for every edge u→v
            if u→v is tense
                Relax(u→v)
    for every edge u→v
        if u→v is tense
            return "Negative cycle!"
```

> [!key] Bellman–Ford
> BellmanFord runs in $O(VE)$ time, for any edge weights. If no negative cycle is reachable from $s$, it ends with $\mathit{dist}(v) = \delta(v)$ for every $v$ and the predecessor pointers form a shortest-path tree; it returns "Negative cycle!" **if and only if** a negative cycle is reachable from $s$.

Each of the $V-1$ iterations takes $O(E)$ time — so Bellman–Ford is always efficient, even with negative edges and even with negative cycles; with non-negative weights Dijkstra is faster in the worst case (and in practice often faster even with negative edges). The "if and only if" holds because a reachable negative cycle $C$ always keeps an edge tense: its vertices have finite labels, and if no edge of $C$ were tense, adding up $\mathit{dist}(v)\le\mathit{dist}(u) + w(u\to v)$ around $C$ would give $0\le w(C) < 0$. A negative cycle that $s$ cannot reach is not reported; to find one anywhere, add a new vertex with an edge of weight 0 to every vertex and run Bellman–Ford from it.

### Moore's improvement

Neither Moore nor Bellman wrote the algorithm in this form. Moore's version ("Algorithm D", in the same 1957 paper as breadth-first search) is breadth-first search with two changes: "$+1$" becomes "$+w(u\to v)$", and a vertex is pushed only if it is **not already in the queue**, so the queue holds at most one copy of each vertex. (Without that check it is still correct, but the $O(VE)$ bound fails.) With the token for the analysis:

```text
Moore(s):
    InitSSSP(s)
    Push(s)
    Push(✠)                                   ⟨⟨start the first phase⟩⟩
    while the queue contains at least one vertex
        u ← Pull()
        if u = ✠
            Push(✠)                           ⟨⟨start the next phase⟩⟩
        else
            for all edges u→v
                if u→v is tense
                    Relax(u→v)
                    if v is not already in the queue
                        Push(v)
```

In each phase every vertex is pulled at most once, so every edge is tested at most once. Every edge that is tense when a phase begins starts at a vertex in the queue (as for Dijkstra, a tense edge's tail is always queued or being scanned), so it is examined during the phase and is not tense right after. Moore is BellmanFord with a queue that keeps track of the vertices whose edges may be tense, instead of testing every edge by brute force:

> [!key] Lemma 8.7
> For every vertex $v$ and every integer $i\ge 0$, after $i$ phases of Moore, $\mathit{dist}(v)\le\mathit{dist}_{\le i}(v)$.

So without negative cycles every distance is final after $V-1$ phases, each costing $O(E)$: **$O(VE)$ time**, and often much less in practice, because an edge $u\to v$ is examined only if $\mathit{dist}(u)$ changed in the previous phase. The vertices pushed during phase $V-1$ are pulled in one more, partial phase, which relaxes nothing; then the queue holds only the token and Moore stops — so it pulls the token at most $V-1$ times. With a reachable negative cycle Moore never halts, so it detects one by counting: **a negative cycle is reachable from $s$ if and only if the token is pulled a $V$th time.** (The book says "if and only if the queue is non-empty right after the $(V-1)$th pull of the token", which is off by one: on the path $s\to v_1\to\cdots\to v_{V-1}$, the token is pulled for the $(V-1)$th time while $v_{V-1}$ is still in the queue.) On the graph of the figure, Moore pulls $s$ ✠ $a\,b\,c$ ✠ $d\,f$ ✠ $d\,e$ ✠ $d$ (the book's Figure 8.17): the token is pulled four times, and the distances of the figure come out.

### Dynamic programming formulation

Bellman derived the algorithm as a dynamic program. The dag recurrence of §8.5 is not a recurrence in a graph with cycles, so he added a parameter that decreases at every recursive call and makes the base case trivial: **the number of edges**.

> [!definition] Function — $\mathit{dist}_{\le i}(v)$
> $\mathit{dist}_{\le i}(v)$ is the length of the shortest walk from $s$ to $v$ with at most $i$ edges ($\infty$ if none), for every vertex $v$ and $0\le i\le V-1$. If $G$ has no negative cycle, we need $\mathit{dist}_{\le V-1}(v)$ for every $v$.

$$
\mathit{dist}_{\le i}(v) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } i = 0 \text{ and } v = s\\ \infty & \text{if } i = 0 \text{ and } v\ne s\\ \min\Bigl\{\mathit{dist}_{\le i-1}(v),\ \min\limits_{u\to v\in E}\bigl(\mathit{dist}_{\le i-1}(u) + w(u\to v)\bigr)\Bigr\} & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}
$$

(Bellman's equation: a shortest walk with at most $i$ edges has at most $i-1$ of them, or it ends with an edge $u\to v$ after a shortest walk to $u$ with at most $i-1$.)

- **Memoization:** a two-dimensional array $\mathit{dist}[0\,..\,V-1, V]$, one row per value of $i$.
- **Evaluation order:** row by row, $i$ increasing in the outer loop; within a row, the vertices in any order.
- **Time:** one row costs $O(V+E)$, so $O(V(V+E))$ in all, which is $O(VE)$ when $s$ can reach every vertex ($E\ge V-1$).

```text
BellmanFordDP(s):
    dist[0, s] ← 0
    for every vertex v ≠ s
        dist[0, v] ← ∞
    for i ← 1 to V − 1
        for every vertex v
            dist[i, v] ← dist[i − 1, v]
            for every edge u→v
                if dist[i, v] > dist[i − 1, u] + w(u→v)
                    dist[i, v] ← dist[i − 1, u] + w(u→v)
```

Three small changes turn it into BellmanFord. First, the order in which row $i$ considers the edges does not matter, so the edge loop can move out of the vertex loop (after copying row $i-1$ into row $i$); it still computes $\mathit{dist}[i,v] = \mathit{dist}_{\le i}(v)$ exactly. Second, reading row $i$ instead of row $i-1$ in the last two lines lets values improve sooner: now $\mathit{dist}[i,v]\le\mathit{dist}_{\le i}(v)$, mirroring Lemmas 8.6 and 8.7, and the final row is still correct. Third, row $i$ then starts as a copy of row $i-1$ and only decreases, so the index $i$ is redundant and one array suffices:

```text
BellmanFordFinal(s):
    dist[s] ← 0
    for every vertex v ≠ s
        dist[v] ← ∞
    for i ← 1 to V − 1
        for every edge u→v
            if dist[v] > dist[u] + w(u→v)
                dist[v] ← dist[u] + w(u→v)
```

This is BellmanFord without predecessor pointers and without the negative-cycle test, both easy to add. Seen as a graph, the table $\mathit{dist}[i,v]$ is a **layered dag**: one copy $(v,i)$ of every vertex per value of $i$, an edge $(u, i-1)\to(v, i)$ of weight $w(u\to v)$ for every edge of $G$, and an edge $(v, i-1)\to(v, i)$ of weight 0. $\mathit{dist}_{\le i}(v)$ is the distance from $(s,0)$ to $(v,i)$ in that dag, and BellmanFordDP is DagSSSP on it, layer by layer — the idea behind the last reduction below.

> [!trap] Bellman–Ford traps
> - **$V-1$ rounds, then one test pass.** Looping "until nothing changes" never stops on a reachable negative cycle; an edge still tense after $V-1$ rounds is the evidence of one.
> - **It detects negative cycles reachable from $s$**, not all of them; add a new source with 0-weight edges to every vertex to detect any.
> - **$O(VE)$ is the bound to quote**, not $O(V^2)$ or $O(E\log V)$; it is the slowest of the four instances, so use it only when weights may be negative and the graph may have cycles.
> - **The dynamic program's subproblem must include the number of edges** $i$; "$\mathit{dist}(v)$ = shortest path to $v$" alone has no valid evaluation order in a graph with cycles.

## Which algorithm when

The first row that applies is the fastest correct choice; the rubric deducts a point for a slower one.

| the graph (after any reduction) | algorithm | time | section |
|---|---|---|---|
| unweighted, or all weights equal | breadth-first search | $O(V+E)$ | 8.4 |
| a dag, any weights | DagSSSP: relax in topological order | $O(V+E)$ | 8.5 |
| non-negative weights | Dijkstra, binary heap | $O(E\log V)$ | 8.6 |
| any weights, no negative cycle reachable from $s$ | Bellman–Ford (or Moore) | $O(VE)$ | 8.7 |
| is a negative cycle reachable from $s$? | Bellman–Ford: is an edge tense after $V-1$ rounds? | $O(VE)$ | 8.7 |
| shortest walk with at most $k$ edges, any weights | DagSSSP in a layered dag ($k$ rounds of BellmanFordDP) | $O(k(V+E))$ | below |
| shortest simple path despite negative cycles; longest path in a general graph | none known: NP-hard | — | 8.2 |

Undirected graphs with non-negative weights: replace every edge by two directed edges, then use the same table. Distances between **all** pairs of vertices: [[2-algorithms/09-all-pairs-shortest-paths|Chapter 9]].

## Shortest paths by graph reduction

On exams, shortest-path algorithms are rarely the question; they are the black box at the end of a **graph reduction**. Build a graph from the input, say which shortest-path problem on it answers the question, cite the fastest algorithm from the table above, and analyze the time in the input's parameters — the four parts of the [[0-toolkit/02-proof-templates#22-solving-a-problem-by-graph-reduction|graph-reduction template]] ([[2-algorithms/05-basic-graph-algorithms#57-graph-reductions-flood-fill|§5.7]], [[concepts/graph-reductions]]). The most useful construction is **layering**: when the rules depend on something besides the current vertex — jumps used, a coupon spent, edges taken so far — make one copy of the graph per value of that state, and let the edges move between copies.

> [!example] Example — fewest moves with at most $k$ jumps (breadth-first search in a layered graph)
> **Problem.** An $n\times n$ grid has free and blocked cells. A robot on a free cell may step to a horizontally or vertically adjacent free cell, or — at most $k$ times in all — **jump** two cells in a straight line over a blocked cell onto a free cell. Steps and jumps each count as one move. Find the fewest moves from the free cell $s$ to the free cell $t$.
>
> **The graph.** Let $H$ be the **directed** graph whose vertices are the triples $(i, j, c)$ with $(i,j)$ a free cell and $0\le c\le k$, where $c$ counts the jumps used so far. For every free cell $(i',j')$ adjacent to $(i,j)$ there is an edge $(i,j,c)\to(i',j',c)$; for every free cell $(i'', j'')$ two cells away from $(i,j)$ in one of the four directions, with a blocked cell in between, and every $c < k$, there is an edge $(i,j,c)\to(i'',j'',c+1)$. No weights.
>
> **The problem on the graph.** Move sequences from $s$ that use at most $k$ jumps correspond exactly to walks in $H$ from $(s,0)$ — the third coordinate counts the jumps, and no edge leads past $c = k$ — and the number of moves is the number of edges. So the answer is the **shortest-path distance from $(s,0)$ to the nearest of $(t,0), (t,1), \ldots, (t,k)$** in $H$, and "impossible" if none of them is reachable.
>
> **The algorithm.** One **breadth-first search** of $H$ from $(s,0)$, then the minimum of the $k+1$ distances to the vertices $(t,c)$. ($H$ is unweighted: Dijkstra would be slower than necessary.)
>
> **The time.** $H$ has at most $(k+1)n^2$ vertices and, with at most 4 steps and 4 jumps out of each, at most $8(k+1)n^2$ edges; it is built in $O(kn^2)$ time, and breadth-first search takes $O(V+E) = O(kn^2)$ time.

> [!example] Example — the cheapest route with one free road (Dijkstra in two layers)
> **Problem.** A directed road network $G = (V,E)$ has a non-negative toll $\ell(u\to v)$ on every road, and you hold one coupon that makes one road of your choice free. Find the cheapest route from $s$ to $t$.
>
> **The graph.** Let $H$ be the **directed** graph with vertices $(v, b)$ for $v\in V$ and $b\in\{0,1\}$ ($b = 1$: the coupon is spent), and for every road $u\to v$ three edges: $(u,0)\to(v,0)$ and $(u,1)\to(v,1)$ of weight $\ell(u\to v)$, and $(u,0)\to(v,1)$ of weight 0 (drive $u\to v$ with the coupon).
>
> **The problem on the graph.** Routes from $s$ with the coupon used on at most one road correspond to walks in $H$ from $(s,0)$, at the same cost. The answer is $\min\{\delta_H((s,0),(t,0)),\ \delta_H((s,0),(t,1))\}$, the **shortest-path distance** from $(s,0)$ to the closer of the two copies of $t$.
>
> **The algorithm.** **Dijkstra's algorithm** in $H$ from $(s,0)$: every weight is non-negative, and $H$ may have cycles.
>
> **The time.** $H$ has $2V$ vertices and $3E$ edges, so building it takes $O(V+E)$ time and Dijkstra $O(E\log V)$.

> [!example] Example — the shortest walk with at most $k$ edges, any weights (a layered dag)
> **Problem.** Given a directed graph $G = (V,E)$ with arbitrary edge weights — negative cycles allowed — two vertices $s$ and $t$ and an integer $k\ge 0$, find the length of a shortest walk from $s$ to $t$ with at most $k$ edges.
>
> **The graph.** Let $H$ be the **directed** graph with vertices $(v, i)$ for $v\in V$ and $0\le i\le k$, and an edge $(u, i-1)\to(v, i)$ of weight $w(u\to v)$ for every edge $u\to v$ of $G$ and every $1\le i\le k$. Every edge goes from layer $i-1$ to layer $i$, so $H$ is a **dag**: ordering the vertices by layer is a topological order.
>
> **The problem on the graph.** Walks in $G$ from $s$ with exactly $i$ edges correspond to paths in $H$ from $(s,0)$ to layer $i$, with the same length. The answer is the **shortest-path distance from $(s,0)$ to the nearest of $(t,0), \ldots, (t,k)$** in $H$ ($\infty$: no walk with at most $k$ edges).
>
> **The algorithm.** **DagSSSP** in $H$ from $(s,0)$, in layer order. (Negative weights are fine in a dag; Dijkstra could be wrong, and Bellman–Ford would be slower.)
>
> **The time.** $H$ has $(k+1)V$ vertices and $kE$ edges: $O(k(V+E))$ time.

With $k = V-1$ and the extra edges $(v,i-1)\to(v,i)$ of weight 0 this is exactly Bellman's dynamic program — Bellman–Ford is DagSSSP in a layered dag.

> [!trap] Where shortest-path reductions lose points
> - **The wrong black box:** Dijkstra on an unweighted graph or a dag (slower than necessary, −1), breadth-first search on a weighted graph (wrong), Dijkstra on a graph that can have negative weights (wrong). Look at the weights **of the graph you built**: a "reward" edge is negative.
> - **A set of targets.** In a layered graph the answer is the minimum over all copies of $t$ — or add one new target vertex with weight-0 edges from every copy.
> - **Modifying Dijkstra** to carry the extra state, instead of putting the state into the vertices: the rubric gives full credit only to a clearly correct modification, partial credit sparingly.
> - **Time in the input's parameters:** $k+1$ layers multiply both $V$ and $E$; "$O(E\log V)$" alone is half an answer.
> - **Undirected input, directed graph:** layers and jumps make $H$ directed even when the input graph is undirected — say "directed".

## Summary

- SSSP: shortest paths from $s$ to every vertex, stored as a shortest-path tree (one $\mathit{pred}$ pointer per vertex); it exists because subpaths of shortest paths are shortest. It is not a minimum spanning tree.
- A shortest walk from $s$ to $t$ exists if and only if $s$ reaches $t$ and no $s$–$t$ walk touches a negative cycle; without negative cycles it can be taken to be a path with at most $V-1$ edges. Undirected negative edges are a different, harder problem.
- Ford's generic algorithm: $\mathit{dist}(v)$, $\mathit{pred}(v)$, relax tense edges ($\mathit{dist}(u) + w(u\to v) < \mathit{dist}(v)$) until none is left. $\mathit{dist}(v)$ is always the length of a walk, never increases, and is correct when no edge is tense; it never halts on a reachable negative cycle.
- Breadth-first search (unweighted): phases = distances (Lemma 8.1), each vertex pushed and pulled once, $O(V+E)$.
- Dags, any weights: $\delta(v) = \min_{u\to v}\{\delta(u) + w(u\to v)\}$ in topological order (DagSSSP, pull or push), $O(V+E)$; longest paths in dags by negating weights.
- Dijkstra (priority queue keyed by $\mathit{dist}$): with non-negative weights the extracted keys never decrease, each vertex is extracted once, $O(E\log V)$ with a binary heap ($O(V^2)$ with an array, $O(E + V\log V)$ with a Fibonacci heap). NonnegativeDijkstra fails with negative edges; the re-inserting version is correct but can be exponential.
- Bellman–Ford: $V-1$ rounds of relaxing every edge, $O(VE)$, any weights; after $i$ rounds $\mathit{dist}(v)\le\mathit{dist}_{\le i}(v)$; a tense edge afterwards means a reachable negative cycle. It is Bellman's dynamic program over the number of edges — DagSSSP in a layered dag; Moore's queue version is often faster.
- Choose by the graph: unweighted → BFS; dag → DagSSSP; non-negative → Dijkstra; otherwise Bellman–Ford. Extra state goes into the vertices (layered graphs).
