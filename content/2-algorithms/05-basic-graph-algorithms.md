> [!abstract] In one breath
> A **graph** is a set of pairs of things: vertices $V$ and edges $E$, unordered pairs $uv$ (undirected) or ordered pairs $u\to v$ (directed), and by the book's convention $V$ and $E$ also stand for their numbers. Unless a problem says otherwise, a graph is stored as a **standard adjacency list**: $\Theta(V+E)$ space, and the neighbors of $v$ are listed in $O(1+\deg(v))$ time. The basic global question is **reachability**, and one generic algorithm answers it: **whatever-first search** keeps candidate edges in a "bag", marks a vertex the first time it comes out, and marks exactly the vertices reachable from the start $s$, while the parent edges form a spanning tree of them — in $O(V + ET)$ time when a bag operation costs $T$. A stack gives **depth-first search**, a queue **breadth-first search** (whose tree contains shortest paths by number of edges), both in $O(V+E)$; a priority queue gives **best-first search** — minimum spanning trees, shortest paths, widest paths — in $O(V + E\log E)$. Wrapped in a loop over all vertices the same search counts and labels components in $O(V+E)$, and the same code works for directed graphs. Most graph problems are solved by **reduction**: build a graph from the input, name the standard problem on it, call the standard algorithm as a black box, and analyze the result in the input's own parameters — flood fill is reachability among same-colored neighboring pixels.

## 5.1 Introduction and history

A graph is a collection of pairs — of cities, people, web pages, game positions, recursive subproblems. The paired objects are **vertices** (or nodes) and the pairs **edges** (or arcs), but they can be anything at all. The book's tour of the history, in brief:

- **Road maps.** The Tabula Peutingeriana, a medieval copy of a Roman road map, is an abstract network like a subway map — enough to find shortest routes between Roman cities.
- **Family trees**, used for centuries to decide which marriages canon law allowed: the rules measured distances to the nearest common ancestor.
- **Graphical statics** (Varignon, published 1725) and its reciprocal force diagrams; Euler's partial solution of the **Königsberg bridges** puzzle (1735) — he proved that a graph with an Euler tour has only even degrees; the converse waited for Hierholzer (1873).
- **Words.** "Tree" for connected acyclic graphs is Cayley's (1857), "graph" Sylvester's (1878); Kőnig wrote the first graph theory book (1936).

## 5.2 Basic definitions

> [!definition] Definition — graph
> A (simple) **graph** $G = (V, E)$ is a nonempty finite set $V$ of **vertices** with a set $E$ of pairs of vertices, the **edges**. In an **undirected** graph the edges are unordered pairs, written $uv$ (so $uv = vu$); in a **directed** graph they are ordered pairs, written $u\to v$, from the **tail** $u$ to the **head** $v$; both $u\to v$ and $v\to u$ may be present. The definition rules out parallel edges and undirected loops; graphs that have them are **multigraphs**, and most algorithms extend to them unchanged.
>
> Following the book, **$V$ and $E$ also denote the numbers** of vertices and edges, so $O(V+E)$ means $O(\lvert V\rvert + \lvert E\rvert)$, and
> $$
> 0\le E\le \binom{V}{2} = \frac{V(V-1)}{2}\ \text{(undirected)},\qquad 0\le E\le V(V-1)\ \text{(directed)}.
> $$
> The directed bound assumes no loops $u\to u$; with loops it is $V^2$.

The vocabulary, side by side:

| | undirected | directed |
|---|---|---|
| neighbors | $u$ and $v$ are **adjacent** (neighbors) if $uv\in E$ | $u$ is a **predecessor** of $v$ and $v$ a **successor** of $u$ if $u\to v\in E$ |
| degree | $\deg(v)$ = number of neighbors | **in-degree** = number of predecessors, **out-degree** = number of successors |
| walk | sequence of vertices, consecutive ones adjacent | **directed walk** $v_0\to v_1\to\cdots\to v_\ell$ along edges |
| path | a walk that visits each vertex at most once | a directed walk that visits each vertex at most once |
| cycle | a closed walk (same first and last vertex) that enters and leaves each vertex at most once, on at least three vertices | a closed directed walk that enters and leaves each vertex at most once |
| reachable | $v$ from $u$: some walk (hence some path) joins them | $v$ from $u$: some directed walk (hence path) from $u$ to $v$ |
| everything reachable | **connected** | **strongly connected** (from every vertex to every other) |
| no cycle | **acyclic** = a **forest** | acyclic = a **dag** (directed acyclic graph) |

> [!definition] Definitions — subgraphs, components, trees
> - $G' = (V', E')$ is a **subgraph** of $G = (V,E)$ if $V'\subseteq V$ and $E'\subseteq E$; a **proper** subgraph is any subgraph other than $G$.
> - The **components** of an undirected graph are its maximal connected subgraphs; $u$ and $v$ lie in the same component if and only if a path joins them. (The book says "components", not "connected components": components are connected by definition.)
> - A **tree** is a connected acyclic graph — equivalently, one component of a forest. A **spanning tree** of $G$ is a subgraph that is a tree and contains every vertex of $G$; it exists if and only if $G$ is connected. A **spanning forest** is a spanning tree of each component.

> [!key] Facts used throughout
> - **Handshake lemma.** $\sum_{v}\deg(v) = 2E$ in an undirected graph, because each edge is counted at both endpoints; in a directed graph, the in-degrees and the out-degrees each sum to $E$.
> - A tree with $k$ vertices has exactly $k-1$ edges, and a connected graph with $k$ vertices and $k-1$ edges is a tree. (Exercise 5.1 lists seven equivalent definitions of a tree.)

> [!trap] Vocabulary traps
> - A **path** never repeats a vertex; a **walk** may. For reachability this makes no difference — a shortest walk is a path — but "longest walk" and "longest path" are very different problems.
> - "Connected" is for undirected graphs. A directed graph is **strongly connected** or not, and its strong components come in [[2-algorithms/06-depth-first-search|Chapter 6]].
> - $V$ and $E$ are sets *and* numbers. $O(E)$ is not $O(V+E)$: isolated vertices make $E < V$ possible (a graph with no edges has $E = 0$).

## 5.3 Representations and examples

The most familiar representation is a **drawing**: a point per vertex and a curve per edge. A graph is **planar** if it has a drawing without crossings, an **embedding**. A drawing is not the graph: the same planar graph can be drawn with crossings, and the book's running example — 13 vertices, 19 edges and two components — is drawn both ways in its Figure 5.4. Other representations the book uses:

- **Intersection graphs**: a vertex for each geometric object, an edge for each intersecting pair. Intervals on the real line give **interval graphs**.
- **Dependency graphs** of recurrences: a vertex for each distinct subproblem, an edge from one subproblem to another if evaluating the second needs the first. They are always dags. The Fibonacci recurrence has vertices $0, 1, \ldots, n$ and edges $(i-1)\to i$ and $(i-2)\to i$ for $2\le i\le n$. Edit distance ([[2-algorithms/03-dynamic-programming#37-edit-distance|§3.7]]) has a grid of $(m+1)(n+1)$ subproblems $(i,j)$, $0\le i\le m$, $0\le j\le n$, in which every non-base subproblem has the three incoming edges $(i-1,j)\to(i,j)$, $(i,j-1)\to(i,j)$ and $(i-1,j-1)\to(i,j)$: $3mn$ edges. A dynamic programming evaluation order is an order in which every subproblem comes after its predecessors ([[2-algorithms/03-dynamic-programming#34-the-pattern-smart-recursion|§3.4]]). With the edges pointing this way — from a subproblem to the ones that need it — that is a **topological order**; Chapter 6 points every edge the other way, from a subproblem to the ones it needs, and so calls the same order a *reverse* topological order.
- **Configuration graphs** of games, puzzles and machines: a vertex for each configuration, an edge for each legal move. They are **memoized game trees** ([[2-algorithms/02-backtracking#22-game-trees|§2.2]]): each configuration appears once instead of many times. The 4-disk Tower of Hanoi has $3^4 = 81$ configurations and 120 edges, and a shortest path between two "all disks on one peg" configurations has $2^4 - 1 = 15$ edges — the optimal solution of [[2-algorithms/01-recursion#13-tower-of-hanoi|§1.3]], which breadth-first search finds mechanically.
- **Automata.** A DFA $M = (\Sigma, Q, s, A, \delta)$ is the directed graph $G_M$ with vertices $Q$ and edges $q\to\delta(q,a)$ for all $q\in Q$ and $a\in\Sigma$. Questions about $L(M)$ become graph questions: $L(M) = \varnothing$ if and only if no accepting state is reachable from $s$ in $G_M$ — one search, $O(\lvert Q\rvert\cdot\lvert\Sigma\rvert)$ time ([[concepts/dfa]]).
- **Implicit graphs.** The subset construction ([[concepts/subset-construction]]) applies to any directed graph $G = (V,E)$: the graph $G' = (2^V, E')$ with $E' = \{A\to B \mid u\to v\in E \text{ for some } u\in A \text{ and } v\in B\}$. Nobody builds $G'$ — it has $2^V$ vertices — because $G$ already represents it implicitly.

Whatever the picture, the formal object is the same: **a graph is a set of pairs of things**.

## 5.4 Data structures

Both standard data structures are arrays indexed by vertices, so they assume the vertices are numbered $1$ to $V$ — in a formal sense, those integers *are* the vertices.

### Adjacency lists

An **adjacency list** is an array of lists: the list of $v$ holds the neighbors of $v$ (the out-neighbors, if $G$ is directed). An undirected edge $uv$ is stored twice, in the lists of $u$ and of $v$; a directed edge $u\to v$ once, in the list of its tail $u$. The space is $\Theta(V+E)$. With linked lists — the **standard adjacency list** — listing the neighbors of $v$ takes $O(1+\deg(v))$ time, deciding whether $u\to v$ is an edge takes $O(1+\deg(u))$, and deciding whether $uv$ is an edge takes $O(1+\min\{\deg(u),\deg(v)\})$ by scanning both lists in parallel until the edge turns up or one list ends. Faster membership tests: a balanced search tree per vertex ($O(1+\log\deg(u))$), or a hash table ($O(1)$, but only *expected* time — a subtle matter).

An **adjacency array** stores all the lists in one array, each vertex's neighbors in a contiguous, sorted interval, plus an array of the first index of each interval; binary search in $u$'s interval then decides whether $uv$ is an edge in $O(\log\deg(u))$ time. For the example graph above (records numbered from 1):

| vertex | $a$ | $b$ | $c$ | $d$ | $e$ | $f$ | $g$ | $h$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| first | 1 | 3 | 6 | 9 | 12 | 14 | 15 | 16 |
| records | b c | a c d | a b d | b c e | d f | e | h | g |

### Adjacency matrices

The **adjacency matrix** is a $V\times V$ array of bits $A[1\,..\,V, 1\,..\,V]$ with $A[u,v] = 1$ if and only if $uv\in E$ (undirected) or $u\to v\in E$ (directed). For an undirected graph it is symmetric with zeros on the diagonal; for a directed graph it need not be symmetric, and a 1 on the diagonal is a loop. It decides adjacency in $\Theta(1)$ time, but listing the neighbors of $v$ means scanning a whole row, $\Theta(V)$ time even when $v$ has one neighbor, and it always takes $\Theta(V^2)$ space.

### Comparison

The book's Table 5.1 (the starred bounds are expected amortized times for dynamic hash tables):

| | standard adjacency list (linked lists) | fast adjacency list (hash tables) | adjacency matrix |
|---|---|---|---|
| space | $\Theta(V+E)$ | $\Theta(V+E)$ | $\Theta(V^2)$ |
| test $uv\in E$ | $O(1+\min\{\deg(u),\deg(v)\}) = O(V)$ | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ |
| test $u\to v\in E$ | $O(1+\deg(u)) = O(V)$ | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ |
| list the (out-)neighbors of $v$ | $\Theta(1+\deg(v)) = O(V)$ | $\Theta(1+\deg(v)) = O(V)$ | $\Theta(V)$ |
| list all edges | $\Theta(V+E)$ | $\Theta(V+E)$ | $\Theta(V^2)$ |
| insert edge $uv$ | $O(1)$ | $O(1)^*$ | $O(1)$ |
| delete edge $uv$ | $O(\deg(u)+\deg(v)) = O(V)$ | $O(1)^*$ | $O(1)$ |

Why not always use hash tables? For dense graphs a matrix is simpler and faster in practice — one contiguous block of memory, no pointers, no hashing. And most graph algorithms never ask "is $uv$ an edge?"; they only list neighbors. The strongest reason is that many graphs come **implicitly** in exactly these two forms. An intersection graph, given as a list of objects with a constant-time intersection test, behaves like an adjacency matrix. A pointer-based data structure, or a configuration graph whose moves out of a configuration can be listed in $O(1)$ time each, behaves like a standard adjacency list — and there is no quick way to ask whether one particular record points to another, or one particular configuration is a move away.

> [!key] The default representation
> Unless explicitly stated otherwise, the book — and the course — assume that every input graph is given as a **standard adjacency list**, and every time bound assumes it. A loop over all vertices and all their neighbors costs $O(V+E)$.

## 5.5 Whatever-first search

**Reachability**: given a graph $G$ and a vertex $s$, which vertices are reachable from $s$? In an undirected graph, exactly the vertices of $s$'s component. The most natural algorithm is **depth-first search**, recursive or iterative — the same algorithm, except that the iterative version shows its stack:

```text
RecursiveDFS(v):
    if v is unmarked
        mark v
        for each edge vw
            RecursiveDFS(w)

IterativeDFS(s):
    Push(s)
    while the stack is not empty
        v ← Pop
        if v is unmarked
            mark v
            for each edge vw
                Push(w)
```

(Since the last neighbor pushed is the first popped, IterativeDFS tries $v$'s neighbors in the reverse of their list order; pushing each list in reverse makes it mark the vertices in exactly RecursiveDFS's order.) Depth-first search is one species of a family the book calls **whatever-first search**: keep candidates in a **bag** — any data structure that lets you put things in and take things out — and take them out in whatever order the bag likes. To prove the algorithm correct, each item is a pair $(p, v)$: the vertex $v$ and the already-marked vertex $p$ that put it there, which becomes the **parent** of $v$ when $v$ is first marked.

```text
WhateverFirstSearch(s):
    put (∅, s) into the bag
    while the bag is not empty
        take (p, v) from the bag              (⋆)
        if v is unmarked
            mark v
            parent(v) ← p
            for each edge vw                  (†)
                put (v, w) into the bag       (⋆⋆)
```

> [!key] Lemma 5.1
> WhateverFirstSearch$(s)$ marks every vertex reachable from $s$, and only those vertices. Moreover, the parent edges $v\,\mathit{parent}(v)$, over all marked $v\ne s$, form a spanning tree of the component containing $s$.

> [!proof]- Proof (click to expand)
> **Every reachable vertex is marked**, by induction on the number of edges of a shortest path from $s$. The first iteration marks $s$. If $v\ne s$ is reachable, take a shortest path $s\cdots u\,v$. Its prefix $s\cdots u$ is a path to $u$ with one edge fewer, so $u$ is closer to $s$ than $v$, and by the induction hypothesis $u$ is marked. When $u$ was marked, the pair $(u,v)$ went into the bag; the loop runs until the bag is empty, so $(u,v)$ is taken out later, and then $v$ is marked, unless it already was.
>
> **Every marked vertex is reachable, and the parent edges form a tree.** Each pair $(v, \mathit{parent}(v))$ with $v\ne s$ is an edge of $G$: $\mathit{parent}(v)$ put $(\mathit{parent}(v), v)$ into the bag while scanning its own edges. By induction on the order in which vertices are marked, following parent edges from any marked $v$ leads back to $s$: $\mathit{parent}(v)$ was marked before $v$, so its parent edges lead to $s$, and one more edge leads from $v$ to $\mathit{parent}(v)$. Hence every marked vertex is reachable from $s$, and the parent edges connect all $k$ marked vertices. There are exactly $k-1$ of them — one per marked vertex other than $s$ — and a connected graph with $k$ vertices and $k-1$ edges is a tree. $\square$
>
> (The book words two steps differently: it says the prefix $s\cdots u$ "is shorter than the shortest path from $s$ to $u$" — it *is* a shortest path to $u$, shorter than the one to $v$ — and it closes the second induction with the edge $s\to\mathit{parent}(s)$ where $v\to\mathit{parent}(v)$ is meant.)

### Analysis

Let $T$ be the time to put one item into the bag or take one out.

- **Line (†)** runs once for each marked vertex: at most $V$ times. Scanning the adjacency lists costs $O(\sum_v (1+\deg(v))) = O(V+E)$ in all.
- **Line (⋆⋆)** runs once per edge per marked endpoint. Each edge $uv$ of $s$'s component goes in twice, as $(u,v)$ and as $(v,u)$: at most $2E$ insertions. Seen from the other side, a vertex $w$ enters the bag once for **each** marked neighbor — up to $\deg(w)$ times (once more if $w = s$) — so the bag can hold many copies of the same vertex, which is why the test "if $v$ is unmarked" comes *after* taking $v$ out.
- **Line (⋆)** cannot take out more than went in: at most $2E+1$ times.

So with a standard adjacency list WhateverFirstSearch runs in $O(V + ET)$ time; with an adjacency matrix, finding the neighbors of a marked vertex costs $\Theta(V)$, and the time grows to $O(V^2 + ET)$. For a directed graph (below) a pair $(v,w)$ goes in once per edge $v\to w$: at most $E+1$ insertions.

> [!example] Example — the example graph $G$, from $a$
> With neighbor lists in alphabetical order and a **stack**, the search takes out `(∅,a), (a,c), (c,d), (d,e), (e,f)`, marking $a, c, d, e, f$; then the stale pairs `(f,e), (e,d), (d,c)`; then `(d,b)`, marking $b$ with parent $d$; and finally six more stale pairs `(b,d), (b,c), (b,a), (c,b), (c,a), (a,b)`. That is $15 = 2\cdot 7 + 1$ pairs for the 7 edges of $a$'s component; $b$ entered the bag $3 = \deg(b)$ times, as $(a,b)$, $(c,b)$ and $(d,b)$, and the last one in was the first out. The depth-first tree has edges $ac, cd, de, ef, db$. With a **queue** the search marks $a, b, c, d, e, f$ in that order, with parents $a, a, b, d, e$; the breadth-first tree $ab, ac, bd, de, ef$ reaches $b, c, d, e, f$ by 1, 1, 2, 3 and 4 edges, their distances from $a$. Neither search ever touches $g$ or $h$.

> [!trap] Whatever-first search traps
> - **Testing the mark when putting in instead of when taking out** gives a different algorithm (the book's EagerWFS, Exercise 5.9): it is still breadth-first search with a queue, but with a stack it is not depth-first search. On a triangle $abc$ searched from $a$, EagerWFS always makes $a$ the parent of both $b$ and $c$ (tree $ab, ac$), while every depth-first tree from $a$ gives $a$ a single child (tree $ab, bc$ or $ac, cb$).
> - **Dropping the test "if $v$ is unmarked"** makes the loop run forever in an undirected graph as soon as $s$ has an edge: $u$ puts $v$ in, $v$ puts $u$ back.
> - **The bag is not bounded by $V$.** It can hold up to $2E+1$ pairs; the bound $O(V+E)$ already accounts for that.

## 5.6 Important variants

What the search computes depends only on the bag:

| bag | takes out | search | time | the parent tree |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| stack | the newest pair | depth-first | $O(V+E)$ | depth-first spanning tree: long and skinny |
| queue | the oldest pair | breadth-first | $O(V+E)$ | breadth-first spanning tree: short and bushy, made of shortest paths from $s$ |
| priority queue | the best priority | best-first | $O(V + E\log E)$ | depends on the priorities (below) |
| anything | anything | whatever-first | $O(V + ET)$ | some spanning tree of $s$'s component |

### Stack: depth-first

Push and pop take $O(1)$ time, so **depth-first search** runs in $O(V+E)$ time; its parent tree is a **depth-first spanning tree**. Its many uses — pre- and postorder, cycles, topological sort, strong components — are [[2-algorithms/06-depth-first-search|Chapter 6]] ([[concepts/depth-first-search]]).

### Queue: breadth-first

Enqueue and dequeue take $O(1)$ time, so **breadth-first search** runs in $O(V+E)$ time too. Its **breadth-first spanning tree** contains a **shortest path** — fewest edges — from $s$ to every vertex of its component; the book proves it with shortest paths in Chapter 8.

> [!intuition] Why the queue finds shortest paths
> The pairs come out of a queue in the order they went in. All pairs put in by vertices at distance $d$ from $s$ enter before any pair put in by a vertex at distance $d+1$, so breadth-first search marks vertices in nondecreasing order of distance, and a vertex at distance $d+1$ is first taken out in a pair from a vertex at distance $d$ — its parent. Following parents from $v$ therefore takes exactly $\mathit{dist}(s,v)$ edges.

### Priority queue: best-first

With a **priority queue**, each pair gets a priority when it is put in, and the search always takes out the pair of best priority: **best-first search**. The queue never holds more than $2E+1$ pairs (the book says one copy of each edge; an undirected edge can be in it once in each direction), so each operation costs $O(\log E)$ and the search runs in $O(V + E\log E)$ time. It is a family of algorithms, one for each way to assign priorities. With non-negative edge weights $w(uv)$ or $w(u\to v)$, the book's three examples, for a pair $(u,v)$ put in by $u$:

| variant | priority of $(u,v)$ | take out | on marking $v$ from $(u,v)$ | computes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| "Prim" | $w(uv)$ | smallest | — | minimum spanning tree |
| "Dijkstra" | $\mathit{dist}(u) + w(u\to v)$ | smallest | $\mathit{dist}(v)\leftarrow\mathit{dist}(u) + w(u\to v)$ | shortest paths |
| widest path | $\min\{\mathit{width}(u), w(u\to v)\}$ | largest | $\mathit{width}(v)\leftarrow\min\{\mathit{width}(u), w(u\to v)\}$ | widest paths |

- **"Prim"** (undirected graphs) builds a **minimum spanning tree** of $s$'s component. If all edge weights are distinct, the minimum spanning tree is unique, so the result does not depend on the start vertex or on the neighbor order.
- **"Dijkstra"** starts from $\mathit{dist}(s) = 0$. If all weights are positive, $\mathit{dist}(v)$ is the **length** of a shortest path from $s$ to $v$ — the smallest possible sum of edge weights — and the parent edges form a shortest-path tree.
- **Widest path** starts from $\mathit{width}(s) = \infty$. The **width** of a path is the smallest weight of its edges, and $\mathit{width}(v)$ is the largest width of a path from $s$ to $v$: a **widest** or **bottleneck shortest path**.

In the last two variants the value stored at $v$ is the priority of the pair that marks it. The names are in quotes, as in the book, because neither algorithm was first published by the person it is named after; minimum spanning trees come in Chapter 7, shortest paths in Chapter 8, and widest paths reappear in maximum flows (Chapter 10).

```text
    for all vertices v
        if v is unmarked
            WhateverFirstSearch(v)
```

Why not just loop over the vertex array and mark everything? Not every graph comes with an explicit list of vertices — and, more to the point, the loop visits vertices in the order of the data structure, while WFSAll visits **one whole component at a time**. That is what makes it useful: adding a counter that is incremented before each call to WhateverFirstSearch gives CountComponents, which returns the number of components; one more line records which component each vertex is in.

```text
CountAndLabel(G):
    count ← 0
    for all vertices v
        unmark v
    for all vertices v
        if v is unmarked
            count ← count + 1
            LabelOne(v, count)
    return count

⟨⟨Label one component⟩⟩
LabelOne(v, count):
    put v into the bag                 ⟨⟨missing in the book⟩⟩
    while the bag is not empty
        take v from the bag
        if v is unmarked
            mark v
            comp(v) ← count
            for each edge vw
                put w into the bag
```

Without the first line of LabelOne, as printed in the book, the bag starts empty, nothing is ever marked, and CountAndLabel returns $V$.

WFSAll marks every vertex once and puts each undirected edge into the bag twice (each directed edge once), plus one start pair per component, and takes out as many: $O(V + ET)$ time in all. In particular, running depth-first or breadth-first search from every unmarked vertex still takes only $O(V+E)$ time, and the parent edges form a **spanning forest**; best-first search with the edge weights as priorities gives a minimum spanning forest in $O(V + E\log E)$. CountAndLabel returns 2 for the example graph $G$: $a$ through $f$ get label 1, and $g$ and $h$ label 2. The wrapper works with **any** bag, not just a stack — the very first implementation of breadth-first search, by Konrad Zuse around 1945, counted and labeled components.

### Directed graphs

Whatever-first search needs no change for directed graphs: when $v$ is marked, it puts all of its *out*-neighbors into the bag ("for each edge $v\to w$"), and with standard adjacency lists or matrices that is literally the same code. The proof of Lemma 5.1 shows that it marks exactly the vertices reachable from $s$ along directed paths, and that the parent edges $\mathit{parent}(v)\to v$ form a tree rooted at $s$ with every edge directed away from $s$ — a spanning tree of the *reachable* vertices, not of $G$, even when $G$ is drawn in one piece: reachability is not symmetric. With $a\to b$ and $c\to b$, a search from $a$ marks only $a$ and $b$. Varying the bag yields depth-first, breadth-first, shortest-path and widest-path trees of the reachable vertices. (The book adds "minimum-weight directed spanning tree", but best-first search by edge weight does not find one: from $s$, with $s\to a$ of weight 2, $s\to b$ of weight 3 and $b\to a$ of weight 1, it picks $s\to a$ and $s\to b$, total 5, while $s\to b$, $b\to a$ weighs 4. Minimum-weight directed spanning trees need a different algorithm, Chu–Liu/Edmonds.)

## 5.7 Graph reductions: flood fill

A **pixel map** is a two-dimensional array of colors, its entries **pixels**. A **connected region** is a connected set of pixels of the same color, where two pixels are adjacent if they are immediate horizontal or vertical neighbors. **Flood fill** — the paint can — changes every pixel of one connected region to a new color; its input is the pixel map, the indices $(i,j)$ of one pixel of the region, and the new color. (The book opens the section with Edward Moore, who proposed one of the earliest modern examples of whatever-first search in the mid-1950s.)

The solution does not invent a new algorithm. It **reduces** flood fill to reachability, and writes the reduction up in four parts — exactly the four parts the course's graph-reduction rubric grades:

> [!example] Flood fill, written up as a graph reduction
> **The graph.** Let $G$ be the **undirected** graph whose vertices are the $n^2$ pixels $(i,j)$ of the $n\times n$ map $P$, with an edge between two pixels whenever they are horizontal or vertical neighbors of the same color: $(i,j)(i+1,j)$ if $P[i,j] = P[i+1,j]$, and $(i,j)(i,j+1)$ if $P[i,j] = P[i,j+1]$. There are no weights.
>
> **The problem on the graph.** By the definition of a connected region, the region of pixel $(i,j)$ is exactly the component of $G$ containing $(i,j)$: the set of vertices **reachable from $(i,j)$** in $G$. Recoloring those vertices is the flood fill.
>
> **The algorithm.** Run **whatever-first search** in $G$ from $(i,j)$, then give every marked vertex the new color. (The book recolors each vertex the moment it is marked, which comes to the same; recoloring afterwards keeps the search a black box.)
>
> **The time.** $G$ has $n^2$ vertices and at most $2n(n-1) < 2n^2$ edges, and can be built by brute force in $O(n^2)$ time, so the algorithm runs in $O(V+E) = O(n^2)$ time.

This tiny example shows every ingredient of a **reduction**. Whatever-first search is used as a **black box**: how it works is irrelevant; all that matters is its specification — given a graph $G$ and a vertex $s$, it marks the vertices of $G$ reachable from $s$. Like any subroutine call, the reduction must say how to build the subroutine's input and how to use its output. And the running time is stated in **the input's parameters** ($n$), not in the $V$ and $E$ of a graph the algorithm made up.

Only once the reduction works do two optimizations make sense:

- **No explicit graph.** The pixel map itself serves as a standard adjacency list: the same-colored neighbors of a pixel are found in $O(1)$ time each. There is no need for separate marks either — a pixel's color says whether it has been recolored.
- **Output-sensitive time.** Each pixel of the region is marked once and puts at most four pairs into the bag, so the time is proportional to the number of pixels in the region — the size of the component of $(i,j)$ — which can be much smaller than $n^2$.

> [!template] Template — solving a problem by graph reduction (rubric: 3 + 3 + 2 + 2 points)
> 1. **The graph (3 points: vertices 1, edges 1, weights or labels 1).** "Let $G = (V,E)$ be the [**directed** / undirected] graph whose vertices are [an explicit set built from the input, e.g. all pairs $(i,j)$ with $1\le i,j\le n$] and whose edges are [an explicit rule, e.g. $(i,j)\to(i',j')$ whenever …], with weights …." Forgetting to say "directed" costs ½. A brute-force construction in linear time needs no separate algorithm.
> 2. **The problem on the graph (3 points: 1 for the high-level problem, 2 for the details).** "The answer is yes if and only if $t$ is reachable from $s$ in $G$" or "the answer is the length of a shortest path in $G$ from $\ldots$ to any vertex of the form $\ldots$", and why that answers the original question. Naming an algorithm earns nothing here: "breadth-first search" is not a problem.
> 3. **The algorithm (2 points).** Invoke the right standard algorithm as a black box: whatever-first search for reachability, breadth-first search for fewest edges, a weighted shortest-path algorithm only when there are weights. A slower algorithm than necessary (Dijkstra where breadth-first search works) costs 1; a more specific one than necessary (breadth- or depth-first where whatever-first suffices) costs ½; re-explaining a standard algorithm instead of invoking it costs 1.
> 4. **The time (2 points)**, in the parameters of the **input**: "$G$ has $O(n^2)$ vertices and $O(n^2)$ edges, so the algorithm runs in $O(n^2)$ time" — not just "$O(V+E)$".

> [!trap] Where graph reductions lose points
> - **Modifying the algorithm instead of the graph.** Changing the inside of a search to fit the problem is hard to get right and terrible engineering; the rubric gives full credit only to an unmistakably correct modification, and partial credit sparingly. Put what the search must remember into the **vertices** (next subsection) and call the algorithm unchanged.
> - **"Try all paths with depth-first search and keep the best."** Every whatever-first search finds **one** path to each reachable vertex. A graph can have exponentially many simple paths and infinitely many walks, and exploring them all is not an algorithm you want.
> - **Time in $V$ and $E$ only.** $O(V+E)$ is half an answer; say what $V$ and $E$ are in terms of the input.
> - **Vague vertices or edges** — "a vertex for each state" — instead of explicit functions of the input.
> - **Color as the mark** in the optimized flood fill breaks when the new color equals the old one: nothing changes, and the search never ends. Return at once in that case.

### State in the vertices: walks of even length

The most useful trick for harder reductions is to make each vertex remember what the question needs about the walk so far. Here the question is about parity.

> [!example] Example — a walk with an even number of edges
> **Problem.** Given an undirected graph $G = (V,E)$ and vertices $s$ and $t$, is there a walk from $s$ to $t$ with an even number of edges?
>
> **The graph.** Let $H$ be the undirected graph with vertices $(v, b)$ for all $v\in V$ and $b\in\{0,1\}$, and, for every edge $uv\in E$, the two edges $(u,0)(v,1)$ and $(u,1)(v,0)$. It has $2V$ vertices and $2E$ edges.
>
> **The problem on the graph.** Walks in $H$ project to walks in $G$, and every walk in $G$ from $s$ lifts to a walk in $H$ from $(s,0)$ whose second coordinate is the parity of the number of edges so far. So $G$ has an $s$–$t$ walk with an even number of edges if and only if $(t,0)$ is **reachable from $(s,0)$** in $H$.
>
> **The algorithm.** Whatever-first search in $H$ from $(s,0)$; answer whether it marks $(t,0)$.
>
> **The time.** $H$ is built from $G$'s adjacency list in $O(V+E)$ time, and the search takes $O(V+E)$ time: $O(V+E)$ in all.

## Summary

- A graph is a set of pairs: $G = (V,E)$, undirected edges $uv$ or directed edges $u\to v$; $V$ and $E$ are also the numbers of vertices and edges, $E\le V(V-1)/2$ undirected, and $\sum_v\deg(v) = 2E$.
- Walks may repeat vertices, paths may not; components are maximal connected subgraphs; a tree is connected and acyclic, with $V-1$ edges; a dag is a directed acyclic graph.
- The default representation is the standard adjacency list: $\Theta(V+E)$ space, neighbors of $v$ in $O(1+\deg(v))$. An adjacency matrix tests adjacency in $O(1)$ but costs $\Theta(V^2)$ space and $\Theta(V)$ per neighbor scan.
- WhateverFirstSearch$(s)$ marks exactly the vertices reachable from $s$ (Lemma 5.1), and its parent edges form a spanning tree of them. Each vertex enters the bag up to $\deg(v)$ times ($s$ once more), at most $2E+1$ pairs in all: $O(V + ET)$.
- Stack: depth-first search, $O(V+E)$. Queue: breadth-first search, $O(V+E)$, shortest paths by number of edges. Priority queue: best-first search, $O(V + E\log E)$ — minimum spanning trees, shortest paths, widest paths.
- WFSAll visits one component at a time: counting and labeling components, spanning forests, $O(V+E)$. Directed graphs: same code, marks the vertices reachable from $s$.
- A graph reduction states the graph, the problem on it, the black-box algorithm, and the time in the input's parameters. Flood fill is reachability, $O(n^2)$; extra state goes into the vertices.

> [!exam] On the exams
> A graph problem on an exam is normally solved by a **reduction** and graded by the course's standard graph-reduction rubric (10 points): **3** for the graph (1 vertices, 1 edges with $-\tfrac12$ for a missing "directed", 1 weights or labels — all explicit functions of the input), **3** for relating the question to a specific problem on that graph (1 for the problem, such as reachability or shortest path; 2 for the details: which graph, from where to where, and why it answers the question), **2** for invoking the right algorithm as a black box ($-1$ if slower than necessary, $-\tfrac12$ if more specific than necessary, $-1$ for re-explaining it), and **2** for the running time in the input's parameters. The rubric warns against two common mistakes: modifying a standard algorithm instead of the input graph, and "trying all paths". Results from lecture and the book — whatever-first, breadth-first and depth-first search and their running times — are cited, not reproved, and graphs are assumed to come as standard adjacency lists. Which exam covers which chapters is on the course website. See also [[0-toolkit/06-how-exams-are-graded|how exams are graded]].
