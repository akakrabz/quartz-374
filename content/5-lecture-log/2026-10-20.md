## What was covered, in order

1. **Where the course is.** Divide and conquer, backtracking and dynamic programming were all ways of designing an algorithm by recursion. Graphs are different: a few standard algorithms, used as black boxes.
2. **What a graph is.** Not a drawing (two different drawings of the same graph were shown) but a pair $G = (V, E)$: $V$ a finite, non-empty set of **vertices**, $E$ a set of **edges** — ordered pairs of vertices if the graph is directed, unordered pairs if it is undirected. Notation, as in the textbook: $u\to v$ for a directed edge, $uv$ for an undirected one. → [[2-algorithms/05-basic-graph-algorithms#52-basic-definitions|§5.2]], [[concepts/graphs]]
3. **Graphs that model something.** Because $V$ can be any set, the objects of a problem can be the vertices: an **intersection graph** (vertices are line segments or disks, edges are intersecting pairs — from computational geometry, the instructor's research area), a **dependency graph** (vertices are the subproblems of a recursive algorithm, with an edge from $u$ to $v$ if $u$ calls $v$), a **configuration graph** (vertices are the states of a puzzle or machine, edges are single moves), and the transition graph of an NFA. Whether a puzzle can get from one configuration to another is whether there is a path between two vertices. → [[2-algorithms/05-basic-graph-algorithms#53-representations-and-examples|§5.3]]
4. **Data structures, as a review** (for simple graphs). The **adjacency matrix**: a $V\times V$ array; testing one edge takes constant time, but listing the neighbors of a vertex takes $O(V)$ time and the matrix always takes $O(V^2)$ space. The **adjacency list**: an array indexed by vertices, each entry a list of neighbors; testing one edge takes $O(\deg u)$ time, listing neighbors takes constant time per neighbor, and the space is $O(V + E)$. From here on the course assumes adjacency lists. → [[2-algorithms/05-basic-graph-algorithms#54-data-structures|§5.4]]
5. **The reachability problem.** Given a graph and a vertex $s$, find every vertex that $s$ can reach (in an undirected graph: the component of $s$). Depth-first search and breadth-first search both solve it, and they are the same algorithm up to implementation details.
6. **Whatever-first search.** Put $s$ into a **bag**. While the bag is not empty, take any vertex $u$ out; if $u$ is unmarked, mark it and put the head of every edge $u\to v$ into the bag. The mark is what prevents running around a cycle forever; which vertex comes out depends on the data structure used as the bag. → [[2-algorithms/05-basic-graph-algorithms#55-whatever-first-search|§5.5]], [[concepts/whatever-first-search]]
7. **Why it is correct** (sketch only). It marks exactly the vertices reachable from $s$: marked implies reachable by induction on the order of marking, and reachable implies marked by induction on the distance from $s$. The full proof is in Erickson's book.
8. **Running time.** If one insertion or removal from the bag takes time $T$: each vertex is marked once, each edge is seen once, so there are at most $E + 1$ insertions and as many removals — $O(V + E\cdot T)$ in all, and $O(V + E)$ with a stack or a queue. Different bags give different orders and different spanning trees (Thursday). → [[2-algorithms/05-basic-graph-algorithms#analysis|§5.5, analysis]]
9. **Reductions.** Reducing problem $A$ to problem $B$ means solving an instance of $A$ by building one or more instances of $B$ and running an algorithm for $B$. Here: build a graph, run a known graph algorithm. → [[2-algorithms/05-basic-graph-algorithms#57-graph-reductions-flood-fill|§5.7]], [[concepts/graph-reductions]]
10. **The first reduction, and how to write one** — see the next two sections.

## Examples and problems worked in class

- **The same graph, four ways.** One graph on the vertices $a$ to $m$ was shown as two drawings, as an intersection graph of segments and of disks, as an adjacency matrix (a 1 in row $b$, column $c$; a 0 in row $d$, column $j$) and as an adjacency list.
- **A dependency graph:** recursive Fibonacci — $F(6)$ needs $F(5)$ and $F(4)$. → [[2-algorithms/03-dynamic-programming#31-matravrtta-fibonacci-numbers|§3.1]]
- **A configuration graph:** the legal positions of the four-disk Tower of Hanoi, with an edge for every single move. → [[2-algorithms/01-recursion#13-tower-of-hanoi|§1.3]]
- **Useless states of a DFA.** Given a DFA with $n$ states over the binary alphabet, a state $q$ is *useless* if no input string leads to it: there is no $x$ with $\delta^*(s, x) = q$. Find all useless states. The motivating picture was the DFA produced by the subset construction, most of whose states are never reached ([[1-models-of-computation/04-nondeterministic-automata#45-nfa-to-dfa-the-subset-construction|Models §4.5]]). The answer given in class:
  - *Graph:* the vertices are the states, $V = Q$; the edges are $p\to q$ for every pair with $\delta(p, \mathtt{0}) = q$ or $\delta(p, \mathtt{1}) = q$.
  - *Problem:* reachability — a state $q$ is useless if and only if $s$ cannot reach $q$.
  - *Algorithm:* whatever-first search from $s$; the useless states are the ones left unmarked.
  - *Time:* the graph has $n$ vertices and $2n$ edges, so $O(n)$.

## What the instructor said about write-ups and exams

> [!quote] On what a reduction answer must contain
> "This is exactly the right amount of detail for a really nice exam solution. Tell us what graph you're building, what are its vertices, what are its edges. If it's not obvious what the vertices and edges are representing, you might want to say a little bit more, but typically it'll be pretty clear. Tell us what problem you're trying to solve and how the solution is useful. Tell us what algorithm to call and how to use the results."

> [!exam] Points made about grading
> - **Name the problem, not a traversal.** A plain "whatever-first search" is read as the stack or queue version. Saying specifically depth-first or breadth-first search when all you need is reachability may cost a little: "that's more detailed than necessary" and "suggests that you're not thinking of solving the actual problem you're trying to solve."
> - **Running time in the input's terms.** Report $O(n)$ for the DFA problem, not $O(V + E)$: "You need to talk about the original input to the problem." Someone who asks for the useless states does not know that a graph is involved.
> - **Building the graph** may be assumed to take time proportional to its numbers of vertices and edges, unless the construction does something complicated; then include that time.
> - **Not required:** the formal induction proof that a state is useless exactly when it is unreachable, and path-induction proofs in general — "I'm not going to ask you to do these kinds of path induction proofs for the class."

The same four parts, with their points, are the graph-reduction rubric: [[0-toolkit/06-how-exams-are-graded#13-graph-reductions|how exams are graded §13]], template [[0-toolkit/02-proof-templates#22-solving-a-problem-by-graph-reduction|§22]].
