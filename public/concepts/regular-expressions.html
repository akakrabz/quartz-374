Definition — regular expression and its language

A regular expression is ∅, a string w (possibly ε), or S+T, ST or S∗ for regular expressions S and T, with parentheses for grouping. Its language is

L(∅)=∅,L(w)={w},L(S+T)=L(S)∪L(T),L(ST)=L(S)∙L(T),L(S∗)=L(S)∗.

Precedence: ∗ binds tightest, then concatenation, then +. Two expressions are equivalent if they have the same language.

Where it comes from. It is the recursive definition of regular languages, written compactly. Every expression is really a tree whose root is the operation applied last; its subtrees are the subexpressions. So a proof about all regular expressions is a structural induction with five cases — ∅, a string, S+T, ST, S∗ — whose hypothesis covers the proper subexpressions S and T (template). By Kleene’s theorem, expressions describe exactly the languages of DFAs.

Recipe — designing a regular expression

  1. List short examples in and out of the language, including ε and every string of length 1 and 2.
  2. Cut a typical string into blocks that a star can repeat; handle the beginning and the end separately.
  3. Write the expression piece by piece, and say in English what each piece does.
  4. Test both directions: nothing extra is generated and nothing is missed; check ε explicitly.
  5. “And” conditions are hard: refine the blocks, or build a product DFA and convert it by state elimination.

Classic mistakes

  • Precedence: 10∗ is 1(0∗), not (10)∗; and 0+1∗ is not (0+1)∗.
  • Only the real syntax: no ∩, no complement, no counters like 0n, no regex shortcuts (?, [01], + for “one or more”, back-references). Write RR∗ for “one or more copies of R”.
  • ε versus ∅: L(∅∗)={ε}. A missing or extra ε is the typical one-mistake deduction.
  • A well-formed substring of R need not be a subexpression: induct on the tree, not on the characters.
  • In the S∗ case of a structural induction, the hypothesis covers S, not S∗.

Example — binary strings with no 00

Idea. Every 0 except possibly the last symbol is followed by a 1, so the string splits into blocks 1 and 01, followed by at most one final 0.

Answer: (1+01)∗(ε+0). Cutting from the other end — every 0 except possibly the first symbol is preceded by a 1 — gives the equivalent (ε+0)(1+10)∗.

Test: ε, 0, 10, 0101 and 0110 match; 00, 100 and 1001 do not.

A wrong answer: (1+01)∗ misses every string of the language that ends in 0 — 0, 10, 110, … — infinitely many, so it earns no correctness credit.
