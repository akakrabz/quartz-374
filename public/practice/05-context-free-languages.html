5.1 Closure and ambiguity, true or false

Easy · true or false · context-free closure · ambiguity

All languages are over Σ={0,1}. Answer each statement Yes (always true) or No, with a one-sentence reason; for a No, give a counterexample. You may use the fact (§5.6) that {0n1n0n∣n≥0} is not context-free.

(a) If L1​∪L2​ is context-free, then L1​ and L2​ are both context-free.

(b) If L is context-free and R is regular, then L∖R is context-free.

(c) If L is context-free and R is regular, then R∖L is context-free.

(d) If L is context-free but not regular, then L is not regular.

(e) If a grammar G is ambiguous, then L(G) is infinite.

(f) Every regular language is generated by some unambiguous grammar.

Source: original.

Hint

Closure under union says nothing about the pieces of a union. For (b) and (c), write each difference as an intersection with a complement, and ask which language gets complemented. For (f), think of the grammar you get from a DFA.

Solution

(a) No. Take L1​={0n1n0n∣n≥0} and L2​=L1​​: their union is Σ∗, generated by S→0S∣1S∣ε, but L1​ is not context-free.

(b) Yes. L∖R=L∩R, the complement of a regular language is regular, and the intersection of a context-free language with a regular language is context-free (construction below).

(c) No. Take R=Σ∗ and L={0n1n0n∣n≥0}​, which is context-free (grammar below); then R∖L={0n1n0n∣n≥0} is not.

(d) Yes. If L were regular, then L=L would be regular too, because regular languages are closed under complement (Corollary 3.4).

(e) No. S→A∣B, A→0, B→0 is ambiguous — 0 has two parse trees — but its language is {0}.

(f) Yes. The grammar of a DFA — one nonterminal [q] per state, rules [q]→a[δ(q,a)], and [q]→ε for every accepting q — gives each accepted string exactly one parse tree, which traces the DFA’s unique run (Problem 5.2 builds one).

The grammar for (c). A string is not of the form 0n1n0n exactly when it has a 1, a later 0 and a still later 1 (so it is not in 0∗1∗0∗), or it is 0a1b0c with a=b, or with b=c:

​S→N∣UZ∣ZVU→0U1∣0Z∣Y1​​N→X1X0X1XV→1V0∣1Y∣Z0​​X→0X∣1X∣εZ→0Z∣εY→1Y∣ε​

Here U generates {0a1b∣a=b}, V generates {1b0c∣b=c}, Z and Y generate 0∗ and 1∗, and X generates every string.

The construction for (b). Given a grammar for L and a DFA M=(Σ,Q,s,A,δ), use nonterminals Bpq​ for every nonterminal B and states p,q, meaning “the strings of L(B) that drive M from p to q”. For every rule B→X1​⋯Xk​ and every choice of states p=r0​,r1​,…,rk​=q, add Bpq​→Y1​⋯Yk​, where Yi​=(Xi​)ri−1​ri​​ if Xi​ is a nonterminal, and Yi​=Xi​ if Xi​ is a terminal with δ(ri−1​,Xi​)=ri​ (if it is a terminal without that transition, skip this choice); a rule B→ε gives Bpp​→ε. A new start symbol with S′→Ssf​ for every f∈A generates L∩L(M). For (b), let M be a DFA for R.

Watch out: (b) and (c) differ only in which language is complemented. Context-free languages survive intersection with a regular language but not complementation, and R∖L=R∩L needs L.

Answer. (a) No (b) Yes (c) No (d) Yes (e) No (f) Yes.

5.2 A grammar from a DFA

Easy · grammar design · parse trees

The DFA M with states 0,1,2, start state 0, accepting states {0} and δ(q,a)=(2q+a)mod3 accepts the binary representations of the multiples of 3 (reading ε as the number 0).

(a) Turn M into a context-free grammar with one nonterminal Rq​ for each state q, and describe the language of each Rq​ in a self-contained sentence.

(b) Give the derivation of 1001 (nine in binary) and compare it with the run of M.

(c) Explain in one or two sentences why your grammar is unambiguous.

Source: textbook exercise style.

Hint

A rule Rq​→aRp​ says “read a, then continue from state p” — the construction of Lemma 5.6 for an automaton with a single module. Where must a derivation be allowed to stop?

Solution

Idea. Rq​ generates “the rest of the input” when M is in state q: the strings that lead from q to an accepting state. One rule per transition, and one ε-rule per accepting state.

(a) The transitions are δ(0,0)=0, δ(0,1)=1, δ(1,0)=2, δ(1,1)=0, δ(2,0)=1, δ(2,1)=2. With start symbol R0​:

R0​→0R0​∣1R1​∣εR1​→0R2​∣1R0​R2​→0R1​∣1R2​

Write val(x) for the number whose binary representation is x. A prefix of value v leaves M in state q=vmod3, and reading x after it gives the value v⋅2∣x∣+val(x), whose remainder mod 3 depends on v only through q. So Rq​ generates the strings x for which q⋅2∣x∣+val(x) is a multiple of 3; for q=0,1,2 that number is the value of x, of 1x and of 10x:

  • R0​ generates the binary representations of the multiples of 3;
  • R1​ generates the strings x such that 1x represents a multiple of 3;
  • R2​ generates the strings x such that 10x represents a multiple of 3.

(b) R0​⇝1R1​⇝10R2​⇝100R1​⇝1001R0​⇝1001. The nonterminals R0​,R1​,R2​,R1​,R0​ are exactly the states 0,1,2,1,0 that M visits on 1001, and the last step, R0​→ε, is allowed because the run ends in the accepting state 0.

(c) Every sentential form has a single nonterminal, at its end, and each Rq​ has one rule per next symbol (plus R0​→ε, which can only finish the derivation), so every step of a derivation of w is forced by the next symbol of w. Hence w has at most one derivation, so at most one parse tree — the run of M on w.

Check: 11 (three): R0​⇝1R1​⇝11R0​⇝11. 10 (two): R0​⇝1R1​⇝10R2​, and R2​ has no ε-rule, so the derivation cannot finish. ε is generated, as the number 0 should be.

Watch out: the ε-rules are where acceptance lives. Without R0​→ε no derivation ever ends and the grammar generates ∅; an ε-rule on a non-accepting state adds wrong strings (R1​→ε would generate 1).

Answer. R0​→0R0​∣1R1​∣ε, R1​→0R2​∣1R0​, R2​→0R1​∣1R2​, start R0​; Rq​ generates the strings that take M from q to state 0, i.e. the x for which x, 1x, 10x (for q=0,1,2) represents a multiple of 3. It is unambiguous because each derivation step is forced by the next symbol.

5.3 Find the error, a union

Easy · find the error · grammar design

A student is asked for a context-free grammar for

L={0n1n∣n≥0}∪{0n12n∣n≥0},

the strings of 0s followed by either equally many or twice as many 1s, and answers:

”S→0S1∣0S11∣ε. Each rule adds one 0 together with one or two 1s, so every 0 is matched with one 1 or with two 1s.”

(a) What is wrong? Give a shortest string that shows it.

(b) Fix the grammar, and describe the language of every nonterminal.

Source: original.

Hint

Does the student’s sentence describe L? Try a derivation that uses both rules.

Solution

(a) The slip: the shape is chosen at every step instead of once. One derivation may mix the two rules, so the grammar generates every 0n1m with n≤m≤2n. The shortest wrong string is 00111:

S⇝0S1⇝00S111⇝00111,

with two 0s and three 1s — neither equally many nor twice as many. Every 0n1n+1 with n≥2 is wrong too, so there are infinitely many wrong strings, which on an exam costs all the correctness points. (Nothing is missing: every string of L is generated.) The student’s sentence describes each 0 separately, but L needs all the 0s to be matched the same way.

(b) Choose the shape once, at the top:

S→A∣BA→0A1∣εB→0B11∣ε
  • A generates {0n1n∣n≥0};
  • B generates {0n12n∣n≥0};
  • S generates L(A)∪L(B)=L.

Check: 0011 comes from A and 011 from B; 00111 comes from neither. The empty string has two parse trees (through A and through B), so the fixed grammar is ambiguous — which costs nothing.

Watch out: the student’s grammar is a correct answer to a different question — it generates {0i1j∣i≤j≤2i}, Example 1 of §5.4. For a union of two shapes, branch once, with S→A∣B.

Answer. (a) One derivation can mix the two rules; the shortest wrong string is 00111, and there are infinitely many more. (b) S→A∣B, A→0A1∣ε, B→0B11∣ε.

5.4 Recursive automaton to grammar

Easy · recursive automata · grammar design

A recursive automaton over Σ={1,+,(,)} has main module E and one more module T; an edge labeled with a module name calls that module.

modulestartacceptingedges
EsE​tE​sE​T​x,  x+​sE​,  xε​tE​
TsT​tT​sT​1​tT​,  sT​(​y,  yE​z,  z)​tT​

(a) Write the grammar of Lemma 5.6, with one nonterminal [q] for each state q.

(b) Describe the language of every nonterminal.

(c) Simplify the grammar to two nonterminals, and decide whether (1+1)+1, 1+ and () are accepted.

Source: original.

Hint

Each edge gives one rule: reading a on an edge from q to p gives [q]→a[p], an ε-edge gives [q]→[p], and a call of module A gives [q]→[sA​][p] — run all of A, then continue at p. The accepting state of each module gets the return rule [tA​]→ε.

Solution

(a) One rule per edge plus one return rule per module (Lemma 5.6), with start symbol [sE​]:

​[sE​]→[sT​][x][sT​]→1[tT​]∣([y]​​[x]→+[sE​]∣[tE​][y]→[sE​][z]​​[tE​]→ε[z]→)[tT​][tT​]→ε​

(b) Call a string an expression if it is t1​+t2​+⋯+tk​ for some k≥1, where each term ti​ is 1 or (e) for an expression e. Then

  • [sE​] generates the expressions, and [sT​] the terms;
  • [x] generates ε and the strings that consist of + followed by an expression — what may follow a term inside an expression;
  • [y] generates the strings that consist of an expression followed by ) — what must follow an opening parenthesis;
  • [z] generates only ), and [tE​] and [tT​] generate only ε.

(c) The nonterminals [tE​], [tT​] and [z] each generate a single string, so substitute it for them; then [y] becomes [sE​] followed by ), and [x] becomes ε or + followed by [sE​]. Renaming [sE​], [sT​] to E, T gives

E→T∣T+ET→1∣(E)

(1+1)+1 is accepted: E⇝T+E⇝(E)+E⇝(T+E)+E⇝∗(1+1)+E⇝(1+1)+T⇝(1+1)+1. 1+ is not: a + must be followed by an expression, and expressions are non-empty. () is not: between the parentheses there must be an expression.

Watch out: a call edge is not a terminal. sE​T​x becomes [sE​]→[sT​][x] — run module T to its end, then continue at x — and not [sE​]→T[x]. And without the return rules [tE​]→ε, [tT​]→ε no derivation ever finishes.

Answer. (a) The nine rules above (seven nonterminals). (b) [sE​]: expressions; [sT​]: terms; [x]: the empty string or + followed by an expression; [y]: an expression followed by ); [z]: only ); [tE​], [tT​]: only ε. (c) E→T∣T+E, T→1∣(E); (1+1)+1 is accepted, 1+ and () are not.

5.5 Two grammars with a separator

Medium · grammar design

Let Σ={0,1,#}. Give a context-free grammar for each language, and describe the language of every nonterminal in a self-contained sentence. No proof is required.

(a) La​={u#v∣u,v∈{0,1}∗, #(1,u)=#(1,v)}: the two sides of the # contain equally many 1s.

(b) Lb​={x#y∣x,y∈{0,1}∗, y is a substring of xR}. For example, 0110#1 and 01#10 are in Lb​, but 01#01 is not.

Source: original.

Hint

(a) Only the 1s need partners: pair the first 1 of u with the last 1 of v, and let 0s appear anywhere. (b) y is a substring of xR exactly when x=uyRv for some strings u and v. Write p=yR; then x#y=u⋅pv#pR, and in the second part p and pR are nested around v#.

Solution

(a) Idea. Match the 1s of u with the 1s of v from the outside in. The 0s are unconstrained, so they can be added at either end at any time.

S→0S∣S0∣1S1∣#

S is the only nonterminal, and it generates exactly La​. Every rule keeps the numbers of 1s on the two sides equal. Conversely, a string u#v∈La​ can be taken apart by the rules: if u starts with 0 or v ends with 0, remove that 0; otherwise either u=v=ε (the string is #), or u=1u′ and v=v′1 with u′#v′∈La​.

Check: S⇝1S1⇝1S01⇝1#01. 1#0 is not generated, because every 1 is created together with a partner on the other side.

(b) Idea. y is a substring of xR if and only if yR is a substring of x, that is, x=upv with p=yR. Then x#y=u⋅(pv#pR): the prefix u is arbitrary, and the rest nests the symbols of p around v#.

S→ZTT→0T0∣1T1∣Z#Z→0Z∣1Z∣ε
  • Z generates all strings over {0,1}.
  • T generates the strings x#y with x,y∈{0,1}∗ such that yR is a prefix of x — equivalently, y is a suffix of xR.
  • S generates Lb​: a string of T with an arbitrary string added in front.

Check: 01#10 has u=v=ε and p=01: S⇝ZT⇝T⇝0T0⇝01T10⇝01Z#10⇝01#10. For 0110#1 take u=0, p=1, v=10. And 01#01 is not generated: p=(01)R=10 is not a substring of 01.

Watch out: the Z in T→Z# is easy to forget. Without it, T generates only the strings p#pR, and the grammar finds y only at the start of xR: it misses 10#1, whose y=1 is a substring of xR=01 but not a prefix.

Answer. (a) S→0S∣S0∣1S1∣#. (b) S→ZT, T→0T0∣1T1∣Z#, Z→0Z∣1Z∣ε.

5.6 The dangling else

Medium · ambiguity · parse trees

The grammar S→0S1S∣0S∣ε generates the set P of strings in which every prefix has at least as many 0s as 1s (Problem 5.10 proves this). Read 0 as if and 1 as else.

(a) Find a shortest string with two different parse trees, and give both as leftmost derivations.

(b) Give an unambiguous grammar for P, describe the language of every nonterminal, and explain why every string of P has exactly one parse tree.

Source: classic.

Hint

(a) An else after two ifs can belong to either of them. (b) Let every 1 belong to the nearest unmatched 0 before it: in a rule S→0M1S, let M generate only balanced strings (equally many 0s and 1s, and every prefix with at least as many 0s as 1s).

Solution

(a) 001. Every string of length at most 2 has at most one parse tree, and among the strings of length 3 only 001 has two:

​S⇝0S1S⇝00S1S⇝001S⇝001S⇝0S⇝00S1S⇝001S⇝001​​(the 1 belongs to the first 0)(the 1 belongs to the second 0)​

As trees: S(0,S(0,S(ε)),1,S(ε)) and S(0,S(0,S(ε),1,S(ε))). This is the dangling else: in if if … else … the grammar does not say which if the else belongs to.

(b) Idea. Let every 1 close the nearest unmatched 0, by requiring the part between a 0 and its 1 to be balanced.

S→0S∣0M1S∣εM→0M1M∣ε
  • M generates the balanced strings: equally many 0s and 1s, and every prefix with at least as many 0s as 1s.
  • S generates P.

Why there is one parse tree. Let w∈P be non-empty; it starts with 0. The rule S→0M1S produces strings 0m1s whose prefix 0m1 is balanced while every shorter non-empty prefix, 0m′ with m′ a prefix of m, has more 0s than 1s; so 0m1 must be the shortest non-empty balanced prefix of the string. The rule S→0S produces strings with no non-empty balanced prefix. Hence:

  • if w has a non-empty balanced prefix, only S→0M1S fits, and the split w=0m1s is forced: cut after the shortest such prefix;
  • otherwise only S→0S fits, with w=0s.

Either way the first rule and the pieces are determined, and the same argument works for M (a non-empty balanced string must be cut after its shortest non-empty balanced prefix). By induction on the length, every string of P has exactly one parse tree. The language is still P: the two cases are exactly those of the proof in Problem 5.10.

Check: 001 now has only S⇝0S⇝00M1S⇝001S⇝001 — the else belongs to the nearest if, the rule of C and Java. Another unambiguous grammar puts the unmatched 0s at the top level instead: S→M∣M0S with the same M.

Watch out: to show ambiguity you need one string with two parse trees — two leftmost derivations; two derivations that only expand the nonterminals in another order build the same tree. And the unambiguous grammar must still generate all of P: test it on strings such as 0101 and 0010.

Answer. (a) 001: S⇝0S1S⇝00S1S⇝001S⇝001 and S⇝0S⇝00S1S⇝001S⇝001. (b) S→0S∣0M1S∣ε, M→0M1M∣ε, where M generates the balanced strings.

5.7 A small CNF conversion

Medium · Chomsky normal form

Consider the grammar

S→0S1∣ABA→0A∣εB→1B∣ε

(a) What language does it generate?

(b) Convert it to Chomsky normal form with the algorithm of §5.9, showing the grammar after each step.

(c) How many steps does every derivation of 0011 take in your final grammar, and why?

Source: original.

Hint

Every step except step 4 changes something. When you look for nullable nonterminals, do not stop at the ε-rules: S→AB makes S nullable, and then the new start symbol too. In step 5, copy the rules of A and B as they are after step 3.

(a) A generates 0∗ and B generates 1∗, so AB generates 0∗1∗; the rule S→0S1 wraps a string of 0∗1∗ in one more 0…1, which stays in 0∗1∗. So the language is 0∗1∗ — regular, which makes every step easy to test.

5.8 Enough 1s at the end

Hard · fooling sets · grammar design

Let L be the set of binary strings whose last #(0,w) symbols are all 1s:

L={w∈{0,1}∗∣w=x1#(0,w) for some string x}.

For example, 0011, 101 and 11 are in L, but 0101 and 10 are not.

(a) Prove that L is not regular.

(b) Give a context-free grammar for L, describe the language of every nonterminal, and explain briefly why the grammar generates exactly L.

Source: original.

Hint

(a) A DFA would have to count the 0s; try F=0∗. (b) Look at the first symbol. Removing a leading 1 keeps a string in L. If a string of L starts with 0, what must its last symbol be, and what is left if you remove both?

Solution

(a) Idea. An algorithm for L must remember how many 0s it has read, to know how many 1s to demand at the end.

Proof. Let F=0∗. Let x and y be arbitrary distinct strings in F; then x=0i and y=0j for some integers i=j. A suffix that distinguishes x from y also distinguishes y from x, so we may name the two strings so that i<j. Let z=1i.

Then xz=0i1i∈L, because it has i 0s and its last i symbols are 1s.

And yz=0j1i∈/L, because it has j 0s, but its last j symbols are 0j−i1i, which contain a 0 because j>i.

So z distinguishes x and y, and F is a fooling set for L. Because F is infinite, L is not regular. ■

(b) Idea. Decide by the first symbol: a leading 1 is harmless, and a leading 0 must be paid for with a 1 at the very end.

S→0S1∣1S∣ε

S is the only nonterminal, and it generates exactly L:

  • Every rule stays inside L. ε∈L. Let x∈L have k 0s, so ∣x∣≥k and the last k symbols of x are 1s. Then 1x has the same k 0s and the same last k symbols, so 1x∈L; and 0x1 has k+1 0s, and its last k+1 symbols are the last k symbols of x followed by 1, all 1s, so 0x1∈L.
  • Every string of L is built this way. Let w∈L be non-empty, with k 0s. If w=1x, then x has the same k 0s and the same last k symbols, so x∈L. If w starts with 0, then k≥1, so the last symbol of w is 1 and w=0x1; now x has k−1 0s, and its last k−1 symbols are the symbols just before the final 1 of w, all 1s, so x∈L. In both cases x is shorter than w, so by induction on the length x is generated, and then so is w. (Problem 5.10 shows how such an argument is written out in full.)

Check: S⇝0S1⇝00S11⇝0011 and S⇝1S⇝10S1⇝101. 0101 starts with 0 and ends with 1, but the middle 10 is not in L, so 0101 is not generated — as it should be.

Watch out: the reflex answer S→0S1∣S1∣ε generates only strings of the form 0m1n with n≥m. It misses 101, 1101, 11101, … — infinitely many strings, because in L some 1s may come before the 0s.

Grading notes. Fooling set: 4 points for an explicit infinite fooling set, 6 for the argument with arbitrary x=y (2 for the suffix, 2 for each membership claim). Grammar: 2 for syntax and 8 for correctness (how exams are graded).

Answer. (a) F=0∗: for 0i and 0j with i<j, the suffix 1i gives 0i1i∈L and 0j1i∈/L. (b) S→0S1∣1S∣ε.

5.9 A 1 in the first half

Hard · fooling sets · grammar design

Let L be the set of binary strings of even length whose first half contains a 1:

L={xy∣x,y∈{0,1}∗, ∣x∣=∣y∣, #(1,x)≥1}.

For example, 10, 0100 and 1000 are in L, but 01, 0010 and 101 are not.

(a) Prove that L is not regular.

(b) Give a context-free grammar for L, and describe the language of every nonterminal.

Source: original.

Hint

(a) With F=0∗, put a single 1 into the suffix, so that it lands in the first half of xz but not of yz — and let the parity of ∣yz∣ help you. (b) Fix one 1 in the first half and write the string as u1v. How must ∣u∣ and ∣v∣ be related? Generate u together with the last ∣u∣ symbols of v.

Solution

(a) Idea. After reading 0i1, an algorithm must remember i: the 1 lies in the first half exactly when at least i+1 more symbols follow (and the total length is even).

Proof. Let F=0∗. Let x and y be arbitrary distinct strings in F; then x=0i and y=0j for some integers i=j. A suffix that distinguishes x from y also distinguishes y from x, so we may name the two strings so that i<j. Let z=10i+1.

Then xz=0i10i+1 has length 2i+2, and its first half 0i1 contains a 1, so xz∈L.

And yz=0j10i+1∈/L, which has length i+j+2. If i+j is odd, that length is odd. If i+j is even, then j≥i+2 (because j−i is even and positive), so the first half has length 2i+j​+1≤j and lies inside the block 0j: it contains no 1.

So z distinguishes x and y, and F is a fooling set for L. Because F is infinite, L is not regular. ■

(b) Idea. Pick a 1 in the first half and write the string as u1v. The length ∣u∣+∣v∣+1 must be even and the 1, at position ∣u∣+1, must be in the first half; together these say that ∣v∣−∣u∣ is odd and positive. So wrap u and the last ∣u∣ symbols of v around a 1 followed by a string of odd length.

S→XSX∣1OO→X∣XXOX→0∣1
  • X generates the one-symbol strings 0 and 1.
  • O generates the binary strings of odd length.
  • S generates the strings u1v in which ∣v∣−∣u∣ is odd and positive, and these are exactly the strings of L. If ∣v∣=∣u∣+2m+1, the length is 2(∣u∣+m+1) and the 1 sits at position ∣u∣+1≤∣u∣+m+1, in the first half. Conversely, a 1 at position k of a string of length 2n with k≤n gives ∣u∣=k−1 and ∣v∣=2n−k, whose difference 2(n−k)+1 is odd and positive.

Check: S⇝XSX⇝0SX⇝01OX⇝01XX⇝010X⇝0100, and 1000 is 1O with O⇝∗000. 0010 is not generated: its only 1 is at position 3 of 4.

Watch out:

  • S→XSX∣1X looks natural, but it only finds a 1 at the last position of the first half: it misses 1000, 1010 and every 102k−1 with k≥2 — infinitely many strings.
  • S→XSX∣1Z, with Z generating all strings, forgets the parity: it generates 1 and 100.

Answer. (a) F=0∗: for 0i and 0j with i<j, the suffix 10i+1 gives 0i10i+1∈L and 0j10i+1∈/L. (b) S→XSX∣1O, O→X∣XXO, X→0∣1.

5.10 Prefixes of balanced strings, a proof

Hard · grammar proof

Let P be the set of strings w∈{0,1}∗ in which every prefix (including ε and w itself) has at least as many 0s as 1s. Prove that the grammar

S→0S1S∣0S∣ε

generates exactly P: prove L(S)⊆P and P⊆L(S), each by induction, in full.

Source: textbook exercise style.

Hint

Write bal(x):=#(0,x)−#(1,x); then bal(xy)=bal(x)+bal(y), and the prefixes of xy are the prefixes of x and the strings xq for prefixes q of y. For P⊆L(S), take a non-empty w∈P and look for its shortest non-empty prefix with balance 0. If there is one, it has the form 0x1.

Solution

Idea. Cut w where its first 0 is “closed”: after the shortest non-empty prefix with equally many 0s and 1s. If there is such a prefix, w=0x1y (rule S→0S1S); if not, the first 0 is never closed and w=0x (rule S→0S).

Notation. bal(x):=#(0,x)−#(1,x), so bal(xy)=bal(x)+bal(y), and P={w∣bal(p)≥0 for every prefix p of w}. The prefixes of xy are the prefixes of x and the strings xq for prefixes q of y.

Claim 1: L(S)⊆P. Let w be an arbitrary string in L(S), fix a derivation S⇝∗w, and assume that every string in L(S) shorter than w is in P. Every rule other than S→ε puts terminals next to its copies of S, so the pieces below are strictly shorter than w; unlike Lemma 5.3, no shortest-derivation trick is needed. There are three cases, by the first rule of the derivation.

  • S→ε. Then w=ε, whose only prefix has balance 0.
  • S→0S. Then w=0x with S⇝∗x and ∣x∣<∣w∣, so x∈P by the induction hypothesis. A prefix of w is ε, or 0q for a prefix q of x, and bal(0q)=1+bal(q)≥1.
  • S→0S1S. Then w=0x1y with S⇝∗x and S⇝∗y, both shorter than w, so x,y∈P by the induction hypothesis. A prefix of w is ε; or 0q for a prefix q of x, with balance 1+bal(q)≥1; or 0x1q for a prefix q of y, with balance 1+bal(x)−1+bal(q)=bal(x)+bal(q)≥0, because x is a prefix of itself and x,y∈P.

In every case every prefix of w has non-negative balance, so w∈P.

Claim 2: P⊆L(S). Let w be an arbitrary string in P, and assume that every string in P shorter than w is in L(S). If w=ε, then S⇝ε. Otherwise the first symbol of w is 0, because the one-symbol prefix has balance at least 0. There are two cases.

  • Some non-empty prefix of w has balance 0. Let p be the shortest one; ∣p∣≥2, because bal(0)=1. Deleting the last symbol of p leaves a non-empty prefix of w shorter than p, whose balance is ≥0 (as w∈P) and =0 (by the choice of p), hence ≥1; one more symbol brings it to 0, so it was exactly 1 and the last symbol of p is 1. So p=0x1 and w=0x1y for some strings x and y. Then x∈P: for a prefix q of x, 0q is a non-empty prefix of w shorter than p, so bal(0q)≥1 and bal(q)≥0. And y∈P: for a prefix q of y, pq is a prefix of w, so 0≤bal(pq)=bal(p)+bal(q)=bal(q). Both x and y are shorter than w, so the induction hypothesis gives S⇝∗x and S⇝∗y, and therefore S⇝0S1S⇝∗0x1y=w.
  • No non-empty prefix of w has balance 0. Write w=0x. For a prefix q of x, 0q is a non-empty prefix of w, so bal(0q)≥1 and bal(q)≥0; hence x∈P. Since x is shorter than w, the induction hypothesis gives S⇝∗x, and S⇝0S⇝∗0x=w.

In every case w∈L(S). Together the two claims give L(S)=P. ■

Check: ε, 0, 01, 001, 0011 and 010 are in P and are generated (010 =0⋅ε⋅1⋅0 by the first case); 1, 10 and 011 are not in P, and are not generated.

Watch out: the cut must be after the shortest balanced prefix. After the longest one, 0101 would split as 0⋅10⋅1, and 10∈/P.

Grading notes. Each inclusion is an induction and is graded like one: an arbitrary string, an explicit strong induction hypothesis, exhaustive cases (one per rule for L(S)⊆P, the two cases above for P⊆L(S)), the base case, and every use of the hypothesis on a string that is visibly shorter (how exams are graded).

Answer. Strong induction on ∣w∣ in both directions. L(S)⊆P: one case per rule, checking every prefix. P⊆L(S): cut a non-empty w∈P after its shortest non-empty balanced prefix 0x1, giving w=0x1y with x,y∈P; if there is none, w=0x with x∈P.
