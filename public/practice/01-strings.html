Easy

1.1 Unfold an accumulator

Easy · recursive functions

The function rcat takes two strings and recurses on its first argument:

rcat(x,y):={yrcat(z, a⋅y)​if x=εif x=a⋅z.​

(a) Compute rcat(011,ε) by unfolding the definition one step at a time, and name the case used at each step.

(b) Compute rcat(10,01).

(c) Guess a formula for rcat(x,y) in terms of x, y and the functions of Chapter 1, and a formula for ∣rcat(x,y)∣. No proof is needed here; Problem 1.4 asks for one.

Source: classic.

Solution

(a) Each step peels the first symbol off the first argument and puts it in front of the second argument with the small dot:

rcat(011,ε)​=rcat(11,0⋅ε)=rcat(11,0)=rcat(1,1⋅0)=rcat(1,10)=rcat(ε,1⋅10)=rcat(ε,110)=110​​second case: a=0, z=11second case: a=1, z=1second case: a=1, z=εfirst case.​

(b) rcat(10,01)=rcat(0,101)=rcat(ε,0101)=0101.

(c) The symbols of x arrive at the front of the second argument one at a time, so they end up in reverse order, in front of the untouched y: in (a), 110=(011)R, and in (b), 0101=(10)R∙01. The guess is

rcat(x,y)=xR∙y,∣rcat(x,y)∣=∣x∣+∣y∣.

Check: for x=ε the guess gives εR∙y=y, which is the first case of the definition.

Watch out: the recursive call changes both arguments — the first gets shorter, the second longer. A proof by induction on x must therefore allow any second argument in its hypothesis (Problem 1.4).

Answer. (a) 110. (b) 0101. (c) rcat(x,y)=xR∙y, of length ∣x∣+∣y∣.

1.2 Substrings, subsequences and prefixes

Easy · true or false · substrings and subsequences

All strings are over {0,1}. Answer each statement Yes (always true) or No (not always true), with a one-sentence justification; for a No, give a counterexample.

(a) If x is a substring of y∙z, then x is a substring of y or a substring of z.

(b) If x is a subsequence of y∙z, then x=u∙v for some subsequence u of y and some subsequence v of z.

(c) If p is a prefix of w, then pR is a suffix of wR.

(d) If x∙y=y∙x, then x=y.

(e) Every string of length n has at least n+1 distinct subsequences.

(f) The string 0101 has exactly 11 distinct substrings, counting ε.

Source: original.

Hint

Every counterexample here needs at most three symbols. For (e), look for n+1 subsequences that are different for an obvious reason.

Solution

  • (a) No. A substring can straddle the boundary: x=01 is a substring of 0∙1=01, but not of y=0 or of z=1.
  • (b) Yes. A subsequence keeps some symbols of yz in order; the kept symbols that come from y form a subsequence u of y, those from z a subsequence v of z, and x=uv because every symbol of y comes before every symbol of z.
  • (c) Yes. If w=p∙x, then wR=xR∙pR by the reversal fact (p∙x)R=xR∙pR, so pR is a suffix of wR.
  • (d) No. x=0 and y=00 commute (0∙00=000=00∙0) but are different; so do x=ε and any non-empty y.
  • (e) Yes. The n+1 prefixes w[1..0],w[1..1],…,w[1..n] are subsequences (a prefix is a substring, and a substring is a subsequence), and they are distinct because their lengths differ. The bound is reached: 0n has exactly the n+1 subsequences ε,0,…,0n.
  • (f) No. It has 8: ε, 0, 1, 01, 10, 010, 101, 0101. Eleven — n(n+1)/2 non-empty substrings plus ε — is only an upper bound; here different positions give equal substrings (01 starts at positions 1 and 3).

Watch out: a Yes needs a reason that covers every case — one example proves nothing. A No needs one concrete counterexample, and a tiny one is best.

Answer. (a) No. (b) Yes. (c) Yes. (d) No. (e) Yes. (f) No: it has 8.

1.3 A proof with a missing case

Easy · find the error · string induction

For a binary string w, wc is its complement (every 0 becomes 1 and vice versa) and wR its reversal. A student proves (wR)c=(wc)R, citing the facts (x∙y)R=yR∙xR and (x∙y)c=xc∙yc:

Let w be an arbitrary binary string, and assume that (xR)c=(xc)R for every string x with ∣x∣<∣w∣. Case w=ε: (εR)c=εc=ε=εR=(εc)R. Case w=u∙v for non-empty strings u and v: then ∣u∣,∣v∣<∣w∣, so

((uv)R)c=(vRuR)c=(vR)c(uR)c=(vc)R(uc)R=(ucvc)R=((uv)c)R.

In both cases, (wR)c=(wc)R. ■

What is wrong with this proof? Repair it.

Source: original.

Hint

Which strings fall into neither case? Can such a string be written as u∙v with both pieces non-empty?

Solution

The slip: the cases do not cover every string. A string of length 1 is neither ε nor a concatenation of two non-empty strings, so the proof says nothing about w=0 or w=1. The u∙v step itself is correct: its five equalities use the reversal fact, the complement fact, the hypothesis for v and for u (both shorter than w), the reversal fact, and the complement fact.

The repair: add the case w=a for a single symbol a. By the definition of reversal, aR=(a⋅ε)R=εR∙a=ε∙a=a: a one-symbol string is its own reversal. The complement ac is again a single symbol, so (ac)R=ac, and therefore (aR)c=ac=(ac)R. With the three cases w=ε, w=a, and w=u∙v with u, v non-empty, every string is covered and the proof is complete.

Why the missing case matters. The same two cases “prove” the false claim #(0,w)=#(1,w): it holds for ε, and if it holds for u and v, then #(0,uv)=#(0,u)+#(0,v)=#(1,u)+#(1,v)=#(1,uv). The claim fails exactly in the missing case: #(0,0)=1=0=#(1,0).

Watch out: the split w=u∙v always needs two base cases, ε and a single symbol (§1.4). On the exams, cases that miss some strings cost the 2 points for exhaustive cases.

Answer. Strings of length 1 are in neither case. Add the case w=a, where (aR)c=ac=(ac)R because a one-symbol string is its own reversal.

1.4 Reversal with an accumulator

Medium · string induction · recursive functions

Let rcat be the function of Problem 1.1: rcat(ε,y):=y and rcat(a⋅z,y):=rcat(z,a⋅y).

(a) Prove that rcat(x,y)=xR∙y for all strings x and y.

(b) Conclude that rcat(x,ε)=xR and ∣rcat(x,y)∣=∣x∣+∣y∣.

Reversal is defined by εR:=ε and (a⋅z)R:=zR∙a; you may also use ∣wR∣=∣w∣.

Source: classic.

Hint

Induct on x, the argument that shrinks. In the recursive case the second argument changes from y to a⋅y, so the induction hypothesis must hold for every second argument, not only for y.

Solution

Idea. rcat recurses on x, so induct on x. The recursive call has second argument a⋅y instead of y; a hypothesis about the fixed y could not be applied to it, so the hypothesis quantifies over all second arguments.

(a) Let x be an arbitrary string. Assume that rcat(z,y′)=zR∙y′ for every string z with ∣z∣<∣x∣ and every string y′. Let y be an arbitrary string. There are two cases.

Case x=ε. By the definitions of rcat, ∙ and R: rcat(ε,y)=y=ε∙y=εR∙y.

Case x=a⋅z for some symbol a and string z. Then ∣z∣<∣x∣, so the induction hypothesis applies to z, with y′=a⋅y:

rcat(a⋅z,y)​=rcat(z,a⋅y)=zR∙(a⋅y)=zR∙(a∙y)=(zR∙a)∙y=(a⋅z)R∙y​​by definition of rcatby the induction hypothesis, with y′=a⋅ybecause a∙y=a⋅yby Lemma 1.3by definition of R.​

In both cases, rcat(x,y)=xR∙y. ■

(b) With y=ε, part (a) and Lemma 1.1 give rcat(x,ε)=xR∙ε=xR. By part (a), Lemma 1.2 and ∣xR∣=∣x∣: ∣rcat(x,y)∣=∣xR∣+∣y∣=∣x∣+∣y∣.

Check: rcat(011,ε)=110=(011)R and rcat(10,01)=0101=01∙01, as computed in Problem 1.1.

Watch out: with the weaker hypothesis ”rcat(z,y)=zR∙y for every shorter z” (the same y), the second line is not justified, because the call is rcat(z,a⋅y). Graders count that as applying the hypothesis with the wrong value of the other argument (2 points on the induction rubric). Proving only the special case rcat(x,ε)=xR by induction fails for the same reason — rcat(a⋅z,ε) calls rcat(z,a), which is not of the form rcat(z′,ε). Here the general statement is easier to prove than the special case.

Answer. (a) Induction on x, with the hypothesis for every second argument: rcat(az,y)=rcat(z,ay)=zR∙(ay)=(zRa)∙y=(az)R∙y. (b) rcat(x,ε)=xR∙ε=xR, and ∣rcat(x,y)∣=∣x∣+∣y∣.

1.5 When subsequences are substrings

Medium · substrings and subsequences

All strings are over {0,1}.

(a) For integers a,b≥1, how many distinct substrings and how many distinct subsequences does 0a1b have? Count ε.

(b) Prove that every subsequence of w is a substring of w if and only if w=0i1j or w=1i0j for some i,j≥0.

Source: original.

Hint

(a) Deleting symbols from 0a1b never puts a 1 before a 0. (b) For “only if”, find a symbol whose copies are not all next to each other, and look at its longest block of consecutive copies.

Solution

Idea. A subsequence of 0a1b is a few 0s followed by a few 1s, and such a string sits around the boundary of 0a1b as a substring. A string of any other shape has a symbol that occurs in two separate blocks, and gluing copies from both blocks gives a subsequence that is too long to be a substring.

(a) A subsequence of 0a1b keeps some of the 0s and some of the 1s, in order, so it is 0p1q with 0≤p≤a and 0≤q≤b; conversely each such string is a subsequence. Each of them is also a substring — the last p zeros and the first q ones, w[a−p+1..a+q] — and every substring is a subsequence. So the substrings and the subsequences are the same set {0p1q∣0≤p≤a, 0≤q≤b}, with (a+1)(b+1) elements (different pairs (p,q) give different strings).

(b) “If.” For w=0i1j this is the argument of (a), which also works when i=0 or j=0. For w=1i0j, exchange the roles of the two symbols.

“Only if”, by the contrapositive. Suppose w has neither form. Since w is not 0i1j, some 1 of w comes before some 0 (otherwise all 0s precede all 1s); since w is not 1i0j, some 0 comes before some 1. Let c be the first symbol of w and cˉ the other one. By one of the two facts (the first if c=0, the second if c=1), some cˉ comes before a later c; with the first symbol, w contains c, then cˉ, then c again, in that order. So the copies of c form at least two separate blocks (maximal runs of consecutive c‘s). Let m be the length of the longest block. The other block has at least one c, so #(c,w)≥m+1, and cm+1 is a subsequence of w. But it is not a substring: a substring made of m+1 copies of c would lie inside a single block, and every block has length at most m. ■

Check: 0110 has neither form, and indeed 00 is a subsequence but not a substring of it. 0011 has (2+1)(2+1)=9 substrings and 9 subsequences.

Answer. (a) (a+1)(b+1) substrings and (a+1)(b+1) subsequences — the two sets are equal. (b) “If”: every subsequence 0p1q of 0i1j is a substring. “Only if”: otherwise some symbol c occurs in two blocks, and if its longest block has length m, then cm+1 is a subsequence but not a substring.

1.6 Rotating a string

Medium · string induction · recursive functions

For symbols a, b and a string x, define

rot(w):={wb⋅rot(a⋅x)​if ∣w∣≤1if w=a⋅b⋅x.​

(a) Compute rot(STAR) by unfolding the definition.

(b) Prove directly from the definition, by induction on w, that ∣rot(w)∣=∣w∣ for every string w.

(c) Prove that rot(a⋅x)=x∙a for every symbol a and every string x.

Source: original.

Hint

(b) The definition has two cases, ∣w∣≤1 and w=a⋅b⋅x, so the proof does too. The recursive call is on a⋅x: is that string shorter than w, and does it matter that it is not the tail b⋅x? (c) Here it is easier to induct on x and keep a fixed.

Solution

Idea. Each call moves the second symbol out to the front and keeps the first symbol inside the call, so the first symbol travels to the end: rot rotates the string by one position. For (b), the strong hypothesis (“every shorter string”) covers the call on a⋅x; for (c), inducting on x makes the call an ordinary call on a shorter tail.

(a)

rot(STAR)=T⋅rot(SAR)=T⋅A⋅rot(SR)=T⋅A⋅R⋅rot(S)=TARS.

(b) Let w be an arbitrary string, and assume that ∣rot(y)∣=∣y∣ for every string y with ∣y∣<∣w∣. There are two cases.

Case ∣w∣≤1. Then rot(w)=w, so ∣rot(w)∣=∣w∣.

Case w=a⋅b⋅x. The string a⋅x has length ∣x∣+1<∣x∣+2=∣w∣, so the induction hypothesis applies to it:

∣rot(w)∣=∣b⋅rot(a⋅x)∣=1+∣rot(a⋅x)∣=1+∣a⋅x∣=2+∣x∣=∣w∣,

by the definition of rot, the definition of ∣⋅∣, the induction hypothesis, and the definition of ∣⋅∣ twice more.

In both cases, ∣rot(w)∣=∣w∣. ■

The string a⋅x is in general not a suffix of w — for w=STAR it is SAR. That does not matter: the hypothesis covers every string shorter than w.

(c) Let a be a symbol and x an arbitrary string. Assume that rot(a⋅y)=y∙a for every string y with ∣y∣<∣x∣. There are two cases.

Case x=ε. rot(a⋅ε)=a=ε∙a, by the first case of rot and the definition of ∙.

Case x=b⋅y. Then ∣y∣<∣x∣, and

rot(a⋅b⋅y)=b⋅rot(a⋅y)=b⋅(y∙a)=(b⋅y)∙a=x∙a,

by the definition of rot, the induction hypothesis for y, and the definition of ∙.

In both cases, rot(a⋅x)=x∙a. ■

Check: rot(STAR)=TARS=TAR∙S, as (c) predicts. And (b) also follows from (c): ∣x∙a∣=∣x∣+1 by Lemma 1.2.

Watch out: choosing what to induct on is part of the proof. Inducting on w in (c) works too, but then the hypothesis must be applied to a⋅y, which is not a tail of w, and the bookkeeping is heavier.

Answer. (a) TARS. (b) Two cases; for w=abx, the hypothesis applies to the shorter string a⋅x, and ∣b⋅rot(ax)∣=1+∣ax∣=∣w∣. (c) Induction on x: rot(a⋅b⋅y)=b⋅rot(a⋅y)=b⋅(y∙a)=(by)∙a.

1.7 Complementing every other symbol

Hard · string induction · recursive functions

For a binary string w, the complement wc is defined by εc:=ε and (a⋅x)c:=aˉ⋅xc, where 0ˉ=1 and 1ˉ=0. Define

zig(w):={εa⋅(zig(x))c​if w=εif w=a⋅x.​

For example, zig(0000)=0101 and zig(0110)=0011.

(a) Prove that zig(wc)=(zig(w))c for every binary string w.

(b) Prove that zig(zig(w))=w for every binary string w.

You may use (yc)c=y for every binary string y, and in (b) you may use (a).

Source: original.

Hint

Write the boilerplate for both parts first. In (a), the case w=a⋅x starts from wc=aˉ⋅xc. In (b), unfolding zig(zig(a⋅x)) twice produces zig(zig(x)c): that is where (a) comes in, applied to the string zig(x).

Solution

Idea. zig keeps the first symbol and complements the result of the recursive call, so the second symbol is complemented once, the third twice, and so on: zig complements exactly the symbols in even positions, and doing that twice changes nothing. In the proof, a complement must be moved past zig; that is what (a) does.

(a) Let w be an arbitrary binary string. Assume that zig(yc)=zig(y)c for every binary string y with ∣y∣<∣w∣. There are two cases.

Case w=ε. zig(εc)=zig(ε)=ε=εc=zig(ε)c, by the definitions of c and zig.

Case w=a⋅x for a symbol a and a string x. Then ∣x∣<∣w∣, so the induction hypothesis applies to x, and

zig(wc)​=zig(aˉ⋅xc)=aˉ⋅(zig(xc))c=aˉ⋅(zig(x)c)c=(a⋅zig(x)c)c=zig(a⋅x)c​​by definition of cby definition of zigby the induction hypothesis for xby definition of cby definition of zig.​

In both cases, zig(wc)=zig(w)c. ■

(b) Let w be an arbitrary binary string. Assume that zig(zig(y))=y for every binary string y with ∣y∣<∣w∣. There are two cases.

Case w=ε. zig(zig(ε))=zig(ε)=ε, by the definition of zig (twice).

Case w=a⋅x for a symbol a and a string x. Then ∣x∣<∣w∣, and

zig(zig(a⋅x))​=zig(a⋅zig(x)c)=a⋅(zig(zig(x)c))c=a⋅((zig(zig(x)))c)c=a⋅zig(zig(x))=a⋅x​​by definition of zigby definition of zigby (a), applied to the string zig(x)because (yc)c=yby the induction hypothesis for x.​

In both cases, zig(zig(w))=w. ■

Check: zig(0110)=0011 and zig(0011)=0110, as (b) says. For w=01, (a) reads zig(10)=11=(00)c=zig(01)c.

Watch out: in the third line of (b) the induction hypothesis is of no use: it is about zig(zig(y)), not zig(yc). Part (a) moves the complement out, and because (a) has been proved for every string, it may be applied to zig(x) with no question of length. The hypothesis of (b) is used once, for x, in the last line.

Answer. (a) Induction on w; for w=ax: zig(aˉxc)=aˉ⋅zig(xc)c=aˉ⋅(zig(x)c)c=(a⋅zig(x)c)c=zig(ax)c. (b) Induction on w, using (a) for zig(x) and the hypothesis for x: zig(zig(ax))=a⋅zig(zig(x)c)c=a⋅zig(zig(x))=ax.

1.8 Binary increment, low bit first

Hard · string induction · recursive functions

Read a binary string as a number with its least significant bit first:

val(w):={0a+2⋅val(x)​if w=εif w=a⋅x,​

where the symbols 0 and 1 count as the numbers 0 and 1; for example, val(011)=0+2(1+2(1+2⋅0))=6. The function

inc(w):=⎩⎨⎧​11⋅x0⋅inc(x)​if w=εif w=0⋅xif w=1⋅x​

adds one, the way you add one by hand: for example, inc(1101)=0011, that is, 11+1=12.

(a) Prove that val(inc(w))=val(w)+1 for every binary string w.

(b) Prove that ∣inc(w)∣=∣w∣+1 if w=1n for some n≥0, and ∣inc(w)∣=∣w∣ otherwise.

Source: original.

Hint

The definition of inc has three cases — ε, 0⋅x, 1⋅x — so both proofs do, and only the case 1⋅x needs the induction hypothesis. In (b), split that case according to whether x is all 1s.

Solution

Idea. Adding one turns the leading block of 1s (the low-order bits) into 0s and the first 0 into a 1; the string gets longer only when there is no 0 to absorb the carry. Both proofs follow the three cases of the definition of inc.

(a) Let w be an arbitrary binary string, and assume that val(inc(x))=val(x)+1 for every binary string x with ∣x∣<∣w∣. There are three cases.

Case w=ε. val(inc(ε))=val(1⋅ε)=1+2⋅val(ε)=1=val(ε)+1, by the definitions of inc and val.

Case w=0⋅x. val(inc(w))=val(1⋅x)=1+2val(x)=(0+2val(x))+1=val(0⋅x)+1, by the definitions of inc and val.

Case w=1⋅x. Then ∣x∣<∣w∣, so the induction hypothesis applies to x:

val(inc(1⋅x))​=val(0⋅inc(x))=0+2val(inc(x))=2(val(x)+1)=(1+2val(x))+1=val(1⋅x)+1​​by definition of incby definition of valby the induction hypothesisby arithmeticby definition of val.​

In all three cases, val(inc(w))=val(w)+1. ■

(b) A small fact first: 1⋅x is of the form 1n if and only if x is. Indeed, 1⋅1m=1m+1; conversely, if 1⋅x=1n, then n≥1 (the string is not empty), so 1⋅x=1⋅1n−1 by the definition of powers, and x=1n−1 because equal pairs have equal second parts.

Let w be an arbitrary binary string. Assume, for every binary string x with ∣x∣<∣w∣, that ∣inc(x)∣=∣x∣+1 if x is of the form 1m, and ∣inc(x)∣=∣x∣ otherwise. There are three cases.

Case w=ε. Here w=10, and ∣inc(ε)∣=∣1∣=1=∣ε∣+1.

Case w=0⋅x. Here w is not of the form 1n, because such a string is empty or starts with 1; and ∣inc(w)∣=∣1⋅x∣=1+∣x∣=∣0⋅x∣=∣w∣.

Case w=1⋅x. By the definitions, ∣inc(w)∣=∣0⋅inc(x)∣=1+∣inc(x)∣, and ∣x∣<∣w∣, so the hypothesis applies to x.

  • If x=1m, then w=1m+1, and ∣inc(w)∣=1+∣x∣+1=∣w∣+1.
  • Otherwise, by the small fact, w is not of the form 1n either, and ∣inc(w)∣=1+∣x∣=∣w∣.

In all cases, the claim holds for w. ■

Check: inc(111)=0001: the value goes from 7 to 8, and the length from 3 to 4. inc(011)=111: the value goes from 6 to 7, and the length stays 3.

Grading notes. Each part is an induction proof of the exam type, graded out of 10: an arbitrary w (1), an explicit hypothesis for every shorter string (2), cases that cover every string — here the three of the definition (2), the base case (1), the hypothesis applied only to the shorter x (2), and justified details (2). See how exams are graded.

Watch out: the claim in (b) has two alternatives, so the hypothesis must state both: in the case w=1⋅x you do not know in advance which one applies to x.

Answer. (a) Three cases; only w=1x uses the hypothesis: val(0⋅inc(x))=2(val(x)+1)=val(1x)+1. (b) Three cases; for w=1x, ∣inc(w)∣=1+∣inc(x)∣, and w is all 1s exactly when x is.
