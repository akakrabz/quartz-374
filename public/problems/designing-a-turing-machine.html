Problem · a Turing-machine design exercise (Chapter 6 material, on the final exam) · uses §6.2, §6.3, §6.6 · concepts: turing-machines, acceptable-and-decidable

Problem

Let Copy={w#w∣w∈{0,1}∗} over the input alphabet {0,1,#}. For example, 01#01 and # are in Copy; 01#10, 0#0# and ε are not.

(a) Describe a single-tape Turing machine that decides Copy: the algorithm in English, the states with their meanings, and the full transition table.

(b) Trace your machine on 01#01, and say where it rejects 01#00.

(c) Explain why it halts on every input, and show that its running time is Θ(n2) for inputs of length n.

(a) The machine

Idea. Copy is not even context-free (a classic fact whose proof is beyond this course), so no grammar or stack can check it — but a Turing machine can, by comparing the two halves one symbol at a time and zigzagging across the #. It cannot remember w, but it can remember one symbol in its state and mark the symbols it has finished with.

The algorithm in English

Repeat rounds. In each round:

  1. Take the leftmost unmarked symbol of the left half, overwrite it with x, and remember it in the state. If the # comes first instead, the left half is used up: go to step 5.
  2. Carry it right, past the rest of the left half, to the #.
  3. Seek past the marked symbols to the right of the # to the first unmarked one. It must be the remembered symbol: mark it with x. Anything else — the other symbol, a blank, a second # — means reject.
  4. Walk back left, past the marked symbols and the #, then past the unmarked rest of the left half to the rightmost x; step right and start the next round.
  5. Check that every symbol right of the # is marked: scan right over xs, accept at the first blank, reject at anything else.

If the input has no #, step 2 runs into a blank: reject.

The tape alphabet is Γ={0,1,#,x,□}. The states and what each one knows:

That is 20 transitions. The rejections are exactly the missing ones: start on □ (the empty input has no #); carry0 or carry1 on □ (no #); seek0 on 1 or seek1 on 0 (a mismatch); either seek state on □ (the right half is too short) or on # (a second #); check on 0 or 1 (the right half is too long) or on # (a second #).

Design traps

  • Falling off the left end. backL walks left until it sees an x, and that x always exists: cell 0 holds x from the machine’s first move on (an input that starts with # never reaches backL). A machine that walks left “to the beginning of the tape” cannot see the beginning — it crashes there (§6.2).
  • Junk inputs. “Decides” means halting with the right answer on every input: ε, 0#, ##, 0#0#, 01, not just strings with mismatched halves. Here each of them ends in a missing transition; list the cases, as above, and test them.
  • Silent missing transitions. They reject only if you say so. Write “missing transitions go to reject” (the book’s convention) — or give every transition.
  • One mark for both halves. Using the same x on both sides works because the # separates them and the marks of the left half form a prefix: the first x that backL meets is the last symbol taken.

(c) Halting and running time

It halts on every input. Each round marks one more symbol to the left of the first #, so there are at most n rounds. Within a round, every phase moves in one direction and stops at a symbol that is guaranteed to be there, or rejects: carry and seek move right and reject on a blank, so they never pass cell n, the first blank; backR stops at the # it crossed on the way right; backL stops at an x. The final check moves right and stops by cell n as well.

At most O(n2) steps. A round sweeps right from its starting cell to at most cell n and back, at most 2n+2 steps; with at most n rounds and a final scan of at most n+2 steps, the machine halts within 2n2+3n+2 steps on every input of length n.

Exactly 2(k+1)2 steps on w#w. Let ∣w∣=k, so n=2k+1. Round i (1≤i≤k) starts on cell i−1:

  • start marks wi​, and carry crosses the other k−i unmarked symbols of the left half and the #: k−i+2 steps;
  • seek crosses the i−1 marked symbols right of the # and marks the next one: i steps;
  • backR crosses those i−1 marks and the #: i steps;
  • backL crosses the k−i unmarked symbols and reads the x on cell i−1: k−i+1 steps.

Every round therefore takes 2k+3 steps, whatever i is. The last phase — start reads the #, check crosses k marks and reads the blank — takes k+2. In all,

k(2k+3)+k+2=2k2+4k+2=2(k+1)2=2(n+1)2​ steps.

For 01#01 (k=2) that is 18, as in the trace: rounds of 7 steps end at steps 7 and 14. Even lengths have no string w#w, but w#w0 (n=2k+2) runs the same k rounds and is rejected when check reads the final 0: again 2(k+1)2=n2/2 steps.

Answer (c)

The machine halts on every input of length n within 2n2+3n+2 steps, and on w#w with ∣w∣=k it takes exactly 2(k+1)2=(n+1)2/2 steps (for even n, w#w0 takes n2/2). So its worst-case running time is Θ(n2).

Could a cleverer one-tape machine do better? Not asymptotically. Every one-tape machine for Copy needs Ω(n2) steps in the worst case — the classic crossing-sequence argument, the same one behind the book’s remark about marked palindromes in §6.6 — while a machine with two tapes decides it in O(n) time: copy w to the second tape, then compare. The quadratic time is the price of a single head, not of a careless design.

Time-analysis traps

  • Counting rounds only. There are about n/2 rounds, and each one walks across the # and back, about n steps: Θ(n) rounds of Θ(n) steps.
  • Θ needs both bounds. The O(n2) upper bound alone is not Θ(n2); the exact count on w#w supplies the matching lower bound.
  • n is the whole input length, # included: n=2k+1 for w#w.

On the exams

Turing machines (Chapter 6) are tested on the final exam only. The course’s standard rubrics have no separate entry for Turing-machine design, so take the DFA rubric’s demands as the guide: a complete, unambiguous description — every state with its meaning, every transition or an explicit “missing transitions reject” — plus a check of the edge cases (ε, no #, two #s, halves of different lengths). Describe the algorithm in English first; the table is then routine.

Sources for this page

{w#w} is a classic example language for Turing machines (and a standard example of a decidable language that is not context-free); the machine, its table, the traces and the exact step count are ours, in the conventions of Erickson, Models of Computation, §§6.2–6.3. The Ω(n2) lower bound for one-tape machines is a classic crossing-sequence result, cited here and not proved; the book states the analogous fact for marked palindromes in §6.6. Checked by tools/checks/problems_2.py: the machine accepts exactly Copy and halts on all 88,573 inputs over {0,1,#} of length at most 10, within the stated bound; it takes 2(k+1)2 steps on every w#w with k≤7, in rounds of 2k+3 steps, and as many on every w#w0; and both traces are as shown.
