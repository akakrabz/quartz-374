2. Chapter 6 — Turing machines

Chapter 6 · concepts: turing-machines, acceptable-and-decidable, universal-turing-machine, church-turing-thesis

Turing machine

A tape alphabet Γ with a blank □; an input alphabet Σ⊆Γ∖{□}; a finite set of states Q with start, accept, reject; and

δ:(Q∖{accept,reject})×Γ→Q×Γ×{−1,+1}.

A configuration is (q,x,i): state, tape contents, head position; the run on w starts at (start,w,0), and a move left from cell 0 crashes. On each input the machine accepts, rejects, crashes or runs forever. M accepts L if Accept(M)=L; M decides L if it also halts — rejecting — on every string outside L.

conventionwhat it means
missing transitionsgo to reject — say so on an exam
the left endmark it before the machine ever walks left; otherwise it may crash
a crashneither accept nor reject: Chapter 7 counts the input as diverging
computing a functionone halting state; the output is the tape at that moment without trailing blanks; M(w)↑ if M never halts
namesacceptable = recognizable = semi-decidable = recursively enumerable; decidable = computable = recursive

Variants and what simulating them costs (§6.4, §6.6, §6.7, §9.4). None of them changes which languages are accepted or decided; they change only the running time.

varianthow a standard machine simulates itcost of t steps
stay-put movesstates Q×{0,1}: move right, then step backat most 2t
k tracksone track over Γ1​×⋯×Γk​t
tape infinite both waysfold it at cell 0 onto two tracksO(t)
k heads, or k tapesextra tracks mark the heads; every step sweeps across themΘ(t2), unavoidable in general

The quadratic loss is real: marked palindromes {w#wR} take O(n) steps with two heads but Ω(n2) with one.

The universal machine (§6.8). U accepts ⟨M,w⟩ iff M accepts w, and rejects it iff M rejects w. Symbols and states are fixed-length bit strings (⟨start⟩=1⋯1, ⟨reject⟩=0⋯0, and on this site ⟨accept⟩=0⋯01, a code the book leaves open); a transition δ(p,a)=(q,b,Δ) is the block [⟨p⟩∙⟨a⟩∣⟨q⟩∙⟨b⟩∙⟨Δ⟩], and transitions into reject are left out; a configuration is a list of cells [⟨q⟩∙⟨a⟩] in which only the head’s cell carries a state other than reject. One step: find the head’s cell, look up its block (none: reject), rewrite the cell, pass the state to the neighbor.

The Church–Turing thesis — every algorithm can be carried out by a Turing machine — is a claim about the world, not a theorem. It is what lets proofs describe machines in pseudocode.

3. Chapter 7 — Undecidability

Chapter 7 · concepts: diagonalization, halting-problem, undecidability-reductions, rices-theorem

The four languages of a machine. Accept(M), Reject(M) and Diverge(M) split Σ∗; Halt(M)=Accept(M)∪Reject(M). M decides Accept(M) exactly when Diverge(M)=∅. ”M does not accept w” means M rejects or diverges.

resultstatement
Lemma 1from any M build MR (swap accept and reject), MA (rejections become endless loops: Reject(MA)=∅) and MH (rejections become acceptances: Accept(MH)=Halt(M))
Lemma 2decidable languages are closed under ∪, ∩, ∖ — and complement, by Lemma 1(a)
Corollary 3if L′ is decidable and L∩L′, L∪L′, L∖L′ or L′∖L is undecidable, then L is undecidable
Lemma 4acceptable languages are closed under ∪ (run both in parallel) and ∩
Lemma 5an acceptable L is decidable iff L is acceptable; so if L is acceptable but undecidable, L is not acceptable
Cantor’s theoremno f:X→2X is onto; so some language is not acceptable

Also undecidable: NeverReject, NeverHalt, NeverDiverge (the same reduction with the other languages of Mw​), DivergeSame (Theorem 14), and {⟨M,w⟩∣M accepts wk for every k≥0} (Theorem 16).

The direction of a reduction

To prove L undecidable, reduce a known undecidable language to L: assume a decider DecideL, and build from it a decider for Accept (or Halt, …) that writes down the encoding of a new machine M′ and asks DecideL about it. The new decider never runs M′. The workhorse is

Mw​(x): erase x; write w; run M on w,Accept(Mw​)={Σ∗∅​if M accepts w,otherwise.​

Deciding L with the help of a decider for Halt proves nothing: every decidable language reduces to Halt.

Template — the three proofs in skeleton form

Diagonalization. Suppose Dec decides L. Build Wrap from Dec (swap its answers, or redirect them to a loop) so that for every M, Wrap accepts (or halts on) ⟨M⟩ iff M does not … ⟨M⟩. Take M=Wrap; both cases contradict.
Reduction. Suppose DecideL decides L. DecideAccept: given ⟨M,w⟩, write ⟨M′⟩, run DecideL on it, return its answer (or the opposite). If M accepts w, then … so ⟨M′⟩∈L; if M rejects or diverges, then … so ⟨M′⟩∈/L. So DecideAccept decides Accept — impossible.
Rice. L=AcceptIn(L) for L={…}; membership depends only on Accept(M); Accept(Y)∈L for Y=…; Accept(N)∈/L for N=…; so L is undecidable by Rice’s theorem.

Rice's theorem and its variants

If Accept(Y)∈L for some machine Y and Accept(N)∈/L for some machine N, then AcceptIn(L)={⟨M⟩∣Accept(M)∈L} is undecidable. (The proof, when ∅∈/L: a reduction from Halt with WTF(x) = “run M on w, then Y on x”; otherwise apply it to the complementary property.) The same holds with Reject, Halt or Diverge in place of Accept, and for DecideIn(L) with deciders Y and N. Use the variant that matches the definition.

Witnesses: MAccept​ accepts everything (Accept=Σ∗); MReject​ rejects everything (Accept=∅, Halt=Σ∗); MDiverge​ moves right forever (Accept=Halt=∅). Corollary 15: “accepts ε”, “accepts UIUC”, “accepts exactly three strings”, “accepts every palindrome”, ”Accept(M) is regular / not regular / undecidable”, "Accept(M)=Accept(N)" are all undecidable.

When Rice’s theorem does not apply (§7.11): a property of the machine’s behavior or text (ThisIsSparta, ”M has 374 states”: decidable); self-reference (RevAccept={⟨M⟩∣M accepts ⟨M⟩R}: undecidable, but by a reduction); a trivial property (no Y or no N: decidable); and unacceptability, which Rice never proves.

The Rice–McNaughton–Myhill–Shapiro theorem ( §7.12)

For a set L of acceptable languages, AcceptIn(L) is acceptable iff L is monotone (acceptable supersets of its members are members), compact (every member has a finite subset that is a member) and finitely acceptable (its finite members, listed out, form an acceptable language). Lemma 17: not monotone ⇒ not acceptable. Corollary 18: ”Accept(M) is finite / infinite / regular / not regular / decidable / undecidable”, ”M accepts some string of SelfDiverge” and "Accept(M)=Accept(N)" are not acceptable.

Sanity check: "Accept(M)=∅" is monotone, compact (a single string suffices) and finitely acceptable — and indeed dovetailing accepts that language.

Behavior (§7.13). NeverLeft is decidable: a machine that only moves right and has read more than ∣Q∣ blanks has repeated a state on a blank and will run right forever. LeftThree is undecidable: a simulation can be padded so that it never makes three left moves in a row until it would accept. Rules of thumb, not theorems: a behavior is probably undecidable if a machine could simulate an arbitrary machine while avoiding it and show it only on acceptance; probably decidable if it confines the machine to finitely many configurations or forces an endless loop after finitely many steps.

5. Chapter 9 — Nondeterministic Turing machines

Chapter 9 · concepts: nondeterministic-turing-machines, cook-levin-theorem

notionwhat to know cold
definitionδ:(Q∖{accept,reject})×Γ→2Q×Γ×{−1,+1}; the runs on w form a computation tree
accept / rejectaccepts w if some branch accepts (others may run forever); rejects w if every branch rejects — then the whole tree is finite; otherwise neither
Theorem 1a deterministic machine accepts and rejects exactly the same strings: breadth-first search of the tree, O(t2r2t) steps on one tape, O(trt) with two; depth-first search can get stuck in an infinite branch
advice and dovetailingnumber the options 1,…,r; N accepts w iff some advice string makes the deterministic M accept (w,x). Dovetailing tries all advice strings of length t for t steps, t=1,2,…: O(trt) with three tapes, O(t2rt) with one
complexity classesTIME, NTIME, SPACE, NSPACE on multi-tape machines; P=⋃k​TIME(nk), NP=⋃k​NTIME(nk); P⊆NP⊆ exponential time; NP = languages with polynomial-time verifiers
Cook–Levinif SAT∈P then P=NP. For L∈NP with a verifier running N=max{1,nk} steps, the formula Φw​ has variables Qt,i,q​ (state q, head on cell i, time t) and Tt,i,a​ (cell i holds a), five kinds of constraints (boundaries, initialization, advice, transitions, acceptance), size O(N2)=O(n2k), and is satisfiable iff w∈L

So SAT is NP-hard — every L∈NP reduces to it — and, being in NP, NP-complete. To prove another problem NP-hard, reduce SAT to it: the same direction rule as for undecidability (§9.6).

7. True or false: twelve to practice

statementanswerone-sentence justification
Every decidable language is acceptable.truea decider accepts its language (§6.2).
If M does not accept w, then M rejects w.falseM may run forever, or crash.
If L and L are both acceptable, then L is decidable.truerun both acceptors in parallel (Lemma 5).
The complement of every acceptable language is acceptable.falseHalt is acceptable, but by Lemma 5 its complement is not.
Halt is undecidable, so it is not acceptable.falsethe universal machine accepts it.
”M has 374 states” is undecidable by Rice’s theorem.falseit is decidable — a property of the machine’s text, not of its language.
Rice’s theorem shows that {⟨M⟩∣Accept(M)=∅} is not acceptable.falseRice proves only undecidability; dovetailing accepts this language.
Deciding L with a subroutine that decides Halt proves that L is undecidable.falsewrong direction: every decidable language reduces to Halt.
A nondeterministic machine rejects w if some branch rejects w.falseevery branch must reject (§9.2).
Every language accepted by a nondeterministic Turing machine is acceptable.trueTheorem 1 of Chapter 9.
Some language decided by a two-tape machine is decided by no one-tape machine.falseone tape simulates two with a quadratic slowdown; only time changes.
A finite-state machine with two stacks is as powerful as a Turing machine.truethe tape is two stacks cut at the head (§8.2).

8. The traps that cost the most points

Across Chapters 6–9

  1. “Does not accept” is not “rejects”. Every second case of a reduction must handle rejection and divergence.
  2. A decider halts on every input, including strings that encode nothing; the decider you build in a reduction must halt even when M′ would not.
  3. Wrong direction. The hypothetical decider for the new language is the subroutine; the language you end up deciding is the known undecidable one.
  4. Running M′ instead of writing it down. DecideAccept only computes ⟨M′⟩; say what M′ does on every input x.
  5. Unnamed machines. “It accepts” is ambiguous with four machines around: name DecideL, DecideAccept, M and M′.
  6. Diagonalization on the wrong machine. The contradiction comes from running the wrapper on its own encoding; say why that run halts.
  7. Rice misapplied: to behavior or to the machine’s text, without the property written as a set of languages, without concrete Y and N, with the wrong variant, or to conclude “not acceptable”.
  8. The union of acceptable languages run in series. The first acceptor may run forever; simulate in parallel (Lemma 4).
  9. Swapping accept and reject complements only a deterministic decider — not a machine that may diverge, not a nondeterministic machine.
  10. Machine design without the conventions: say that missing transitions reject, mark the left end, and trace ε and near-misses.
  11. Confusing time with power. Tapes, heads and nondeterminism change running times, never which languages are decidable or acceptable.

9. What to put on your cheat sheet

The final allows two double-sided, handwritten letter sheets for the whole course. For Chapters 6–9:

  • The Turing-machine tuple, δ‘s type, configurations, the four outcomes, and accepts versus decides.
  • The variants table with its costs (2t, Θ(t2), O(tlogt), O(t2r2t), O(trt)).
  • Lemmas 1–5 in one line each, and Corollary 3.
  • The table of named languages: decidable, acceptable, complement acceptable.
  • The three proof skeletons with their rubrics (4+6, 4+3+3, 4+4+2), and Mw​.

10. A study plan for the final

Chapters 6–9 between Midterm 2 (Nov 9) and the final (Dec 11)

  1. Read first, once. Chapter 6 for the definitions and conventions; Chapter 7 slowly, section by section, rewriting each template from memory; Chapters 8 and 9 for the statements in §4 and §5 above.
  2. Drill the three proofs. Work the worked problem without looking, then the Chapter 7 practice problems: one diagonal argument, two reductions and two Rice proofs a day for a week, each graded against its rubric.
  3. Classify languages fast. Go down the table of §3 and the zoo of the big picture covering the answers: decidable? acceptable? complement acceptable? which tool?
  4. Design one machine end to end (Chapter 6 practice): passes in English, table, meanings, traces.
  5. Write the cheat sheets by hand from §9 here and §9 of the Chapters 1–5 review, then finish with the true/false tables of both reviews and the traps.
