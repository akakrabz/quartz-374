A paraphrase, not the rubrics themselves

Everything on this page paraphrases the CS/ECE 374 A (Fall 2026) course website: its exam schedule, the Midterm 1 practice exams and the standard grading rubrics. The course website is authoritative. Where this page disagrees with it, or with the instructions printed on an exam, they win.

On every problem (§10): “clearly”, “trivial” and “obviously” draw scrutiny; ambiguity is read unfavorably; an NFA where a DFA was asked earns half credit; ∅ or Σ∗ as a regular expression or grammar earns nothing; and any result from lecture, the lecture notes, labs or homework solutions may be used without proof.

4. Induction

pointsfor
1starting from an arbitrary object: “let w be an arbitrary string”
2an explicit, strong induction hypothesis — weak induction is discouraged
2exhaustive cases: together they cover every object
1the base cases
2correct use of the induction hypothesis
2the other details

The wording (“an arbitrary object”) fits every induction in the course — over strings, regular expressions, numbers or derivations. A proof that a grammar generates a language is two of them.

What loses points

  • A hypothesis that is never written down, or a weak one (“assume the claim for x; prove it for ax”). State it for every smaller object.
  • Applying the hypothesis to something that is not smaller than w — or not saying why it is smaller.
  • Cases that miss strings: a definition with a case w=abx needs base cases for ε and for every single symbol.
  • Inducting on the wrong argument: concatenation takes its left argument apart, so the right one never gets smaller.
  • A step justified by “clearly” instead of by a definition, the hypothesis or a lemma.

Templates: string induction, structural induction, what a grammar generates · worked: sorting a string, a grammar proof · traps in context: §1.4, induction on strings

5. Regular expressions and grammars

pointsfor
2syntax
8correctness (on exams)

Exam deductions are twice the homework ones:

mistakehomeworkexam
one mistake — often a missing or extra ε−1−2
a few wrong strings−2−4
infinitely many wrong stringsno correctness creditno correctness credit: −8
the answer ∅ or Σ∗no creditno credit

Homework answers must also explain each piece in English, and for a grammar, every nonterminal needs a self-contained description of its language. Ambiguity is not penalized: an ambiguous grammar that generates the right language earns full credit.

What loses points

  • ε, forgotten or let in by accident. Test it first.
  • Precedence: 10∗ is not (10)∗, and a misplaced star usually changes infinitely many strings.
  • Syntax that regular expressions do not have: ∩, complement, 0n, a “plus” that means “one or more”.
  • A union with a forgotten case, like a grammar for “not of the form 0n1n” that misses every string containing 10 (§5.4, Example 2).
  • A nonterminal described by its role (“the middle part”) instead of by its language.

Template: write-up · worked: 01 exactly twice (b), fooling set and grammar (b), state elimination by hand · traps in context: §2.3, §5.4

6. DFAs and NFAs

pointsfor
2an unambiguous, complete description: a start arrow and double circles; say so if you leave out a trash (dump) state; a description in text gives the whole transition function; an NFA states its ε-transitions; a product construction describes each factor and the accepting states
6correctness (on exams)
2mnemonic state names

An NFA where a DFA was asked earns half credit. The rubric has no line for minimality; a clear machine with a few extra states is better than a minimal one you cannot explain (§3.5).

What loses points

  • Missing transitions and no sentence about a dump state: the machine is incomplete — or an NFA, which earns half credit.
  • States named q0​,q1​,… with no meaning attached.
  • “Run both machines in parallel” with no description of the factors, the pairs and the accepting pairs.
  • Factors of a product that are not complete DFAs over the same alphabet: the union and the difference then lose strings.

Templates: describing a DFA, product construction · worked: 01 exactly twice (a), a product, written formally, subset construction by hand · traps in context: §3.2, §3.6

7. Fooling sets

pointsfor
4an explicit infinite set that really is a fooling set — a finite set earns nothing
2for arbitrary distinct x,y∈F: an explicit suffix z
2showing that one of xz and yz is in L
2showing that the other is not

What loses points

  • One example pair instead of arbitrary distinct x and y, written with parameters i=j.
  • “Assume i<j” without “without loss of generality” — or when the argument is not symmetric, because z was built from x.
  • Only one half shown, or a half with no reason.
  • A set described by what it should do (“all the prefixes we need”) instead of explicitly.
  • A suffix that depends on a DFA you are trying to rule out.

8. Language transformations

pointsfor
2a formal, complete description of the new machine M′, built from an arbitrary DFA M — say whether M′ is a DFA or an NFA, and give its ε-transitions
2an English explanation of the states and of the accepting states
6correctness: 1 for the states, 1 for the start state, 1 for the accepting states, 3 for the transitions

Before handing in, run the construction on the edge cases the rubric names: L=∅, {ε}, 1∗ and Σ∗.

What loses points

  • A construction for one particular DFA or language: the claim is about every regular L.
  • δ′ left undefined for some state and symbol, or for ε.
  • The two kinds mixed up: {w∣f(w)∈L} simulates M on f(w); {f(w)∣w∈L} guesses w.
  • Complementing an NFA by swapping its accepting states.

9. Undecidability

Chapters 6–9 are tested on the final exam only. An undecidability proof is graded with the rubric of its method.

Diagonalization

pointsfor
4the wrapper machine, built from the hypothetical decider
6the contradiction: 3 for each of the two cases

Reduction

pointsfor
4the reduction: the new decider and the machine M′ it writes down
3”if”: a yes-instance (M accepts w) makes the new decider accept
3”only if”: a no-instance (M rejects or diverges) makes it reject

Rice’s theorem

pointsfor
4a machine whose language has the property
4a machine whose language does not
2the details, including the right variant of the theorem

What loses points

  • The wrong direction. Deciding L with the help of a decider for Halt proves nothing; the hypothetical decider for L must be the subroutine.
  • Running M′. The new decider only writes ⟨M′⟩ down; it must halt on every input.
  • “Does not accept” read as “rejects”. It also means “diverges”, and the “only if” case must cover both.
  • Unnamed machines. Name the hypothetical decider, the decider you build, M and M′, and never write “it”.
  • Rice where it does not apply: a behavior (ThisIsSparta), self-reference (RevAccept), a trivial property — or the wrong variant, such as a property of Halt(M) treated as one of Accept(M).
  • Diagonalizing against the wrong machine. The contradiction comes from running the machine built from the decider on its own encoding.

Templates: diagonalization, reduction, Rice · worked: AtLeastTwo; Theorem 6 in §7.5 · traps in context: §7.9, §7.11

10. General rules

  • “Clearly”, “trivial” and “obviously” draw scrutiny. A grader reads the step they introduce more closely. Write the reason instead: a definition, the hypothesis, a lemma.
  • Ambiguity is read unfavorably. When an answer can be read two ways, expect the reading that earns less: define every symbol, name every machine, label every edge. (An ambiguous grammar is a different thing, and costs nothing.)
  • An NFA where a DFA was asked earns half credit.
  • ∅ or Σ∗ as a regular expression or grammar earns no credit.
  • Cite instead of reproving. Any result from lecture, the lecture notes, labs or homework solutions may be used without proof.
  • Cheat sheets are handwritten: one double-sided letter sheet for a midterm, two for the final.
